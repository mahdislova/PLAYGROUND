# The Rose of Nazarovo

Hugh considered the question, and the man asking it, and decided the truth would serve better than anything clever.

"Because they're mine now," he said. "Floriana and her people swore to me at Erro. They hold my keep and they march under my banner. Where I go to treat, my captain comes with me."

Sir Everec's eyebrows rose toward the fringe of hair that ringed his scalp. "Your captain," he repeated, savouring the word as though it were an unfamiliar wine. "How very... progressive. How very bold. My lord the baron will be enchanted, I am certain of it, quite enchanted, though I confess I cannot predict in which direction." He turned his mild gaze on Floriana, who stood at Hugh's shoulder with her arms folded and her expression carved from granite. "Madam. Five years have been kind to you."

"They've been kind to you too," said Floriana. "You've grown a second chin to keep the first company."

Everec laughed, a high delighted sound, and clapped his gloved hands together once. "Oh, she is charming. Charming! Come, come, the road awaits, and my lord awaits at the end of it, and neither grows any younger."

He wheeled his pony about and set off between the towers at a sedate walk, his escort falling in around him, and after a moment Hugh signalled the column forward.

"You know him?" he asked quietly as they walked.

"Everyone in these hills knows Everec." Floriana did not look at him. "He talks like a perfumed fool and he remembers everything. He was at the Bratsk ford when we hit the salt convoy. Didn't draw his sword once, just sat on a hillock taking notes while his men died, and three weeks later Niestor's soldiers knew the name of every woman who'd ridden with me." She spat to one side of the road. "Don't let the sleeves fool you."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good." A pause. "My lord."

Hugh glanced at her, but her face gave nothing away, and he let it lie.

* * *

The road from the towers ran down through steep pine forest for the rest of the evening, and they made camp in a meadow by a stream that Everec assured them, with many flourishes, was the finest camping ground in all the barony, blessed by no fewer than three saints. Hugh doubled the watch regardless. Floriana tripled it without being asked, and he pretended not to notice.

Morale was no better that night. The Poison Sisters ate in their own knots around their own fires, and when one of Everec's men wandered over to ask for a light for his pipe, the conversation died so completely that the poor fellow went back to his own fire without it. Hugh walked the lines before turning in, speaking a word here and there, and was met everywhere with the same flat courtesy. Yes, my lord, no trouble, my lord, good night, my lord.

They did not trust him. He could hardly blame them. He had marched them into the lands of the man who'd hanged their friends, and asked them to smile about it.

Morwyn found him by the stream afterward, washing the dust from his face.

"They'll come round," she said.

"Will they?"

"Some of them. When they see you won't sell them." She crouched beside him and trailed her fingers in the cold water, watching the current. "That's what they're waiting for, you know. Not for you to win them anything. Just to see whether you'll trade them for an alliance the first time someone offers a good price."

Hugh dried his face on his sleeve. "And if someone does?"

"Then we'll find out what sort of man you are," said Morwyn, and smiled at him, and went back to her blankets.

He sat by the stream a while longer after that, listening to the water, and did not sleep well.

* * *

They came down out of the pines the next morning and saw Nazarovo laid out below them in the crook of the river.

It was larger than Hugh had expected. A walled town of perhaps four thousand souls, its roofs tiled in faded red, smoke rising from a hundred chimneys, the river bending around three sides of it like a protective arm. A stone bridge crossed to the town gate, and on a bluff at the town's northern edge stood the castle, square, old and severe, with nothing about it that was meant to please the eye. The fields around the walls were neatly kept, the hedgerows trimmed, the orchards heavy with late apples. Whatever else Niestor was, he was a careful lord.

And everywhere, roses.

They climbed the walls of the farmhouses and spilled over the garden fences. They grew in tended beds along the road, red, white, and a deep, bruised purple, their scent heavy in the cool morning air. Even the town gate was hung with them, great swags of red blooms woven through iron hooks above the arch, so that the column marched beneath a curtain of petals that shed and drifted onto their shoulders.

"Charming custom," said Morwyn under her breath, brushing a petal from her sleeve.

"Every petal a man he's hanged," muttered one of the Poison Sisters behind them, a lanky woman named Dita with a crossbow slung across her back. "That's what they used to say in the hills."

"That's not true," said Floriana, without turning.

"No?"

"No. He'd need a lot more roses."

Nobody laughed.

Everec reined in before the bridge and turned his pony to face them, beaming. "My lord Hugh! Here we must, with the deepest regret, part with the larger portion of your splendid company. My lord the baron has prepared a camp for them in the water meadow yonder, where they will want for nothing, nothing at all; there is beef, there is beer, there is good clean straw. But within the walls, he begs your indulgence, no more than twenty. The townsfolk, you understand, are simple people. They startle."

Hugh had expected it. Twenty was a reasonable number for an embassy and a useless one for a fight, which was precisely the point; any lord with sense would have asked the same.

"Twenty," he agreed. "Captain?"

Floriana chose them in less than a minute, and Hugh noted that every one she picked was a veteran, scarred and steady, and that she did not pick Dita. The rest she put under her senior lieutenant with orders to keep their weapons close and their tempers closer, and to answer any insult from the baron's men with a smile and a note of the man's name.

"And if they do more than insult?" asked the lieutenant.

Floriana glanced at Hugh.

"Then you defend yourselves," he said, "and I'll answer for it."

The lieutenant looked at him a while, then nodded, and saluted. It was a small thing. But it was the first salute any of them had given him that had not felt like it was being paid out under protest, and Hugh found it lifted his heart more than it probably should have.

* * *

The great hall of Nazarovo was long and cold, lined with banners, and at the far end of it, on a plain oak chair raised two steps above the floor, Baron Niestor sat waiting for them.

He was not what Hugh had imagined. Five years of stories had built him up into something monstrous, a broad scarred brute with a hangman's rope coiled at his belt. The man on the chair was tall and spare, perhaps sixty, with a long grey face, close-cropped grey hair, and a beard trimmed so short it was more shadow than beard. He wore black, unadorned except for a single red rose enamelled on a silver brooch at his throat, and his hands, folded in his lap, were the hands of a gardener: broad, brown, the knuckles nicked and scarred, the nails cut short and still faintly rimmed with soil. His eyes were pale blue and very steady, and they went first to Hugh, and then, with no change of expression at all, to Floriana.

They stayed there.

Hugh had been in rooms with men who hated him. He had been in rooms with men who'd wanted him dead and had gone to some trouble to make it happen. But he could not remember a silence quite like this one, which went on and on while the banners stirred overhead and nobody in the hall seemed to breathe.

Everec cleared his throat - a small, apologetic sound - and Morwyn stepped forward and announced them with all their titles, her voice ringing off the stone. When she was done, Niestor inclined his head a precise inch.

"Lord Hugh," he said. His voice was quiet, dry, and carried perfectly. "You are welcome to Nazarovo. You'll forgive me if I don't rise. My knees are not what they were, and I find the older I get, the less I care to stand for people." He gestured at a table set to one side of the hall, laid with bread, cheese, and a pitcher of wine. "Eat, if you're hungry. Drink, if you're thirsty. Then tell me why you've come, and why you've brought her into my house."

There was no point in drawing it out. Hugh had rehearsed this a dozen times on the march, and found in the end that the simplest version was the best.

"I've come to offer you peace," he said. "Aleksandr is dead. The Fystov Keep is mine, rebuilt and garrisoned, and I mean to hold it. That pass has been a wound between your lands and mine for longer than either of us has been alive; raiders going south, soldiers coming north, nobody able to farm the high valleys for fear of who'd come down the road next. I'd like to close that wound. Free passage for honest trade, both directions. Neither of us to shelter the other's outlaws. And if trouble comes over the mountains from the east, as I think it will, we stand together rather than each waiting to see whether the other falls first."

Niestor listened without interrupting. When Hugh was finished, he was quiet for a while.

"A fair offer," he said at last. "Generous, even. You'll have heard I'm not a generous man, so you'll appreciate I don't say that lightly." He turned the rose brooch slightly between finger and thumb. "And you've brought her to sweeten it, I assume."

"I've brought her because she's my captain."

"Yes. Everec sent word." The pale eyes went back to Floriana. "Your captain burned Lesnoy."

Floriana did not move. Hugh did not know what Lesnoy was, and he saw by Morwyn's face that she did not either.

"Lesnoy was a village of eighty souls in the high valley south of here," Niestor went on, in that same quiet, dry voice. "Charcoal burners, mostly. A few goats. Five years ago, in the autumn, her people came down out of the pass and burned the charcoal stores, the barns, the chapel. When the people came out to fight the fires, they took the cattle, the silver, even the young men's boots, and rode away. Eleven people died. One of them was a boy of fifteen who went back into the chapel for the altar cloth because his grandmother had sewn it. His name was Teodor. He was my son."

Nobody in the hall said anything.

"So I'll be plain with you, Lord Hugh, since you've been plain with me." Niestor folded his gardener's hands again. "I'll take your peace. I'll take it gladly; I'm tired, and my people are tired, and you're right about the east. I'll sign whatever your herald puts in front of me. On one condition."

Hugh knew what it was before he said it. He suspected everyone in the hall did.

"Give her to me," said Niestor. "Today, here, and I'll give you everything else you've asked for."

* * *

There was a moment, afterwards, that Hugh would think about for a long time.

It was not long. A breath, perhaps two. But in that breath he saw it all laid out as clearly as a map: the alliance signed, the pass secure, trade flowing over the mountains, the high valleys farmed again, Erro safe from the north for a generation. Thousands of lives made easier. And on the other side of the scale, one woman, who'd burned a village and killed a boy, and by her own law and anyone else's had earned a rope ten times over.

He had made that kind of trade before. A long time ago, in another life, he had weighed a few lives against many and called it wisdom, and he had been very sure of himself while he did it. He still woke some nights with the smell of that sureness in his nose.

He felt Floriana go still beside him. Not tense; still. The stillness of someone who'd been waiting five years for this exact moment and had decided long ago how she'd meet it.

That's what they're waiting for, Morwyn had said. To see whether you'll trade them.

"No," said Hugh.

Niestor did not react, except that his thumb paused on the brooch.

"No," Hugh said again, more steadily. "I'm sorry for your son, my lord. I mean that. I'm sorry for Lesnoy. If I'd been lord of Erro five years ago it wouldn't have happened, or I'd have hanged the people who did it myself. But I wasn't, and it did, and she swore to me, and I swore to her in return. I don't sell the people who've sworn to me. Not for a pass, not for a peace, not for anything. If that's the price, I can't pay it."

"Then you've marched a long way for nothing," said Niestor.

"Perhaps. But I'd rather go home with nothing than go home having made myself the kind of man you'd be a fool to sign anything with." Hugh met the pale eyes squarely. "Think about it. If I'd hand you my own captain for a treaty, what would I hand Annaro's enemies for a better one? What would I hand you over to, the first time it suited me?"

Something moved in Niestor's face then. It might almost have been the beginning of a smile, though it went no further than the corner of his mouth.

"That's a lawyer's answer," he said.

"It's an honest one."

"The two aren't always strangers." Niestor sat back in his plain chair. "So. You won't give her to me. And I won't sign without her. And we're both too old and too tired to go to war over a pass that neither of us can afford to hold alone." He looked past Hugh. "What do you say to that, bandit? You've been very quiet. It isn't like you."

Floriana unfolded her arms.

"I was waiting to see what he'd do," she said.

"And now you've seen?"

"Now I've seen." She stepped forward, past Hugh, into the open floor before the dais, and Hugh nearly put out a hand to stop her, then thought better of it. Her boots rang on the flagstones. She stopped at the foot of the two steps and looked up at the man who'd hunted her for five years.

"I didn't know about your boy," she said. "That's not an excuse. It's just true. We hit Lesnoy because your tax men had taken the charcoal quota from the valleys on our side of the pass, three years running, until there was nobody left there who wasn't starving, and I was angry, and I was stupid, and I wanted you to know what it felt like to lose a winter's stores. I didn't go into the village. I sat my horse on the ridge, watched it burn, and thought I was very clever." She paused. "I didn't know the chapel had caught until we were three miles off and I saw the smoke turn black. I've known his name for four years. Teodor. I asked a pedlar. I don't know why. I think I wanted it to cost me something."

Niestor said nothing at all.

"I'm not asking you to forgive me," said Floriana. "I wouldn't, in your boots. And I won't let you hang me, because I've a hundred people out in your water meadow who'll be dead in a month without me. But I'll tell you what I'll do, if you'll let me." She took a breath. "Lesnoy. I'll rebuild it. The barns, the stores, the chapel, stone instead of timber. My people will cut the stone, haul it, and lay it with their own hands, and I'll be there every day until it's done, and when it's done I'll stand in front of whoever's left of those charcoal burners and tell them my name, and let them do as they please."

It was very quiet in the hall.

"That's worth less than a rope," said Niestor at length.

"Probably," said Floriana. "But a rope doesn't build anything."

* * *

They talked for two more hours after that, though most of it was Morwyn and Everec haggling over clauses while Hugh and Niestor sat across the table from each other and drank the baron's wine, which was better than Hugh had expected, and said little. Floriana stood by the door the whole time with her arms folded and spoke to nobody.

In the end it was decided the way most such things are decided: imperfectly, with nobody entirely pleased. The treaty would be signed, but not today. Floriana would go up to Lesnoy with a working party of thirty, under the eye of Niestor's own men, and labour there through the autumn. If the work was done and done well by the first snows, and if the people of Lesnoy did not kill her in the meantime, the baron would put his name to the parchment at the Fystov Keep, before witnesses from both sides of the pass.

If she ran, or if one of her people so much as lifted a hand against his, there'd be no treaty, and Niestor would come over the mountains in the spring.

"It's a gamble," Hugh said, when the baron had been helped from his chair by a servant and had limped from the hall, and they were alone with Everec and the banners.

"Oh, an enormous gamble," agreed Everec happily. He had produced a small bag of sugared almonds from somewhere inside his puffed sleeves and was eating them one at a time. "My lord adores a gamble. He so rarely gets to indulge. Most people simply do what he tells them, which is restful but dull, and I believe, if you will permit an old servant a liberty, that you have made him feel something, anything, for the first occasion in five years. Well - the second. I count the day he hanged the Brodsky brothers. Whether that is a good thing remains to be seen." He offered the bag. "Almond?"

"Thank you, no."

"Your loss. They are from my sister's tree." Everec popped another one into his mouth. "Do you know, he has never once spoken his son's name aloud in this hall? Not in five years. Not even to me. Tonight he said it to a bandit and a stranger." He chewed thoughtfully. "I find I shall have to revise my notes."

* * *

Hugh found Floriana on the castle wall - or she let herself be found, which with Floriana amounted to the same thing - an hour after dark, looking out over the water meadow where the campfires of her people burned in neat rows by the river.

He came and leaned on the parapet beside her and did not say anything for a while. It was cold up there, with a wind off the river, and the smell of roses came up faintly from the town below.

"You didn't have to do that," he said at last.

"Didn't I?" She did not look round. "You'd have walked away. You'd have marched us all home again and said it was the price of honour, and in the spring he'd have come over the pass, and half the people down there would have died for my pride. No. I had to."

"I'd have found another way."

"Maybe." She was quiet a moment. "You meant it, though. Back there, when you said no."

"I meant it."

"I know. I watched your face." She rubbed her thumb along a crack in the stone. "I've followed a lot of people, one way and another. Before I led anyone, I followed. And every single one of them, when it came down to it, would have sold me for a good enough price. I always knew it. I never blamed them; it's what people do." She shrugged. "So I suppose I didn't believe you, at Erro, when you said you wouldn't. I thought you were just saying the sort of thing lords say."

"And now?"

"And now I've got to go and haul stone for a man who wants me dead, in a village I burned, for six weeks in the autumn rain," said Floriana. "So thank you very much, my lord. It's been a wonderful day."

Hugh laughed despite himself, and after a moment, to his surprise, she did too, a short rough sound like something rusty being worked loose.

"I'll come up," he said. "Once a week, to see how it's going."

"You'll do no such thing. They'll think you don't trust me."

"Then I'll come up to help."

She turned and looked at him properly for the first time, and her expression was strange, something between exasperation and something he could not name.

"You're a very odd lord," she said.

"So I'm told."

They stood a while longer in silence, watching the fires. Down in the courtyard a door opened, spilling yellow light across the cobbles, and a figure came out and looked about, then saw them on the wall and started up the stone stair. It was Everec, puffing a little, his half-cloak wrapped tight against the wind. He reached the top, paused to catch his breath, and bowed with a flourish to each of them in turn.

"My lord. Madam. Forgive the intrusion, forgive it a thousand times." He straightened, and for once there was no smile on his round face. "The baron has asked me to bring a message to the captain, and to wait for her answer."

Floriana's hand had gone to the knife at her belt, almost without her seeming to notice. "Go on."

"He leaves for Lesnoy at first light, with you," said Everec, "and he would like to know whether you'd care to ride beside him, so that you can show him on the way which ridge you watched it burn from."
