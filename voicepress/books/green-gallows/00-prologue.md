# Prologue: The Furrow

*The Harding woods, 1780*

The thing had come down in the night and taken a quarter mile of beeches with it, and by morning the furrow was still steaming.

Alistair Harding stood at the top of it with a lantern he didn't need. The sun was up. He'd brought the lantern because he wanted something to hold. Below him the beeches lay root-up on both sides of the trench, and the earth between them was black and wet. Frost had come in hard overnight over the whole county. Not in there.

Three Hargrave men had walked up from the village with axes, because the squire's son had asked them to and the squire's son paid. They stopped at the lip of the furrow. The youngest was perhaps fourteen, all wrists and ears in a coat cut down from his father's. He crossed himself. His father cuffed him for it, then did the same when he thought nobody was looking.

"Well," said the father. His name was Tom Webb and he'd cut wood on this estate for thirty years. "That's... I don't know what that is, sir."

"Nor do I, Tom."

"Is it the French?"

"No."

"Only Peggy Dunn said she heard thunder. Then she said it weren't thunder, it were more of a - " He glanced at his boy and stopped. "Aye. Well. Peggy says a lot."

"You'll wait here," said Alistair, which they had already decided to do.

It took him the better part of an hour to reach the bottom. The trench was deeper than it looked. The walls kept sliding and twice he went in to the knee. The mud was warm. It smelled of nothing at all. At the far end, half buried where it had finally stopped, lay a hull the colour of a frozen pond, greenish grey, ribbed along its length. He couldn't find a seam. He couldn't find anything he'd call a rivet. It was very long and nothing about it was a ship, and he knew it was a ship anyway.

It was humming. One note, so low he felt it in his back teeth more than heard it.

Where the hull had split against a boulder there was a gap. When he lifted the lantern, it found a hand.

Long-fingered, pale blue, with frost on the nails, it lay palm up on the lip of the break and Alistair stood looking at it for some time. He had been an inventor for eleven years and a failure at it for ten. He'd built a loom that tore its own threads and a pump that drowned the man who demonstrated it, and his father had stopped asking what he did all day. Looked at the hand. Thought, quite clearly: this is the most important thing that will ever happen to me.

The fingers moved. They curled toward the palm, slowly, and opened again toward the light. Alistair didn't take the hand. He stepped over it.

Inside it was close and cold, and the walls gave a little under his glove. The hum was louder here. Under it, further in, something else was going. A pulse, steady and unhurried. Each beat lit something faintly green far down the dark and then let it go.

He followed it, ducking under ribs that curved overhead, past shapes he didn't look at closely because he didn't yet have names for them, with the lantern swinging and the pulse getting no louder and no nearer for a long time, until he found the source at last. It was a small thing. A bulb of the same pond-coloured glass, set into the wall at the height of his chest. Beating.

Back at the break in the hull, something made a sound. He didn't think it was a word.

He had no idea what the bulb was for. He knew what it was doing, though. He'd built enough machines that called for help when they broke.

He set the lantern down. Lifted his boot and brought the heel down on the glass, once, then again, *crack* and *crack*, until the pulse stopped.

The hum went on. Outside, the woods had gone very quiet.

When he climbed back up the furrow his boots were black to the knee and he was carrying nothing. The Hargrave men looked at him and did not ask. The boy was staring past him, down the trench, at the break where the blue hand still lay on the lip of the glass. It wasn't reaching for anything now.

"Fetch rope," said Alistair. "And a cart. More men, if the village has them."

Tom Webb looked at the hull, then at the squire's son, then at his own boy.

"How many, sir?"

"All of them."
