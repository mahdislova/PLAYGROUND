# Prologue: The Furrow

*The Harding woods, 1780*

The thing had come down in the night and taken a quarter mile of beeches with it, and by morning the furrow was still steaming.

Alistair Harding stood at the top of it with a lantern he didn't need. The sun was up. He'd brought the lantern because a man ought to be holding something when he walks toward the end of the world, and his hands had wanted occupying since four o'clock that morning, when the windows of the Hall had gone green and then white and then rattled in their frames, and his father had sat up in bed and said, quite distinctly, "French," and gone back to sleep.

Not the French. Below him the beeches lay root-up on both sides of the trench, two neat ranks of them, as if something had combed the wood. The earth between was black and wet. Frost had come in hard over the whole county overnight. Not in there.

Three Hargrave men had walked up from the village with axes, because the squire's son had asked them to and the squire's son paid. They stopped at the lip of the furrow. The youngest was perhaps fourteen, all wrists and ears in a coat cut down from his father's. He crossed himself. His father cuffed him for it, then did the same when he thought nobody was looking. Alistair, who was looking, decided not to have seen. You let a man keep his small superstitions on a morning like this. You might want to borrow one.

"Well," said the father. Tom Webb had cut wood on this estate for thirty years and had never once, in Alistair's hearing, been lost for a word. "That's... I don't know what that is, sir."

"Nor do I, Tom. Isn't it mar - well. Isn't it something."

Tom looked at him as if he'd suggested they eat it.

"Only Peggy Dunn said she heard thunder. Then she said it weren't thunder, it were more of a - " He glanced at his boy. "Aye. Well. Peggy says a lot."

"You'll wait here," said Alistair, which they had plainly already decided to do.

It took him the better part of an hour to reach the bottom. The walls kept sliding, and twice he went in to the knee, and the mud was warm as a bed somebody has just left. Smelled of nothing at all. Eleven years he had been an inventor. Ten of them a failure. He had built a loom that tore its own threads and a pump that drowned the man who demonstrated it, and his father had long since stopped asking what he did all day, and the Royal Society returned his letters unopened, which he felt was taking things rather far. And now this. Laid at his feet, in his own wood, as though the sky had read his correspondence and taken pity.

Steady, Alistair. You haven't seen it yet.

Then he had.

It lay half buried at the end of the furrow where it had finally stopped, and it was very long. Longer than the Hall. Longer than the church at Hargrave with the tower laid flat beside it. The colour of a frozen pond, greenish grey, ribbed along its length, with no seam he could find and nothing he'd call a rivet, and it went back into the hillside so far that the morning mist took the end of it. Nothing about it was a ship. It was a ship anyway. He stood in the mud with the useless lantern and understood, in the flat calm way a man understands that he has been shot, that every map he had ever been taught was a map of one small room, and somebody had just opened the door.

It was humming. One note, so low it went into his back teeth before it reached his ears.

Where the hull had split against a boulder there was a gap. When he lifted the lantern, it found a hand.

Long-fingered, pale blue, with frost on the nails, it lay palm up on the lip of the break. A hand. Of course a ship has sailors. Of course. Some fool part of him had been picturing clockwork.

The fingers moved. They curled toward the palm, slowly, and opened again toward the light.

Alistair didn't take the hand. He would think about that, later, for the rest of his life, and he would never once be able to remember deciding. Only the hand, opening. And his own boot, lifting over it.

Inside it was close and cold, and the walls gave a little under his glove, like gristle. The hum was louder here. Under it, further in, something else was going: a pulse, steady and unhurried. Each beat lit something faintly green far down the dark and then let it go.

Followed it, ducking under ribs that curved overhead, past shapes he didn't look at closely because he didn't yet have names for them and wasn't sure he wanted any, with the lantern swinging and the pulse getting no louder and no nearer for a long time, until at last he found it. A small thing, after all that. A bulb of the same pond-coloured glass, set into the wall at the height of his chest. Beating.

Back at the break in the hull, something made a sound. Not a word. He was fairly sure it wasn't a word.

He had no idea what the bulb was for. What it was doing, though, was plain as day. Every failed machine he'd ever built had done the same thing in its last minutes, rattling and flashing, shouting into the empty workshop for somebody to come. It was calling home. And whoever was at home would come, and they would take their ship and their sailor, and Alistair Harding would be left in a muddy trench with a lantern, explaining to the Royal Society that he'd very nearly had something.

No.

Set the lantern down. Lifted his boot and brought the heel down on the glass, *crack*, and again, *crack*, until the pulse stopped.

The hum went on. Outside, the woods had gone very quiet.

When he climbed back up the furrow his boots were black to the knee and he was carrying nothing. The Hargrave men looked at him and did not ask. The boy was staring past him, down the trench, at the break where the blue hand still lay on the lip of the glass. It wasn't reaching for anything now.

"Fetch rope," said Alistair. "And a cart. More men, if the village has them."

Tom Webb looked at the hull, then at the squire's son, then at his own boy, and something went out of his face that Alistair would not see in it again.

"How many, sir?"

"All of them."

It was the kind of thing a young man says on the best morning of his life. Thirty years on, when there was no Hargrave left to fetch men from, he would remember saying it.
