# Chapter One: Nixon's Chair

*Nathaniel*

The gauntlet threw him into the coal bin at a quarter past eleven, which was a pity, because he'd only just had his hair cut.

One moment Nathaniel was standing at the bench with the armature strapped to his forearm and the coil spinning up to a clean high whine, the note he'd been chasing for a month, the exact and perfect note, and the next he was on his back in the coal with his legs in the air and the whine coming down through every other note there was. Something on the bench went *pank* and rolled away. A lump of anthracite settled on his chest and stayed there.

Well. That was new.

He lay still for a while. It seemed the dignified thing, and nobody was watching, and there was a crack in the coach-house ceiling shaped like Portugal that he'd never had leisure to study.

The workshop had been built by his grandfather for a carriage nobody could now remember, two streets back from the main house, with a skylight that leaked over the lathe and a stove that worked when it felt appreciated. Nathaniel slept out here more nights than not. His mother called it his little house, in the voice she used for other people's dogs. His father didn't call it anything. Nailed to the beam above the bench hung the gauntlet's ancestors, eleven of them, each with the date of its failure inked on a luggage label, like a row of hanged men in a ballad. This one was to have been number twelve. This one he was taking to the Exhibition tonight, in front of two hundred people and a professor who'd come all the way from Edinburgh to see it.

Get up, Nat. Coal's not getting any softer.

Got up. Brushed off what he could, unstrapped the gauntlet, opened the casing.

The coil was fine. Of course it was fine. It sat there in its housing looking beautiful and innocent, a spiral of drawn gallowglass wire wound round a brass core, each turn laid at a slight angle to the last so the whole thing climbed. It solved, or nearly solved, the problem every engineer at the Works had been fighting for ten years. Heat bleed. Any coil of green threw off half its power as warmth before it did anything useful. His didn't. His held it, which was why it had thrown him into the coal. It had held it a good deal longer than he'd expected and then let go all at once, like a man who'd been very polite at dinner and finally said what he thought.

He'd drawn it at three in the morning, six weeks ago. Remembered waking, and lighting the lamp, and the pencil moving. Couldn't remember thinking of it. Not one step.

Now he took a fresh sheet and tried again to get there from first principles. Why the angle. Why that angle. Why brass. He got as far as the third turn and stopped, as he had every time, with the queasy feeling of reading his own handwriting in a letter he had no memory of sending. The thing was right. Anyone could see it was right. And Nathaniel Harding, who had taken apart every clock in Harding House by the age of ten and put most of them back, could not for the life of him say how he'd known.

Two light raps at the coach-house door, then a third.

"It's open, Byron."

Byron came in sideways, because the door stuck and had stuck for eleven years and Byron had never once mentioned it. He was in his morning coat, white gloves folded over one wrist, and he took in the bench, the gauntlet, the scorch mark on the ceiling, the coal, and Nathaniel, in that order and without any change of expression at all. Byron had been butler at Harding House since before Nathaniel was born. He was sixty-four, silver-haired, straight as a rule, with a long grave face that had never in Nathaniel's lifetime been surprised by anything, and Nathaniel had spent a good part of his childhood trying to change that. Frogs. A clockwork mouse. Once, regrettably, a small fire.

"Luncheon, Master Nat. Her ladyship asked particularly."

"How particularly?"

"She said 'see that Nathaniel comes', sir. I took that as particularly."

Nathaniel looked at the coil. Six hours till the Exhibition. He could rewind the outer turns, reset the angle half a degree, bleed it slower. If he worked through lunch.

"Tell her I'll be along. I've just got to - "

"Particularly," said Byron again, mildly, and held the door.

He'd lost, then. He usually did. They went across the wet yard together, and on the back stairs Byron stopped him on the landing and, without a word, began picking coal out of his hair. He'd done the same when Nathaniel was eight, with the same small dry clicks of the tongue. Each lump went carefully into Byron's own coat pocket so it wouldn't mark the runner.

"You'll be wanting a fresh collar, sir."

"Is it... well. How bad?"

Byron considered the collar for a respectful moment.

"It's an engineer's collar, sir. Her ladyship has asked for a son's."

* * *

The dining room at Harding House had been built to be lit, and it was. The chandelier over the table was the old Works pattern, nine tiers of cut crystal around a gallowglass heart, and at noon on a grey November day it threw a light so clean and green-white that the silver looked wet. Nathaniel had taken that chandelier apart at twelve to see how the feed worked. Three days and a great deal of shouting to put it back. He still remembered how the core seated: you pushed it in and felt it bite, and the whole thing hummed against your palm for a moment, warm, like something pleased to be home.

His father sat at the head of the table with the light behind him, which was not an accident; Eldgard Harding had never in his life sat with a window in his face. His mother sat at the foot. Jamie was in his place on his mother's right, carving the end off a bread roll with the absorption of a surgeon, and at his father's right hand there was an empty chair.

Nathaniel had sat below the salt for most of his life. There were reasons. He was the second son. He was late to everything and usually smelled of oil. But the chair at his father's right hand was empty and he was standing beside it, and it seemed such a small and ordinary thing, to pull it out and sit down.

"That's Nixon's chair, Nat," said his father.

He didn't look up from his plate. He didn't say it unkindly. It was the voice he used for a wrong door.

Nathaniel stood up again. Moved down one place. A footman came and quietly squared Nixon's chair back under the table for whoever was coming back to it.

Which wasn't going to be Nixon. Not today. Nixon was in the east on their father's orders, nine days' ride out along the lines, looking at whatever it was in the east that needed looking at. Nobody at this table ever said what. Nathaniel had asked, once, and been told it was Guard business, and that he had an Exhibition to worry about.

So he worried about the Exhibition. He ate soup. Nixon's chair stayed empty all through the grouse, and he couldn't stop looking at it. A loose tooth of a chair.

"Nathaniel." His mother set down her spoon. She had a way of setting down cutlery that called a table to order. Always had. Josephine Harding was fifty and handsome and wore grey silk with black lace at the throat, because the Hardings were always mourning someone, somewhere, and it saved changing. Her gloves lay folded beside her plate. She was the only woman Nathaniel knew who took her gloves off to eat and laid them out like a second set of cutlery. "We've had a letter from Lord Lambrick."

Here it comes, whatever it is. "Have we."

"You've met his daughter."

He had to think. "Tamara. At the Guildhall thing. She asked me about the con - about the condensers."

"Did she," said his mother, in a voice that suggested this was more than she'd dared hope. "You're to marry her."

He waited for the rest. There didn't seem to be any. Typical of the family, really: forty years of a man's life, settled in two sentences, and the second one was about manners.

"It's settled," said his father, still to his plate. "The families have agreed. You'll meet her properly tonight at the Exhibition. Be civil."

"I'm always civil."

"Be more civil than that."

"I'll be... yes. All right." What else was there? Nobody had asked. That was the thing he'd remember afterwards, oddly. Not that it was done, but that it had been done so neatly, over grouse, between one course and the next, as though they were settling who'd have the last of the claret.

Across the table, Jamie set down his knife. Jamie was twenty-five, fair where Nathaniel was dark, easy where Nathaniel was awkward, the son people looked at first in any room and who somehow made you feel he'd looked at you first. He raised his glass, solemnly, to his brother.

"Congratulations, Nat," said Jamie. "You've been sold."

Their mother didn't laugh. Their father did, once, a short sound, and went back to his grouse. Nathaniel felt his ears go hot. He turned on Jamie, ready to be angry, and found his brother watching him with no joke in his face at all. Jamie hadn't slept. Not properly, not for days; there were bruises under his eyes the colour of weak tea, and he'd cut that bread roll into eleven pieces and eaten none of them. Something on the far side of today had its teeth in him.

What's wrong with you? Nathaniel nearly said it aloud.

Above them the chandelier dipped.

It wasn't much. The light went down toward brown for the space of three breaths, and the hum of the feed dropped half a note, and then it came back up. Nathaniel looked up. Nobody else did. His father cut a piece of grouse. His mother refolded her napkin. Jamie looked at his glass. Every one of them had seen it, and every one of them had decided not to.

Third time this week. He'd counted. Last night he'd gone round the house with a pencil and a notebook and his father's best watch and timed the dips in the hall, the library and the landing, and they'd all come at the same moment, to the second. Which meant the fault was in the feed and not the chandeliers. In the Works. Something at the Works, under the Works, was faltering, and every lamp in London was faltering with it, and his family was eating grouse.

Opened his mouth to say so.

"Eat something, Nat," said Jamie quietly. He reached across the corner of the table while their parents were talking and squeezed Nathaniel's shoulder, once, two fingers and a thumb. Their old signal from the receiving lines of boyhood, when one of them looked about to say something unforgivable to a bishop. Do you want out? Nathaniel covered his brother's hand with his own, which meant: no. I'll stay.

Jamie smiled, a little, and let go.

Years afterward Nathaniel would try to remember whether that hand had been shaking. He never could.

* * *

After luncheon his father took Jamie into the study, and Nathaniel, who'd meant to go straight back to the workshop and the coil, found he'd stopped on the landing outside the study door instead, holding a fresh collar he hadn't yet put on.

Eavesdropping, Nat. Very gentlemanly. Byron would be ashamed of you. Byron would also, he suspected, have done exactly the same.

The door was open three inches. It was always open three inches. The hinge had dropped years ago, and his father wouldn't have the carpenter in, because the carpenter talked.

"Tonight the family stands up," his father was saying. There was the small clean sound of a decanter being unstoppered. "You give the speech. You smile for them. You know how."

"I know how."

"Whatever you think of it. You smile for them anyway."

Through the gap Nathaniel could see a slice of the desk, and on it, near his father's hand, the thing his father kept there. It had sat on that desk all Nathaniel's life and he'd never once been allowed to touch it. A fist of greenish glass the size of a large orange, caged in brass, with a faint light moving in it like weed in a slow river. Visitors took it for a paperweight. Nathaniel, at nine, had taken it for a heart, and been sent to bed without supper for saying so at table.

It was humming. Funny. He'd never noticed that before. Or he had, and decided not to, like everyone else in this house.

"Does it know where it is?" said Jamie.

A pause. Glass on glass. His father handing his brother a drink.

"Drink that," said Eldgard Harding. "And then go and change. You're giving the speech."

Nathaniel stood on the landing with his collar in his hand and waited for one of them to come to the door and find him there. Neither did. After a while he went downstairs and out across the yard to the coach house, and sat down at the bench in front of a coil he didn't understand, and began, very carefully, to take it apart.
