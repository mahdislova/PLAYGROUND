# Chapter One: Nixon's Chair

*Nathaniel*

The gauntlet threw him into the coal bin at a quarter past eleven, which was a pity, because he'd only just had his hair cut.

One moment Nathaniel was standing at the bench with the armature strapped to his forearm and the coil spinning up to a clean high whine, exactly the note he'd been chasing for a month, and the next he was on his back in the coal with his legs in the air and the whine coming down through all the notes there were. Something on the bench went *pank* and rolled away. A lump of anthracite settled on his chest and stayed there.

He lay there for a while. It seemed the dignified thing.

The workshop was a coach house his grandfather had built for a carriage nobody could now remember, two streets back from the main house, with a skylight that leaked over the lathe and a stove that worked when it felt appreciated. Nathaniel had been sleeping out here more nights than not since the spring. His mother called it his little house. His father didn't call it anything. Above the bench, nailed to the beam in a row, hung the gauntlet's ancestors, eleven of them, each with the date of its failure inked on a luggage label. This one would have been number twelve, and it was meant to be the one he took to the Exhibition tonight, in front of two hundred people and a professor who had come all the way from Edinburgh to see it.

He got up eventually and brushed off what he could, then unstrapped the gauntlet and opened the casing to look at the coil.

It was beautiful. He could admit that, alone, with coal in his collar. A spiral of drawn gallowglass wire, wound tight around a core of brass, each turn laid at a slight angle to the last so the whole thing climbed. It solved, or nearly solved, the problem every engineer at the Works had been fighting for ten years. Heat bleed. Any coil of green threw off half its power as warmth before it did anything useful. His didn't. His held it, which was why it had thrown him into the coal. It had held it a good deal longer than he'd expected and then let it all go at once.

He'd drawn it at three in the morning six weeks ago. Remembered waking, and lighting the lamp, and the pencil moving. Couldn't remember thinking of it.

Now he took a fresh sheet and tried again to work it out from first principles: why the angle, why that angle, why brass. He got as far as the third turn and stopped, as he had every time, with the uneasy feeling of a man reading his own handwriting in a letter he doesn't remember sending. The thing was right. He could see it was right. He couldn't see how he'd known.

There was a knock at the coach-house door, two light raps, then a third.

"It's open, Byron."

Byron came in sideways, because the door stuck. He was in his morning coat, with his white gloves folded over one wrist, and he took in the bench, the gauntlet, the scorch on the ceiling, the coal, and Nathaniel, in that order, without any change of expression at all. Byron had been butler at Harding House since before Nathaniel was born, and he had a face like a well-kept clock.

"Luncheon, Master Nat. Her ladyship asked particularly."

"Particularly?"

"She said 'see that Nathaniel comes', sir. I took it as particular."

Nathaniel looked at the coil. Six hours till the Exhibition. He could rewind the outer turns, reset the angle, bleed it slower. If he worked through lunch.

"Tell her I'll be along. I've just got to - "

"Particularly," said Byron again, mildly.

They went across the yard together, and on the back stairs Byron stopped him on the landing and, without a word, began picking lumps of coal out of his hair. He'd done the same when Nathaniel was eight, with the same small dry clicks of the tongue. Put each lump in his own coat pocket so it wouldn't mark the runner.

"You'll want to change your collar, sir."

"Is it... well. How bad?"

Byron considered the collar. "It's an engineer's collar, sir. Her ladyship has asked for a son's."

* * *

The dining room at Harding House had been built to be lit, and it was. The chandelier over the table was the old Works pattern, nine tiers of cut crystal around a gallowglass heart, and at noon on a grey November day it threw a light so clean and green-white that the silver looked wet. Nathaniel had taken that chandelier apart when he was twelve to see how the feed worked. It had taken him three days and a great deal of shouting to put it back. He still remembered how the core seated: you pushed it in and felt it bite, like a tooth into an apple, and then the whole thing hummed against your palm for a moment.

His father sat at the head of the table with the light behind him. His mother sat at the foot. Jamie was already in his place on his mother's right, carving the end off a bread roll with great concentration, and on his father's right there was an empty chair.

Nathaniel had been at the far end of the table, below the salt, for most of his life. There were reasons. He was the second son. He was late to everything and usually smelled of oil. But the chair at his father's right hand was empty and he was standing next to it, and it seemed a small and ordinary thing to do, to pull it out and sit down.

"That's Nixon's chair, Nat," said his father.

He didn't look up from his plate. He didn't say it unkindly. It was the voice he used for a wrong door.

Nathaniel stood up again. Moved down one place. Somebody, a footman, came and quietly pushed Nixon's chair back in under the table so it sat square for whoever was coming back to it.

Nixon wasn't coming back to it. Not today. Nixon was in the east, on his father's orders, nine days' ride out along the lines, looking at whatever it was in the east that needed looking at. Nobody at this table had said what that was. Nathaniel had asked, once, and been told it was Guard business, and that he had an Exhibition to worry about.

So he worried about the Exhibition. He ate soup. Nixon's chair stayed empty all through the grouse.

"Nathaniel." His mother set down her spoon. She had a way of setting down cutlery that called a table to order, and she knew it. Josephine Harding was fifty and handsome and wore grey silk with black lace at the throat, because the Hardings were always mourning someone, somewhere, and it saved changing. Her gloves lay folded beside her plate. She was the only person Nathaniel knew who took her gloves off to eat and laid them out like a second set of cutlery. "We've had a letter from Lord Lambrick."

"Have we."

"You've met his daughter."

He had to think. "Tamara. At the Guildhall thing. She asked me about the con - about the condensers."

"Did she," said his mother, in a voice that suggested this was more than she'd hoped for. "You're to marry her."

He waited for the rest. There didn't seem to be any.

"It's settled," said his father, still to his plate. "The families have agreed. You'll meet her properly tonight at the Exhibition. Be civil."

"I'm always civil."

"Be more civil than that."

"I'll be... yes. All right."

Across the table, Jamie set down his knife. Jamie was twenty-five, fair where Nathaniel was dark, easy where Nathaniel was awkward, the son people looked at first in any room and the one who always, somehow, made you feel he'd looked at you first. He raised his glass, solemnly, to his brother.

"Congratulations, Nat," said Jamie. "You've been sold."

Their mother didn't laugh. Their father did, once, a short sound, and then went back to his grouse. Nathaniel felt his ears go hot. He looked at Jamie, ready to be angry, and found his brother watching him with an expression he couldn't read at all. It wasn't a joke, whatever it had sounded like. Jamie looked tired. Hadn't slept, Nathaniel thought, not properly, not for days. There was something on the far side of today that his brother was trying very hard not to think about.

Above them the chandelier dipped.

It wasn't much. The light went down toward brown for the space of three breaths, and the hum of the feed dropped half a note, and then it came back up. Nathaniel looked up. Nobody else did. His father cut a piece of grouse. His mother refolded her napkin. Jamie looked at his glass. Every one of them had seen it, and every one of them had decided not to.

That was the third time this week. Nathaniel had counted. He'd also, last night, gone round the house with a pencil and a notebook and timed the dips in the hall, the library and the landing, and they had all come at the same moment, to the second, which meant the fault was in the feed and not the chandeliers. In the Works. Something at the Works, under the Works, was faltering, and every lamp in London was faltering with it, and his family was eating grouse.

He opened his mouth to say so.

"Eat something, Nat," said Jamie quietly. He reached across the corner of the table as their parents were talking and squeezed Nathaniel's shoulder, once, two fingers and a thumb, their old signal from the receiving lines of boyhood, when one of them looked about to say something unforgivable to a bishop. *Do you want out?* Nathaniel covered his brother's hand with his own. *No. I'll stay.*

Jamie smiled, a little, and let go.

* * *

After luncheon his father took Jamie into the study, and Nathaniel, who had meant to go straight back to the workshop and the coil, found that he'd stopped on the landing outside the study door instead, holding a fresh collar he hadn't yet put on.

The door was open three inches. It was always open three inches. The hinge had dropped years ago, and his father wouldn't have the carpenter in, because the carpenter talked.

"Tonight the family stands up," his father was saying. There was the small clean sound of a decanter being unstoppered. "You give the speech. You smile for them. You know how."

"I know how."

"Whatever you think of it. You smile for them anyway."

From where he stood Nathaniel could see a slice of the desk, and on it, near his father's hand, the thing his father kept there. It had sat on that desk all Nathaniel's life and he had never once been allowed to touch it. A fist of greenish glass the size of a large orange, caged in brass, with a faint light moving in it like weed in a slow river. Visitors took it for a paperweight. Nathaniel, at nine, had taken it for a heart, and had been sent to bed without supper for saying so at table.

It was humming. Never noticed that before. Or he had, and had decided not to.

"Does it know where it is?" said Jamie.

There was a pause. Glass on glass. His father handing his brother a drink.

"Drink that," said Eldgard Harding. "And then go and change. You're giving the speech."

Stood on the landing with his collar in his hand. Waited for one of them to come to the door and find him there. Neither did. After a while he went downstairs, and out across the yard to the coach house, and sat down at the bench in front of a coil he didn't understand, and began, very carefully, to take it apart.
