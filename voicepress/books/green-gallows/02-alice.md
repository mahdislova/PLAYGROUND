# Chapter Two: Me. Always Me.

*Alice*

Alice was meant to be on the corner of Lime Wharf Street, watching for constables. She was forty feet up in the rafters of the Lambrick warehouse instead, with pigeon muck on both knees and her heart going hard enough to hear.

Nobody had seen her come in. That was the point. She'd gone up the hoist chain on the loading door while Miriam and Jennie were still crossing the roof, and then along the tie-beam on her belly, one arm hooked over, the old wood cold and furred with dust under her cheek. Now she lay flat above the counting-house end of the floor and tried to breathe through her nose. Below her the rain came down through a broken pane in the skylight and went *tik, tik, tik* on a tarpaulin. Somewhere a pigeon was making its stupid noise. *Hrrooo. Hrrooo.*

She'd had twenty years of the corner. She was twenty today.

The warehouse was long and low. It smelled of tar and river. Crates stood in rows down the middle, stencilled for Rotterdam, each one sealed with a blob of red wax and the Lambrick ram pressed into it. Somebody had prised the lid half off the nearest, and inside, nested in straw, was glass. Core-cut gallowglass in bars the length of her forearm, greenish even in that light, and she could see where the Works crest had been chiselled off the end of every bar. Somebody had done it carefully. Somebody had been paid to.

Four men down there. A clerk at a high desk with a lamp, a big one sitting on a crate eating a pie, and two by the door, smoking. None of them looked up. Men never looked up. Miriam had told her that when she was six, and it was the first useful thing anyone ever taught her.

On the skylight a shadow moved. Then a second, smaller. Miriam on the glass with Jennie beside her holding the rope, both of them crouched, and Alice could see her sister's lips going. Counting. She always counted before she dropped. Alice had asked her once what she was counting to. *To when I go,* Miriam had said, which wasn't an answer at all.

Miriam was twenty-four and the most dangerous person Alice had ever met, and she didn't look it, which was the trick. She was tall and thin, with black hair pinned flat under a man's cap and a face all cheekbone and freckle, a chip off one front tooth from a fight she'd won. She wore a man's oilskin cut down to fit, trousers tucked into soft-soled boots she'd stolen off a ballet master, and on her hands the red mittens with the fingertips cut out that she'd worn every winter Alice could remember. Even on a job. Especially on a job. People remembered the mittens and forgot the face.

Jennie was smaller, nineteen, square, with a white scar that went through her left eyebrow and made her look permanently surprised by something she'd decided not to mention. She had laundry hands, red to the wrist. She didn't talk on jobs. She didn't talk much off them.

Neither of them knew Alice was here. Alice found she was grinning into the dust.

Miriam stopped counting. She took the rope from Jennie, wrapped it once round her forearm, lifted the broken pane out of its frame with two fingers like a lid off a pot. Dropped.

It was beautiful. Alice would have said so to anyone. One moment the clerk at his desk was dipping his pen. The next Miriam was standing on the desk in front of him with rain coming down around her, his pen was in her hand instead of his, and he was looking at it.

"Evening," said Miriam.

The pie man stood up. The two by the door dropped their pipes and came. Miriam stepped down off the desk to meet them as if she'd been asked to dance. Alice watched the first go down holding his knee. The second got a fistful of Miriam's oilskin and then didn't have it any more, and folded over a crate with a noise like *hoff*. That left the pie man.

Three, then. Alice shifted on the beam to see better.

The beam was old. Her boot found the one place on it that was rotten. A lump of something soft broke away under her heel and went down, and with it, from the dark on her left, went every pigeon in the roof at once. *Brrrap-brap-brap-brap.* Wings everywhere. Feathers. Muck. The pie man looked up. The clerk looked up.

And Miriam looked up, straight at her, and in that half second her face did something Alice had never seen it do.

The clerk had a knife. He came off his stool with it while Miriam was still looking at the rafters, and Alice saw it, the whole of it, the blade coming low out of his coat, and she opened her mouth and nothing at all came out.

Miriam didn't turn round. She didn't need to. Put out one hand behind her, without looking. Caught him by the wrist, turned her hip into him, threw him. One hand. Over the hip and down, and the clerk went into the crate behind her back-first and lay in the straw among the gallowglass bars looking at the skylight with an expression of great surprise.

Alice had watched that throw her whole life. She'd practised it on a flour sack, on the bedpost, on Jennie once, which Jennie hadn't liked. She had never made it work. Not once.

The pie man sat back down on his crate and put his hands on his knees. He was not a fool.

"You can come down now," said Miriam, to the rafters, without raising her voice.

Alice came down. It took a long time and nobody helped her. When her boots finally touched the floor Miriam was already at the iron safe under the clerk's desk with the clerk's own keys, and she didn't look round.

"I was keeping watch," said Alice.

"I can see that."

"From up there you can see all four of - "

"Bag."

Alice held the bag open. Into it went a fat leather purse that chinked, and after it a ledger bound in brown calf with the Lambrick ram stamped on the cover in gold. Miriam flicked through the ledger once before she put it in, frowning, then shut it hard. Jennie had come down the rope and was standing by the door with the two smokers' pipes in her hand, one of them still lit. Drew on it, thoughtful. Looked at Alice, then away.

"Window," said Miriam.

The window at the river end was small and high, opening onto a ledge above the water. The drop to the barge below was further than Alice liked. She stood under it looking up.

"Who goes out first?" she said. "If they've got someone on the wharf. Who goes first?"

Miriam was already on the sill. She had the bag over her shoulder and the rain behind her. Looked down at Alice with no expression at all.

"Me," she said. "Always me."

Then she was gone.

* * *

The Shadwell laundry stood on the corner of Cable Street and Dock Lane, three storeys of brown brick weeping steam at every window. Inside it was always August. The coppers boiled in a row along the back wall, each one with a girl over it working a dolly-stick, *thunk-slosh, thunk-slosh*, and the mangles groaned on their cranks, and the air was so thick with lye and starch that Alice could taste it on her teeth. Sheets hung from the ceiling on lines in long white aisles. You walked between them and couldn't see where you were going, and somewhere at the far end of the aisles, at a table under the only window that didn't steam, sat Mother Grise.

She was seventy, or said she was. She was small and wide. Sat very upright in a carved chair that had come out of a church, in black bombazine gone green at the seams. Her cap was white lace starched so stiff it could have stood up on the table by itself. Her face was soft, pink, creased like a baked apple. One of her eyes was milky. You never knew which one was looking at you. Under the table she wore a man's carpet slippers. Her hands, folded in front of her, were the colour of boiled ham, swollen across every knuckle from fifty years in hot water, and she wore no rings on them at all. Grise didn't need to show what she had.

Miriam put the ledger on the table in front of her and kept the bag on her shoulder.

Grise didn't touch it at first. She looked at it. Then she drew it toward her with one finger and began to turn the pages, slowly, licking her thumb. Halfway through she stopped. Read something twice. Smiled. Alice didn't like the smile. It was a nice smile. That was what she didn't like.

"Well," said Grise. "Isn't Hector a clever boy. Isn't he a worried clever boy." She turned another page. "Was there a purse, Miriam?"

"Was there?"

"I'm asking, dear. I'm only... asking."

"There was a ledger," said Miriam. "There it is."

Grise looked at her with the good eye. Then she laughed, a little dry sound, *heh*. Closed the ledger and set her hand flat on top of it.

"Keep it, then," she said. "Buy yourself something warm."

Alice let out the breath she'd been holding. She was standing a little behind Miriam's shoulder, where she always stood, and she hadn't meant to be seen. But Grise's head turned, and the milky eye came round and rested on her. It went over her slowly from her boots up. Over the coat that had been Miriam's, over the hands, the hair. Alice had once watched Grise go over a bolt of good Irish linen a girl lifted from a draper's. Feeling for the weave. It was that look.

"Your sister's quick," said Grise. "I saw her come in the door just now. Didn't make a sound."

Miriam moved. She didn't move much. One step, sideways, so she was between them.

"Keep your hands off her."

There was a pause in which every mangle in the room seemed to go on very loudly.

"I haven't touched her, dear," said Grise, mildly. "I only looked."

"Don't look, either."

Outside in the street, Miriam walked fast with her head down and didn't speak, and Alice had to half run to keep up. They were all the way to the corner of Dock Lane before Alice remembered her cap. She'd taken it off in the heat and left it on a mangle. It was the only cap she had.

"I've left my - "

"Leave it."

"It's my *cap*, Miri."

Miriam made a noise through her teeth and stopped under a lamp, which was one of the dark ones, and folded her arms. "Go on, then. Quick."

Alice went back in through the side door, the one the girls used. The heat closed over her again. Found the cap on the end of the third mangle, damp now and smelling of starch. As she turned to go she saw down the long aisle of sheets to the window and the table.

Grise was holding a letter. It was folded small and sealed with plain black wax, no crest, no device, nothing pressed into it at all, and Grise was breaking the seal with her thumbnail. She read it. She read it twice. Then she lifted her head and called down the room, not loudly.

"Jennie, love."

Jennie came out from behind a copper, drying her red hands on her apron.

Grise said something Alice didn't catch. Then a little louder, two words, as Jennie was already turning away.

"Follow Miriam."

Jennie nodded. She took her shawl off its peg. Alice ducked back behind a sheet, though she didn't know why she ducked, and went out by the side door with her cap pressed against her chest.

Grise kept an eye on everyone. It was how she stayed Grise. And it was Jennie, and Jennie was all right. Jennie had once carried Alice two miles on her back with a sprained ankle and never said a word about it after. If anyone was going to be following Miriam about tonight, Alice was glad it was her.

She jammed the cap on her head. Miriam was waiting under the dark lamp, and they walked home.

* * *

The Bounds room was four flights up over a tallow chandler's on Gin Lane, and it always smelled faintly of hot fat, which in winter was almost a comfort. One window, with a crack across it stuffed with newspaper. One stove that ate coal faster than they could steal it. Two narrow beds against opposite walls with a sheet strung between them on a cord, which they never drew. A chair. A shelf with a cracked cup, a tin of tea, a comb, and a china dog that had belonged to their mother, with one ear.

Out of the window, Gin Lane went down toward the river in a slope of wet roofs. Every third lamp on it was dark. Alice had counted them once, when she was small, and it had been every third then too.

Miriam was dressing to go out.

Not in the oilskin. She'd washed her face. Taken her hair down out of the cap and pinned it up again, properly, with pins. Now she was putting on the blue wool dress. Alice had seen that dress twice. It lived in paper at the bottom of Miriam's box. It had cost more than the room.

Alice sat on her bed with her knees up and watched her do the buttons.

"Where are you going?"

"Out."

"In that?"

"It's a dress, Ali. People go out in them." Miriam did the last button and turned to look at herself in the black window, and for a moment she was somebody Alice didn't know at all. Then she pulled on the red mittens over the good dress, and she was Miriam again.

She reached into the pocket of the oilskin where it hung on the door, took something out, threw it underarm. Alice caught it without thinking.

An orange. A whole one, from a cart, the skin cold and pocked, and it smelled so strongly in the little room that for a second it covered the fat and the coal and everything else.

"Happy birthday," said Miriam. "Tomorrow you choose where. Anywhere you like. I mean it."

Alice held the orange in both hands. She'd had one before. Twice, maybe. She turned it over and found the little green star where the stalk had been.

"Miri... where are you going?"

Miriam sat down on the end of her own bed, across the gap, so their knees nearly touched. She took one mitten off again and rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, which she never did.

"I lost my temper today," she said. "In the warehouse. I'm sorry for it."

"You didn't. You didn't even raise your - "

"I lost it. You didn't see." She put the mitten back on. "You shouldn't have been in the rafters, Ali. Not today. Any day but today."

"I wanted to help."

"I know."

"I'm twenty. I'm not a - I've been on the corner since I was *eight*, Miri, and you've never once let me - "

"I know." Miriam looked at the floor. When she looked up her face was doing the thing again. The thing from the warehouse, when the pigeons went up. "There's something. I'm going to tell you. I'm going to tell you all of it, I swear, and you'll want to hit me, and you can. Just not tonight."

"Then when?"

"Tomorrow. When you choose where."

She stood up. Picked up the Lambrick purse and weighed it in her hand. Looked at Alice for what felt like a long time. Then she put two fingers under Alice's chin and tipped it up, as she'd done when Alice was small and had a splinter, or a nightmare, or a belt mark.

"Not tonight," she said again. Softer. "Wait for me."

"I'll wait."

"Promise."

"I *said*."

Miriam smiled, the chipped tooth showing. She opened the door. Cold came in from the stair with the smell of the chandler's fat. Then the door shut and her boots went away down four flights, *tok-tok-tok-tok*, and the street door banged.

Alice sat very still.

After a while she got up and put on her coat. It was Miriam's old one, too long in the sleeve. Buttoned it to the throat and stood at the door with her hand on the latch. Jennie would be out there. Jennie would be following. Alice could follow Jennie, and nobody would know, and she'd see for herself where her sister went in a blue dress at night with a purse full of somebody else's gold. It would be easy. It would be the easiest thing in the world.

*Wait for me.*

She stood there with her hand on the latch for as long as it took to count to eight. Then she counted it again.

Nobody had ever asked her to wait before. They had told her to. Miriam had *told* her to, a thousand times. Stay on the corner, stay under the bed, stay. That was different. This had been asking.

Alice took her hand off the latch. She took off the coat, one button at a time, and hung it back on its nail.

Then she sat on the edge of her bed and dug her thumbnail into the top of the orange. She did it slowly and very carefully, round and round, trying to get the whole skin off in one long curling strip, as their mother used to, and she nearly managed, and broke it only at the very end. The room filled up with the smell. She split the orange in two with her thumbs. The juice ran down her wrist into her sleeve and she let it.

She ate her half one piece at a time, sitting on her bed, looking at the door.

The other half she carried across the gap between the beds and put on Miriam's pillow, in the dip where her head would go, with the long curl of peel beside it so she'd know which of them had done it. Then she lay down on her own bed in her clothes, on her side, facing the door, and pulled the blanket up to her chin, and waited.
