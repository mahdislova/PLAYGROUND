# Chapter Two: Me. Always Me.

*Alice*

Alice was meant to be on the corner of Lime Wharf Street, watching for constables. She was forty feet up in the rafters of the Lambrick warehouse instead, with pigeon muck on both knees and her heart going hard enough to hear, and it was the best she'd felt in years.

Nobody had seen her come in. That was the beauty of it. She'd gone up the hoist chain on the loading door while Miriam and Jennie were still crossing the roof, then along the tie-beam on her belly, one arm hooked over, the old wood cold and furred with dust under her cheek. Now she lay flat above the counting-house end of the floor and breathed through her nose. Rain came down through a broken pane in the skylight and went *tik, tik, tik* on a tarpaulin. Somewhere in the dark a pigeon was making its stupid noise. *Hrrooo.* Shut up, you. Some of us are working.

Twelve years she'd done the corner. Twelve years of standing in doorways with her hands in her armpits, whistling twice for a constable and once for a dog, while the real work happened somewhere she wasn't allowed to see. She was twenty today. Twenty was too old for corners. She'd decided that about four o'clock this morning, lying awake, and once Alice Bounds decided a thing it stayed decided, which Miriam called stubborn and Alice called character.

The warehouse was long and low and smelled of tar and river. Crates stood in rows down the middle, stencilled for Rotterdam, each sealed with a blob of red wax and the Lambrick ram pressed into it. Somebody had prised the lid half off the nearest, and inside, nested in straw, lay bars of core-cut gallowglass the length of her forearm, greenish even in that light. The Works crest had been chiselled off the end of every one. Carefully. Somebody had been paid to be careful, and somebody else had paid them, and none of it was Alice's business, which was exactly the kind of thing she'd been told her whole life.

Four men below. A clerk at a high desk with a lamp, a big one sitting on a crate eating a pie, and two by the door, smoking. Not one of them looked up. Men never looked up. Miriam had told her that when she was six, and it was the first useful thing anybody ever taught her, and the second was how to pick a lock with a hairpin, and the third was never tell Mam about the first two.

On the skylight a shadow moved. Then a second, smaller. Miriam crouched on the glass with Jennie beside her holding the rope, and Miriam's lips were going. Counting. She always counted before she dropped. Alice had asked her once what she was counting to. To when I go, Miriam had said, which wasn't an answer, which was the point.

Miriam was twenty-four and the most dangerous person Alice had ever met, and she didn't look it, which was the trick. Tall and thin, black hair pinned flat under a man's cap, a face all cheekbone and freckle, a chip off one front tooth from a fight she'd won and refused to describe. A man's oilskin cut down to fit. Trousers tucked into soft-soled boots she'd lifted off a ballet master in Covent Garden. And the red mittens with the fingertips cut out, which she'd worn every winter Alice could remember. Even on jobs. Especially on jobs. People remembered the mittens and forgot the face.

Jennie was smaller, nineteen, square as a brick, with a white scar through her left eyebrow that made her look permanently surprised by something she'd decided not to mention. Laundry hands, red to the wrist. She didn't talk on jobs. She didn't talk much off them either, but she'd once carried Alice two miles on her back with a turned ankle and then never brought it up, not once, not even when Alice was being unbearable, which was a kind of talking.

Neither of them knew Alice was here. She was grinning into the dust like an idiot. Well. Twenty today. Idiots got the day off on their birthday.

Miriam stopped counting. She took the rope from Jennie, wrapped it once round her forearm, lifted the broken pane out of its frame with two fingers, and dropped.

Oh, it was beautiful. One moment the clerk at his desk was dipping his pen; the next Miriam was standing on the desk in front of him with rain coming down around her and his pen in her hand instead of his.

"Evening," said Miriam.

The pie man stood up. The two by the door dropped their pipes and came, and Miriam stepped down off the desk to meet them as if somebody had asked her to dance and she was considering it. The first went down holding his knee. The second got a fistful of oilskin and then didn't have it any more and folded over a crate with a noise like *hoff*. Two down. Easy. Alice shifted on the beam for a better view, because when was she going to see it this close again -

The beam was old. Her boot found the one rotten place in forty feet of it, and a lump of something soft broke away and went down, and with it, from the dark on her left, went every pigeon in the roof at once. *Brrrap-brap-brap-brap.* Wings. Feathers. Muck. The pie man looked up. The clerk looked up.

Miriam looked up. Straight at her.

And Alice saw her sister's face do a thing it had never done in twenty years. It wasn't anger. Anger she could have stood. It was the face of somebody watching a cup go off the edge of a table, too far away to catch it.

The clerk had a knife. He came off his stool with it while Miriam was still looking at the rafters, the blade low out of his coat, and Alice saw the whole of it and opened her mouth and nothing came out. Nothing. Twenty years old and too old for corners, and she lay on a beam with her mouth open like a landed fish.

Miriam didn't turn. Didn't need to. Put out one hand behind her without looking, caught him by the wrist, turned her hip into him, threw him. One hand. Over the hip and down, and the clerk went into the open crate back-first and lay in the straw among the gallowglass bars, staring at the skylight with an expression of mild and total surprise.

The one-handed throw. Alice had watched it all her life. Practised it on a flour sack, on the bedpost, on Jennie once, which Jennie hadn't liked. Never once made it work.

The pie man sat back down on his crate and put his hands on his knees. Not a fool, the pie man. Alice could have kissed him.

"You can come down now," said Miriam to the rafters, without raising her voice.

Alice came down. It took a long time and nobody helped. Fair enough. She wouldn't have helped her either. By the time her boots touched the floor Miriam was at the iron safe under the desk with the clerk's own keys and did not look round.

"I was keeping watch," said Alice.

"I can see that."

"From up there you can see all four of - "

"Bag."

Alice held the bag. Into it went a fat leather purse that chinked, and after it a ledger bound in brown calf with the ram stamped on the cover in gold. Miriam flicked through the ledger once before she put it in, frowning, and shut it hard. Jennie had come down the rope and stood by the door with the smokers' pipes in her hand, one of them still lit. She drew on it thoughtfully, looked at Alice, and raised the eyebrow with the scar through it about a sixteenth of an inch.

Which from Jennie was a speech. Alice glared at her. Jennie blew smoke at the ceiling.

"Window," said Miriam.

The window at the river end was small and high, opening onto a ledge above the water, and the drop to the barge below was a good deal further than Alice cared for. She stood under it looking up, and her voice came out smaller than she'd meant.

"Who goes out first? If they've got someone on the wharf. Who goes first?"

Miriam was already on the sill with the bag over her shoulder and the rain behind her. She looked down at Alice for a moment, and there was nothing in her face at all now, nothing, the cup gone off the table and swept up.

"Me," she said. "Always me."

And she was gone.

* * *

The Shadwell laundry stood on the corner of Cable Street and Dock Lane, three storeys of brown brick weeping steam from every window, and inside it was always August. Coppers boiled along the back wall, each one with a girl over it working a dolly-stick, *thunk-slosh, thunk-slosh*, and the mangles groaned on their cranks, and the air was so thick with lye and starch that Alice could taste it on her teeth. Sheets hung from the ceiling in long white aisles. You walked between them half blind, and at the far end, at a table under the only window that didn't steam, sat Mother Grise.

Seventy, or said she was. Small and wide. She sat very upright in a carved chair that had come out of a church, in black bombazine gone green at the seams, with a cap of white lace starched so stiff it could have stood up on the table by itself. Her face was soft and pink and creased like a baked apple. One eye was milky. You never knew which was looking at you, and Alice had long suspected that was deliberate. Under the table she wore a man's carpet slippers. Her hands were the colour of boiled ham, swollen across every knuckle from fifty years in hot water, and there wasn't a ring on them. Grise didn't need to show what she had.

Miriam put the ledger on the table and kept the bag on her shoulder.

Grise didn't touch the ledger at first. She looked at it. Then she drew it toward her with one finger and began turning pages, licking her thumb, slow. Halfway through she stopped. Read something twice. Smiled.

It was a nice smile. Alice hated it on sight. Nice smiles, in Shadwell, were how you found out what things cost.

"Well," said Grise. "Isn't Hector a clever boy. Isn't he a worried clever boy." Another page. "Was there a purse, Miriam?"

"Was there one, Mother?"

"I'm asking, dear. I'm only... asking."

"There was a ledger," said Miriam. "There it is."

Grise looked at her with the good eye for a long, long while. Then she laughed, *heh*, a small dry sound, and set her hand flat on the ledger.

"Keep it, then," she said. "Buy yourself something warm."

Alice let out a breath she hadn't meant to hold. She was standing a little behind Miriam's shoulder, where she always stood, out of the light. But Grise's head turned, and the milky eye came round and rested on her. It went over her slowly, boots up. Over the coat that had been Miriam's, over the hands, the hair. Alice had once watched Grise go over a bolt of good Irish linen some girl had lifted from a draper's, rubbing it between finger and thumb, feeling for the weave and pricing it.

That look. Exactly that one.

"Your sister's quick," said Grise. "I saw her come in the door just now. Not a sound."

Miriam moved. Not much. One step sideways, so she was between them.

"Keep your hands off her."

Every mangle in the room seemed to go on very loudly.

"I haven't touched her, dear," said Grise mildly. "I only looked."

"Don't look, either."

Out in the street Miriam walked fast with her head down and didn't speak, and Alice had to half run to keep up. Something had happened back there and she wasn't being told what, which made a change from never, she supposed. They were nearly to the corner of Dock Lane before she remembered her cap. She'd taken it off in the heat and left it on a mangle. It was the only cap she had.

"I've left my - "

"Leave it."

"It's my *cap*, Miri. It's the only - "

Miriam made a noise through her teeth and stopped under a lamp, one of the dark ones, and folded her arms. "Go on, then. Quick. Quicker than that."

Back through the side door the girls used. The heat closed over her. She found the cap on the end of the third mangle, damp now and stinking of starch, and as she turned to go, there at the end of the long aisle of sheets was the table by the window.

Grise was holding a letter. It was folded small and sealed in plain black wax, no crest, nothing pressed into it at all, and Grise broke the seal with her thumbnail and read it, then read it again. Lifted her head and called down the room, not loudly.

"Jennie, love."

Jennie came out from behind a copper, drying her red hands on her apron.

Grise said something Alice didn't catch. Then two words, a little louder, as Jennie was already turning away.

"Follow Miriam."

Jennie nodded and took her shawl off its peg. Alice ducked behind a sheet without knowing why she ducked, and went out by the side door with her cap pressed to her chest.

Well, and so what? Grise kept an eye on everyone. That was how Grise stayed Grise. And it was Jennie. Jennie with the eyebrow and the two-mile piggyback. If anybody was going to be following Miriam about tonight, Alice was glad it was her. Somebody ought to be watching that idiot's back. Somebody other than Alice, apparently.

She jammed the cap on her head. Miriam was waiting under the dark lamp with her arms folded, and they walked home.

* * *

The Bounds room was four flights up over a tallow chandler's on Gin Lane and always smelled faintly of hot fat, which in winter was nearly a comfort. One window, with a crack across it stuffed with newspaper. One stove that ate coal faster than they could steal it. Two narrow beds against opposite walls with a sheet strung between them on a cord, which they never drew. A chair. A shelf with a cracked cup, a tin of tea, a comb, and a china dog that had been their mother's, with one ear. Alice had knocked the other off at four. Miriam had told Mam she did it.

Home, then. Such as it was. Out of the window Gin Lane went down toward the river in a slope of wet roofs, and every third lamp was dark. Alice had counted them once, when she was small. Every third then, too. Some things in London you could set your watch by, if you had a watch.

Miriam was dressing to go out.

Not in the oilskin. She'd washed her face and taken her hair down out of the cap and pinned it up again properly, with pins, and now she was putting on the blue wool dress. Alice had seen that dress twice. It lived in paper at the bottom of Miriam's box. It had cost more than the room.

Alice sat on her bed with her knees up and watched her do the buttons and said nothing, because she'd decided on the stairs to say nothing, and then lasted almost a minute.

"Where are you going?"

"Out."

"In that?"

"It's a dress, Ali. People go out in them. You'd know if you ever wore one." Miriam did the last button and turned to look at herself in the black window, and for a moment there was somebody standing in their room that Alice didn't know at all. Somebody younger. Then Miriam pulled on the red mittens over the good dress and she was Miriam again.

She took something out of the oilskin's pocket and threw it underarm. Alice caught it without thinking.

An orange. A whole one, from a cart, the skin cold and pocked, and it smelled so strong in that little room that for a second it covered the fat and the coal and everything else, and Alice was six and somebody's hand was on her hair.

"Happy birthday," said Miriam. "Tomorrow you choose where. Anywhere you like. I mean it."

Don't cry, you great baby. It's fruit. Alice held it in both hands. She'd had one before. Twice, maybe. She turned it over and found the little green star where the stalk had been, and her throat did something she didn't care for.

"Paris."

"Within *reason*, Ali."

"Margate, then."

Miriam laughed. A real one, surprised out of her, the chipped tooth showing. "Margate. God help me. All right. Margate."

"Miri... where are you going?"

Miriam sat down on the end of her own bed, across the gap, so their knees nearly touched. She took one mitten off and rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, which she never did, not even at the end of a job, not even after the night at the Limehouse stairs that nobody talked about.

"I lost my temper today," she said. "In the warehouse. I'm sorry for it."

"You didn't. You didn't even raise your - "

"I lost it. You didn't see." She put the mitten back on. "You shouldn't have been in the rafters, Ali. Not today. Any day but today."

"I wanted to help."

"I know."

"I'm twenty. I'm not a - I've been on that corner since I was *eight*, Miri, and you've never once let me - "

"I know." Miriam looked at the floor. When she looked up her face was doing the thing again, the warehouse thing, the cup going over. "There's something. I'm going to tell you. All of it, I swear, and you'll want to hit me, and you can. You can black both my eyes. Just not tonight."

"Then when?"

"Tomorrow. In Margate, if you like."

She stood. Picked up the Lambrick purse and weighed it in her hand. Then she put two fingers under Alice's chin and tipped it up, the way she'd done when Alice was small and had a splinter, or a nightmare, or a belt mark. The old way. Alice let her.

"Not tonight," Miriam said again, softer. "Wait for me."

"I'll wait."

"Promise."

"I *said*, didn't I?"

The door opened and cold came in from the stair with the smell of the chandler's fat, and then the door shut and Miriam's boots went away down four flights, *tok-tok-tok-tok*, and the street door banged.

Alice sat very still.

After a while she got up and put on her coat. It was Miriam's old one, too long in the sleeve; she buttoned it to the throat and stood at the door with her hand on the latch. Jennie would be out there. Jennie would be following. Alice could follow Jennie, and nobody would ever know, and she'd see for herself where her sister went at night in a blue dress with a purse full of somebody else's gold. Easy. Easiest thing in the... well. Easy. She'd been following people for twelve years. She was good at it. It was the one thing she was good at.

Wait for me.

She stood with her hand on the latch for as long as it took to count to eight. Then she counted it again, because eight was a number they didn't talk about either, and her hand wasn't moving.

Nobody had ever asked her to wait before. They'd told her to. Miriam had told her to a thousand times, stay on the corner, stay under the bed, stay. That was different. Tonight Miriam had asked. Miriam Bounds, who didn't ask anybody for anything, had sat on the end of the bed in a dress that cost more than the room and asked.

So that was that, then.

She took her hand off the latch. Took the coat off, one button at a time, and hung it back on its nail, and felt about nine years old and also, oddly, about a hundred.

Then she sat on the edge of her bed and dug her thumbnail into the top of the orange.

She did it slowly and very carefully, round and round, trying to get the whole skin off in one long curling strip, as their mother used to, and she nearly managed and broke it only at the very end. Typical. The room filled up with the smell. She split the orange in two with her thumbs, and the juice ran down her wrist into her sleeve and she let it.

She ate her half one piece at a time, sitting on her bed, looking at the door.

The other half she carried across the gap and set on Miriam's pillow, in the dip where her head would go, with the long curl of peel beside it so she'd know who'd done it and how close she'd come. Then she lay down on her own bed in her clothes, on her side, facing the door, and pulled the blanket up to her chin.

Margate. She'd have to find out where Margate was.

She was still working on it when she fell asleep.
