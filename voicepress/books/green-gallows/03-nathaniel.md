# Chapter Three: The Exhibition

*Nathaniel*

The great hall of the Works had been built by his great-grandfather to make men feel small, and at nine o'clock on Exhibition night it was doing its job.

Two hundred people stood under a ceiling Nathaniel had never seen the top of. The roof was iron and glass, braced with girders painted the dark green of a railway engine, and from every girder hung a chandelier of the old pattern, forty of them in four long rows, each one turned up as high as the feed would take it. The light came down green-white and absolute. It found every diamond. It found every pore. Under it the guests looked lit from within, polished, a little unwell, and they drifted between the long tables of exhibits with glasses in their hands, talking in the low expensive murmur of people who had paid to be seen paying.

Under the floor, under the music and the talk, the Works hummed. You stopped hearing it after a minute. Nathaniel never had.

He stood at the side of the dais with the gauntlet in its case at his feet and his new collar sawing at his neck, and tried to remember the order of his demonstration. Introduce the problem. Introduce the coil. Fire the armature at the target, a stack of iron plates bolted to a frame at the far end of the floor. Watch the professor's face. He had rewound the outer turns that afternoon and reset the angle by half a degree and tested it three times in the coach house, and three times it had held.

He didn't know why it held. He'd stopped asking, round about the second test.

"Nathaniel." His mother's hand on his arm. "Here's Miss Lambrick."

Josephine Harding was the hostess tonight and she was wearing it like armour: dark grey silk, jet at the throat, her gloves on, a smile she could keep up for six hours and had. She steered a young woman toward him across the last few feet of floor and stepped neatly out of the way, as if delivering a parcel.

Tamara Lambrick was nineteen, and taller than he remembered, nearly his height. She had black hair worn in a single severe coil at the back of her head, no curls, no ornaments, and a long pale face with a straight nose and a mouth that looked as though it did a good deal of thinking before it did anything else. Her dress was dark green silk, three years out of fashion and plainly chosen on purpose. She carried a pair of gold spectacles folded in her left hand and didn't put them on. There was an ink stain on the second finger of her right hand that someone had scrubbed at and not quite beaten.

"Mr Harding."

"Miss Lambrick." He bowed, a little late. She curtsied, a little later, as if to make him feel better about it.

"We met at the Guildhall."

"I remember. You asked about the condensers."

"You told me about them for twenty minutes."

"Did I?"

"It was very thorough," said Tamara. "I've used it since, to get to sleep."

He looked at her. She looked back, entirely straight-faced. Behind them the orchestra finished one waltz and began another that sounded exactly like it.

"I gather we're to be married," he said.

"So I'm told. My father wrote it down." She turned the spectacles over in her fingers. "I've been thinking about it. I believe we could be very polite to each other for forty years. People manage it. My aunt managed it for thirty-two and only threw one plate."

"That seems a lot to ask of us."

"The forty years or the plate?"

"I was thinking of the politeness."

That was when she almost smiled. It came and went at one corner of her mouth before she could stop it, and it was the first thing all evening that Nathaniel had wanted to look at twice.

She nodded down at the case by his feet. "Is that the coil?"

"The gauntlet. The coil's inside."

"How does it work?"

People asked that. They meant *say something clever so I can nod*, and he'd got an answer ready for them. He opened his mouth to give it, and found that she'd put the spectacles on. She was looking at him through them, properly, waiting, as though she actually wanted to know.

"I don't... it's a spiral," he said. "Each turn sits at an angle to the last. It holds heat that should bleed. I can show you that it does. I can't show you why."

"You don't know?"

"I drew it at three in the morning. I've been trying to work backwards from it for six weeks."

Tamara looked at the case for a while. Then she took the spectacles off again and folded them.

"That's how I do it, too," she said. "With ciphers. You wake up and it's on the page, and then you spend a month learning how you knew." She paused. "Nobody believes me either."

Someone was tapping a glass. Up on the dais his father had risen, and the room went quiet in a long ripple from the front, and Nathaniel's mother was beside them again with her hand on Tamara's elbow, taking her away.

* * *

Jamie gave the speech.

He was good at it. Nathaniel had forgotten how good. His brother stood at the front of the dais in black and white with one hand in his pocket and the other holding a glass he never drank from, and he made two hundred people laugh in the first minute, and then he made them quiet. He talked about their great-great-grandfather, Alistair, the failed inventor who'd walked into a wood one morning in 1780 and walked out with the future. He talked about the lamps of London, and the men who kept them burning, and the hall clapped in all the right places. Nathaniel watched his father watching Jamie. Eldgard's face showed nothing at all, which on Eldgard was approval.

And Jamie smiled. All the way through. He smiled at the Lambricks, at the aldermen, at the man from the *Times*, and at their mother, and he never once looked at the chandeliers.

When it was over he stepped down off the side of the dais into the crowd. People reached for his hand. He shook some of them and slid past the rest, and Nathaniel lost sight of him somewhere near the back of the hall, by the doors.

"Mr Nathaniel Harding," said someone. "And Professor Halloran, of Edinburgh."

Professor Halloran was seventy if he was a day, long and stooped, in a frock coat that had been black once and was now a sort of tired green, with white hair standing straight up off his head and very bright blue eyes, and his hands, when he shook Nathaniel's, were dry and hard, freckled with tiny burn scars. He'd written the book on gallowglass resonance. Literally. Nathaniel had a copy under his bed, its spine broken in four places.

"Well. Show me, then," said Halloran. "I came a long way, and the trains... never mind the trains. Show me."

Nathaniel showed him. He went through the problem and the coil. Heard his own voice going in the big room, a little too fast, and made himself slow down. Opened the case and strapped the gauntlet on, and the leather was cold. The armature sat along his forearm with the coil housed at the wrist, and he could feel it, faintly, before he'd even turned it over, a warmth that wasn't his own.

At the far end of the floor the iron plates stood waiting on their frame. The crowd had pulled back to make a lane. Two hundred faces, all lit green.

He turned the key.

The coil spun up. He heard it climb, the clean high whine, the right note, exactly the note, and he felt it in his teeth and his wrist, and for one second he thought, *There.* Then the note went sour.

It coughed, there was no other word for it. *Pfff-tuk.* Something deep in the housing caught and let go and caught again, *tuk, tuk-tuk*, and the gauntlet bucked on his arm and pulled him forward. He went with it. Two steps, four, half running, half falling across the polished floor toward the plates with his arm out in front of him, a man being taken for a walk by his own invention.

He stopped about halfway. The coil died with a little sigh. A wisp of smoke came out of the wrist housing and went straight up in the still air.

There was a pause.

Then somebody laughed near the front, one man, and then all of them.

Two hundred people. Most of it wasn't cruel. You'd give the same laugh to a dog that ran into a door. It went round the hall and up into the girders, and came back down. It went on for what seemed a very long time. Nathaniel stood in the middle of the floor with his arm still out, smoke coming off his wrist, and could not, for the life of him, think what to do with his face.

He looked for his father. Eldgard was on the dais, talking to the alderman beside him. He wasn't looking at the floor. He wasn't looking anywhere near it.

Looked at Halloran, who had taken out a notebook and was writing something in it.

And he looked, he didn't know why, for Tamara. She was standing by a pillar a little way off with her spectacles on, and she wasn't laughing. Not even politely. She was looking at the gauntlet on his arm, at the smoke, frowning, the same frown she'd turned on the case. As though it was a page she'd almost read.

Nathaniel unbuckled the gauntlet. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Put it back in its case. Closed the lid. Then he walked off the floor through the lane they'd made for him, and it closed up again behind him.

By the side doors Byron stood with a tray. An old lady in purple, one of the Pembertons, had him by the sleeve.

"But where *is* Josephine? I've been looking for her everywhere. She was to introduce me to the professor."

"Her ladyship has gone home, madam," said Byron. "A headache."

"A *headache*? Josephine? I've known her thirty - well. Goodness."

"Very sudden, madam. She asked me to make her apologies."

Nathaniel stopped. Byron's eyes came round to him over the old lady's head and rested there a moment, and his face was the face it always was, smooth and closed.

His mother had never had a headache in her life. Nathaniel had once seen her host a dinner for thirty with a broken wrist.

Byron looked away first.

* * *

The roof of the Works was flat lead ringed by a stone parapet, and nobody went up there, which was why Nathaniel did.

He sat on the parapet with the case beside him and his legs hanging over sixty feet of nothing. It was raining a little, a fine cold drizzle he could hardly feel. Below him London went away in every direction, and it was green. The lamps of Mayfair and St James's burned in long clean emerald strings, square after square, every one of them fed from the hum under his feet. To the east it was patchier. In the East End, past the Tower, the lights went into a sort of broken pattern, lit, dark, lit, and he knew without counting that it would be every third one out. Nobody had ever told him why. He'd never asked.

He took the gauntlet out of its case and sat with it in his lap. The coil had stopped smoking. It looked exactly as it had that morning. Beautiful, and right. His, and not.

Heard the roof door behind him, *creak* and *clunk*. Didn't turn round.

Jamie came and sat on the parapet beside him, close, not quite touching. Didn't say anything. It was the best thing he could have done. They sat there. The drizzle came and went. Down in the hall, the orchestra had started again, faint as something heard through water.

After a while Jamie held out his hand. Nathaniel gave him the gauntlet.

His brother turned it over in the light from the roof-lamp. Looked at it a long time. The housing. The scorch mark where the smoke had come out. He touched the casing over the coil with one finger, carefully. Nathaniel had never seen him look at anything Nathaniel had made with that much attention. He'd never seen him look at anything with that much attention.

"It nearly worked," said Jamie.

"It worked for one second."

"I saw the second." Jamie handed it back. He put his hand on the back of Nathaniel's neck, briefly. He'd done that when they were small and Nathaniel fell out of the walnut tree. Then he took it away. "Nat. Listen. Never lose the light within you."

Nathaniel looked at him. "That's... well, that's on the sampler," he said. "In the nursery. Above the washstand."

"I know."

"Mother stitched it. When she was a girl."

"I know where it's from, Nat." Jamie was smiling, but only with his mouth. "I'm saying it anyway. I'm saying it to *you*. Would you just - for once in your life, would you just let somebody say something to you?"

Nathaniel didn't know what to say to that, so he didn't say anything. Jamie squeezed his shoulder. Two fingers and a thumb. Their old signal from the receiving lines. *Do you want out?*

This time Nathaniel didn't cover his hand. He was holding the gauntlet. By the time he thought to, Jamie had already stood up.

"Where are you going?"

"Oh... home," said Jamie. "I don't know. Home, I suppose." He seemed to hear himself. "Home." "I've got something to do."

"Now? Father's going to want you for the - "

"Father's going to want me for a great many things." Jamie buttoned his coat. He looked down at the lit squares of Mayfair for a moment, and east, at the broken ones. "Stay up here a bit. It's quieter. Go down when you're ready, not before."

The roof door went *creak*, and *clunk*, and he was gone.

* * *

He did stay. He stayed till his legs were cold and the drizzle had soaked through the shoulders of his coat, and then he went down, because there was nowhere else to go.

The hall had thinned. It was nearly midnight. Perhaps a hundred and fifty people left, louder now, drunker, the orchestra playing something with a polka in it. Nobody looked at him as he came in. That was almost worse. He found a pillar and stood against it with the case at his feet, and he was watching the floor, not thinking about anything, when the light went.

Not out. Down.

Every chandelier in the hall sank at once, all forty, the green-white draining out of them into brown and then into a sullen orange, an ember glow. The hum under the floor dropped a full octave in a second, *vvvvoooommm*, and he felt it in the soles of his feet and his back teeth. The orchestra stopped. A woman shrieked, and then laughed, embarrassed. People were looking up. For once everybody was looking up.

And under the hum, under the floor, very far down, somebody was crying.

A woman. He heard it quite clearly, not shrieking, not calling out. Just crying, a long hopeless sound from someone who'd been at it for years and had stopped expecting anyone to come. It came up through the floorboards and the soles of his shoes. It went on. Nobody else in the hall turned their head.

Nathaniel looked round wildly. A man near him was relighting a cigar. Two girls were giggling in the half dark. Tamara was by the far wall, looking up at the chandeliers through her spectacles with her lips moving. Counting something.

"Do you hear that?" he said, to nobody, too loudly.

The man with the cigar looked at him and edged away.

On the dais, a boy in Works livery had come running in from the side door, soaked through, without his cap. He went straight to Eldgard and said something into his ear. Nathaniel saw his father's face change, not much. It went perfectly still. Then Eldgard set down his glass on the table beside him, very precisely, and walked off the dais and out through the side door with the boy at his heels, and didn't say a word to anyone.

The chandeliers came back up, slowly, to green-white, and the hum climbed back to its note. Somebody started clapping. Others joined in, glad to have been part of something.

The crying had stopped. Or he couldn't hear it any more. He stood against his pillar with his heart going hard and couldn't tell which.

* * *

There were four constables in the front hall at Harding House.

Nathaniel stood in the doorway with the rain behind him and the gauntlet case in his hand and looked at them. They looked back at him. One of them was writing in a notebook. Another stood at the foot of the main stair with his helmet off, turning it round in his hands. He was very young. His face was the colour of the plaster.

Every lamp in the hall was lit. The house was full of light and nobody was speaking.

Nathaniel put the case down. Put his bare hand on the newel post, as he always did coming in, without thinking. Took it away again fast.

The banister was ice. Not cold: ice. A white fur of frost ran up the polished mahogany as far as he could see, thickening as it went, up toward the first landing, and when he took his palm off it the skin stung and some of it stayed behind.

From the back of the hall, from the door to the servants' stair, came a small noise. Ada was standing there. She'd been doing the landing lamps; she still had her rags over one arm and her taper in the other hand, unlit. She was a small round woman of sixty who'd polished the lamps in this house since before Nathaniel was born, and he had never once seen her without her cap on straight. It was crooked now.

"Master Nat," she said. "He come up the back stair, Master Jamie did. He had a spanner. One of the big ones from the Works. I said, I said why aren't you at the... and he said go to bed, Ada. Ever so kind about it. Go to bed." She looked at the rags in her hand. "I only went down the one flight."

Someone was coming down the main stair. Byron, in his coat still, wet at the shoulders from the carriage. He came down slowly, holding the frosted rail with a gloved hand. Stopped three steps from the bottom, so that his face was level with Nathaniel's, and put himself in the way.

"Sir," said Byron. "Don't go up."

Nathaniel went up.

He went past Byron, who didn't stop him and didn't try very hard to, and up the stair two at a time with the frost crackling under his palm on the rail, *crk, crk, crk*. On the landing it was on the walls too, in long feathered sheets across the wallpaper. The landing lamps were lit and their glass had frosted over, and they gave a strange furred light, white and green. His breath smoked. The carpet crunched.

The study door was open. Not three inches, either. All the way.

Cold came out of it. Real cold, January cold, straight through the cloth of his coat. The window on the far side of the room was gone. Not open. Gone, the frame and all, burst outward over the street, and the rain was coming in through the hole and turning to sleet as it crossed the sill. Papers lay frozen to the carpet. The decanter on the side table had split down its length and the brandy inside it had frozen solid in the shape of the glass.

On the desk, the brass cage that had held the ornament all his life was broken open, its bars bent outward, empty. There were shards of green glass across the blotter. They were still faintly glowing. As he watched, one of them went out.

Jamie was on the rug in front of the desk.

He was on his side with one arm flung out. The big Works spanner lay beside his hand. His shirt front had gone dark and his face had gone white, and frost had grown up out of his collar, over his jaw and his throat, in fine branching lines like the veins of a leaf. Something cold had gone in through him and out the other side, and left the shape of itself behind.

His chest moved. Just, up and down.

Nathaniel heard someone make a sound. He thought it was probably him.

He crossed the room. The frost on the rug cracked under his knees as he went down beside his brother, and he put his hands on him without knowing where to put them, on his shoulder, his face, the wet dark front of his shirt. It was warm. It was the only warm thing in the room. He pressed down hard on it with both palms as if he could hold it all in.

Jamie's eyes opened and found him, and Nathaniel saw his brother know him.

"Nat," said Jamie. His lips barely moved. "I let it - "

"Don't talk. Don't. Byron's sending for - Byron! *Byron!*"

Jamie's hand came up off the rug, slowly, and found Nathaniel's shoulder. Two fingers and a thumb. It didn't have the strength to squeeze.

Nathaniel covered it with his own and held it there, hard. Bent over his brother on the frozen rug. Shouted for help until his voice went.
