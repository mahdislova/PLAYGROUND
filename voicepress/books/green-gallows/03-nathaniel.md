# Chapter Three: The Exhibition

*Nathaniel*

The great hall of the Works had been built by his great-grandfather to make men feel small, and at nine o'clock on Exhibition night it was doing its job beautifully.

Two hundred people stood under a ceiling Nathaniel had never seen the top of. The roof was iron and glass, braced with girders painted the dark green of a railway engine, and from every girder hung a chandelier of the old pattern, forty of them in four long rows, each turned as high as the feed would bear. The light came down green-white and total. It found every diamond. It found every pore. Under it the guests looked lit from inside, polished and a little unwell, and they drifted between the long tables of exhibits with glasses in their hands, talking in the low expensive murmur of people who had paid to be seen paying.

Under the floor, under the music and the talk, the Works hummed. Most people stopped hearing it after a minute. Nathaniel never had. It was the first sound he could remember, and some nights in the coach house he lay awake listening for it the way other people listened for rain.

He stood at the side of the dais with the gauntlet's case at his feet and his new collar sawing at his neck, running through the demonstration. Introduce the problem. Introduce the coil. Fire the armature at the target, a stack of iron plates bolted to a frame at the far end of the floor. Watch the professor's face. He'd rewound the outer turns that afternoon, reset the angle by half a degree, and tested it three times in the coach house, and three times it had held.

Why it held, he couldn't have said. He'd stopped asking around the second test. It seemed rude, somehow, like asking a horse how it knew the way home.

"Nathaniel." His mother's hand on his arm. "Here's Miss Lambrick."

Josephine Harding was hostess tonight and wore it like armour: dark grey silk, jet at the throat, gloves on, a smile she could hold for six hours and frequently had. She steered a young woman toward him across the last few feet of floor and stepped neatly out of the way, as though delivering a parcel she'd been asked to sign for.

Tamara Lambrick was nineteen and taller than he remembered, nearly his height. Black hair worn in a single severe coil at the back of her head. No curls, no ornaments. A long pale face with a straight nose and a mouth that looked as if it did a good deal of thinking before it did anything else. Her dress was dark green silk three years out of fashion and so plainly chosen on purpose that Nathaniel, who knew nothing about dresses, felt it as a sort of manifesto. She carried gold spectacles folded in her left hand and didn't put them on. On the second finger of her right hand was an ink stain someone had scrubbed at and not quite beaten.

"Mr Harding."

"Miss Lambrick." He bowed, a little late. She curtsied, a little later, as if to make him feel better about it.

"We met at the Guildhall."

"I remember. You asked about the condensers."

"You told me about them for twenty minutes."

Oh, God. "Did I?"

"It was very thorough," said Tamara. "I've used it since, to get to sleep."

He looked at her. She looked back, face entirely straight. Behind them the orchestra finished one waltz and began another that sounded exactly like it, and somewhere in Nathaniel's chest something that had been clenched since luncheon let go about a quarter of an inch.

"I gather we're to be married," he said.

"So I'm told. My father wrote it down." She turned the spectacles over in her fingers. "I've been thinking about it. I believe we could be very polite to each other for forty years. People manage it. My aunt managed thirty-two and only threw one plate."

"That seems a great deal to ask of us."

"The forty years or the plate?"

"The politeness. I've never managed it past the soup."

There. The corner of her mouth went, just once, before she caught it. It was the first thing all evening Nathaniel had wanted to look at twice, and he found, to his alarm, that he'd have quite liked to make it happen again.

She nodded at the case by his feet. "Is that the coil?"

"The gauntlet. The coil's inside."

"How does it work?"

People asked that. They meant say something clever so I can nod, and he kept an answer ready for them, smooth as a coin. He opened his mouth to hand it over and found that she'd put the spectacles on. She was looking at him through them, properly, waiting. As though she actually wanted to know.

Which was unfair. Nobody had warned him she'd do that.

"I don't... it's a spiral," he said. "Each turn sits at an angle to the last. It holds heat that ought to bleed. I can show you that it does. I can't show you why."

"You don't know?"

"I drew it at three in the morning. I've spent six weeks trying to work backwards from it and I get as far as the third turn."

Tamara looked at the case for a while. Then she took the spectacles off and folded them.

"That's how I do it too," she said. "With ciphers. You wake up and it's on the page, and then you spend a month learning how you knew." A pause, very slight. "Nobody believes me either."

He'd have answered. He had something nearly ready, something that wasn't about condensers. But a glass was being tapped up on the dais, and the room was going quiet in a long ripple from the front, and his mother was back at Tamara's elbow, taking her away like a cup she'd lent.

* * *

Jamie gave the speech.

He was good at it. Nathaniel had forgotten how good. His brother stood at the front of the dais in black and white with one hand in his pocket and the other holding a glass he never drank from, and he had two hundred people laughing inside a minute and silent inside three. He talked about their great-great-grandfather Alistair, the failed inventor who'd walked into a wood one morning in 1780 and walked out with the future. He talked about the lamps of London and the men who kept them lit. The hall clapped in all the right places. Nathaniel watched his father watching Jamie, and Eldgard's face showed nothing at all, which on Eldgard was approval.

And Jamie smiled. All the way through. He smiled at the Lambricks and the aldermen and the man from the *Times*, and at their mother, and he never once looked up at the chandeliers. Nathaniel noticed that because he'd spent his whole life looking at chandeliers, and he'd never known Jamie not to glance up when a room was this bright. It was a thing you did. Like checking for your watch.

When it was over Jamie stepped down off the side of the dais into the crowd. People reached for his hand. He shook some and slid past the rest, and somewhere near the doors at the back Nathaniel lost him.

"Mr Nathaniel Harding," said someone. "And Professor Halloran, of Edinburgh."

Professor Halloran was seventy if he was a day, long and stooped, in a frock coat that had been black once and had settled into a tired green. White hair stood straight up off his head as if it had been surprised there. His eyes were very bright and very blue, and his hands, when he shook Nathaniel's, were dry and hard and freckled with tiny burn scars. He had written the book on gallowglass resonance. Literally. Nathaniel's copy was under his bed with its spine broken in four places.

"Well. Show me, then," said Halloran. "I came a long way, and the trains... never mind the trains. Show me."

So he showed him. He went through the problem and the coil, and heard his own voice going in that enormous room, too fast, and made himself slow down. Opened the case. Strapped the gauntlet on. The leather was cold, and the armature lay along his forearm with the coil housed at the wrist, and it was there before he'd even turned the key, faint: a warmth that wasn't his.

At the far end of the floor the iron plates stood waiting. The crowd had pulled back to make a lane. Two hundred faces, all lit green. Somewhere among them was Tamara. Somewhere among them, he supposed, was his father.

Don't look for him. Look at the plates.

He turned the key.

The coil spun up. The clean high whine climbed, the right note, *the* note, into his wrist, into his teeth, and for one second: there. There you are.

Then the note went sour.

It coughed. There was no other word for it. *Pfff-tuk.* Something deep in the housing caught and let go and caught again, *tuk, tuk-tuk*, and the gauntlet bucked on his arm and pulled him forward. He went with it. Two steps, four, half running, half falling across the polished floor toward the plates with his arm stuck out in front of him, a man being taken for a walk by his own invention.

He stopped about halfway. The coil died with a little sigh. A wisp of smoke came out of the wrist housing and went straight up in the still air, and hung there, and was the only thing in the hall moving.

Then somebody laughed near the front, one man, and then all of them.

Most of it wasn't even cruel, which was nearly the worst part. It was the laugh you'd give a puppy that ran into a door: fond, delighted, a little relieved it wasn't you. It went round the hall and up into the girders and came back down, and it went on for what seemed a very long time, and Nathaniel stood in the middle of the floor with his arm out and smoke coming off his wrist and could not for the life of him think what to do with his face.

He looked for his father. Eldgard was on the dais, talking to the alderman beside him. He wasn't looking at the floor. He wasn't looking anywhere near it, and he was doing it with great care.

Halloran had taken out a notebook and was writing something in it.

And Nathaniel looked, he couldn't have said why, for Tamara. She stood by a pillar a little way off with her spectacles on, and she wasn't laughing. Not even politely. She was looking at the gauntlet on his arm, at the smoke, with the same frown she'd turned on the case, as though it were a page she had nearly read.

Well, Nat. At least one person in London is taking you seriously. Pity she's the one you've been sold to.

He unbuckled the gauntlet. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Put it in the case and closed the lid. Then he walked off the floor through the lane they'd made for him, and it closed behind him like water.

By the side doors Byron stood with a tray, and an old lady in purple, one of the Pembertons, had him by the sleeve.

"But where *is* Josephine? I've looked everywhere. She was to introduce me to the professor."

"Her ladyship has gone home, madam," said Byron. "A headache."

"A *headache*? Josephine? I've known her thirty - well. Goodness."

"Very sudden, madam. She asked me to make her apologies."

Nathaniel stopped. Byron's eyes came round to him over the old lady's lace and rested there, and his face was the face it always was, smooth and closed as a good watch.

His mother had never had a headache in her life. Nathaniel had once watched her host a dinner for thirty with a broken wrist and not mention it until the ices.

Byron held his eyes a moment longer than a butler should. Then, very slightly, he shook his head. Not now, Master Nat. Whatever it is. Not here.

And Nathaniel, who had spent his whole childhood trying to make that face show something, let it go.

* * *

The roof of the Works was flat lead ringed by a stone parapet, and nobody ever went up there, which was why Nathaniel did.

He sat on the parapet with the case beside him and his legs hanging over sixty feet of nothing. A fine cold drizzle came and went. Below him London ran off in every direction, and it was green. The lamps of Mayfair and St James's burned in long clean emerald strings, square after square, every one of them fed by the hum under his boots. Eastward it went patchy. Past the Tower the lights broke into a pattern, lit, dark, lit, every third one out. No need to count. Nobody had ever told him why.

He'd never asked. Sitting up there with smoke still in his nose, that struck him for the first time as a strange thing for a curious man never to have asked.

He took the gauntlet out and sat with it in his lap. It looked exactly as it had that morning. Beautiful, and right. His, and not.

Behind him the roof door went *creak* and *clunk*. He didn't turn round. There was only one person in the building who knew he came up here.

Jamie sat down on the parapet beside him, close, not quite touching, and didn't say anything. That was the best thing he could have done, and of course Jamie knew it. Jamie always knew it. It was infuriating. They sat. The drizzle came and went. Down in the hall the orchestra had started again, faint as something heard through water.

"Mrs Pemberton," Jamie said eventually, "is telling everyone you were magnificent."

"She is not."

"She is. She thinks it was a dance. She's asked Mother who your teacher is."

Nathaniel laughed before he could stop it, a horrible snorting noise, and then he couldn't stop it, and he sat on the edge of the roof of the Works with his ruined gauntlet in his lap and laughed until his eyes ran, and Jamie sat beside him with a face of perfect gravity and waited for him to finish, the bastard, the way he'd been waiting for Nathaniel to finish things all his life.

When he was done Jamie held out his hand. Nathaniel gave him the gauntlet.

His brother turned it in the light from the roof lamp. Looked at it a long time: the housing, the wrist, the scorch where the smoke had come out. He touched the casing over the coil with one finger, carefully. Nathaniel had never seen him look at anything Nathaniel had made with that much attention. Had never seen him look at anything with that much attention.

"It nearly worked," Jamie said.

"It worked for one second."

"I saw the second." Jamie handed it back. He put his hand on the back of Nathaniel's neck, briefly. He'd done that when they were small and Nathaniel fell out of the walnut tree and broke his arm and wouldn't cry because Nixon was watching. Then he took it away. "Nat. Listen. Never lose the light within you."

Nathaniel looked at him. "That's... well, that's on the sampler," he said. "In the nursery. Above the washstand."

"I know."

"Mother stitched it. When she was a girl. It's got a rabbit on it."

"I know where it's from, Nat." Jamie was smiling, but only with his mouth. "I'm saying it anyway. I'm saying it to *you*. Would you just - for once in your life, would you let somebody say something to you without taking it apart to see how it works?"

There wasn't an answer to that. Nathaniel didn't have one, anyway. Jamie squeezed his shoulder. Two fingers and a thumb. Do you want out?

This time Nathaniel didn't cover his hand. He was holding the gauntlet, and by the time he thought to let go of it, Jamie had stood up.

It was the last time he would ever see his brother standing. He didn't know that. He was looking at the coil.

"Where are you going?"

"Oh... home," said Jamie. "I don't know. Home, I suppose." Then he heard himself, and something in his face set. "Home. I've got something to do."

"Now? Father's going to want you for the - "

"Father's going to want me for a great many things." Jamie buttoned his coat. He looked down at the lit squares of Mayfair for a moment, and then east, at the broken ones. "Stay up here a bit. It's quieter. Go down when you're ready, Nat, not before. Promise me that much."

"All right. I promise."

The roof door went *creak*, and *clunk*, and he was gone.

* * *

He stayed. He stayed till his legs were cold and the drizzle had soaked through the shoulders of his coat, and then he went down, because there was nowhere else to go and he'd promised he'd go down when he was ready, and he was never going to be ready.

The hall had thinned. It was nearly midnight. A hundred and fifty people, louder now, drunker, and the orchestra playing something with a polka in it. Nobody looked at him as he came in. That was almost worse. He found a pillar and stood against it with the case at his feet, watching the floor, thinking of nothing, when the light went.

Not out. Down.

Every chandelier in the hall sank at once, all forty, the green-white draining out of them into brown and then into a sullen ember orange. The hum under the floor fell a full octave in a second, *vvvvoooommm*, up through his soles into his back teeth. The orchestra stopped. A woman shrieked and then laughed, embarrassed. People were looking up. For once in their lives everybody was looking up.

And under the hum, under the floor, very far down, somebody was crying.

A woman. Clear as anything. Not shrieking, not calling out. Just crying, a long hopeless sound from someone who'd been at it for years and had stopped expecting anyone to come. It came up through the floorboards and the iron and the soles of his shoes. It went on. Nobody else in the hall turned their head.

Nathaniel looked round wildly. A man near him was relighting a cigar. Two girls were giggling in the half dark. Tamara stood by the far wall with her spectacles on, looking up at the chandeliers with her lips moving. Counting something.

"Do you hear that?" he said, to nobody, much too loudly.

The man with the cigar edged away from him. Of course he did. The Harding boy who'd danced with his own gauntlet, now hearing voices in the floor. They'd be dining out on him for a month.

On the dais a boy in Works livery had come running in at the side door, soaked through and capless. He went straight to Eldgard and said something into his ear, and Nathaniel saw his father's face change, not much. It went perfectly still. Then Eldgard set his glass down on the table beside him, very precisely, walked off the dais and out through the side door with the boy at his heels, and didn't say a word to anyone.

The chandeliers came back up, slowly, to green-white, and the hum climbed back to its note. Somebody started clapping. Others joined in, glad to have been part of something.

The crying had stopped. Or he couldn't hear it any more. He stood against his pillar with his heart going hard and could not, for the life of him, have told you which.

* * *

There were four constables in the front hall at Harding House.

Nathaniel stood in the doorway with the rain behind him and the case in his hand and looked at them, and they looked back. One was writing in a notebook. Another stood at the foot of the main stair with his helmet off, turning it round in his hands. He was very young. His face was the colour of the plaster.

Every lamp in the hall was lit. The house was full of light and nobody was speaking. Think, Nat. Who sends four constables to a house in Mayfair at midnight?

Nathaniel put the case down. Put his bare hand on the newel post, as he always did coming in, without thinking. Took it away again fast.

The banister was ice. Not cold: ice. A white fur of frost ran up the polished mahogany as far as the eye could follow, thickening as it climbed toward the first landing, and when he snatched his palm off it the skin stung, and some of it stayed behind on the wood.

From the back of the hall, by the door to the servants' stair, came a small noise. Ada was standing there. She'd been doing the landing lamps. Her rags were still over one arm and her taper in the other hand, unlit. She was a small round woman of sixty who'd polished the lamps in this house since before Nathaniel was born, who saved him the burnt ends of the Sunday joint because he liked them and told nobody, and he'd never once seen her without her cap on straight. It was crooked now.

"Master Nat," she said. "He come up the back stair, Master Jamie did. He had a spanner, one of the big ones from the Works. I said, I said why aren't you at the... and he said go to bed, Ada. Ever so kind about it. Go to bed." She looked at the rags in her hand as if somebody else had put them there. "I only went down the one flight."

Somebody was coming down the main stair. Byron, still in his coat, wet at the shoulders from the carriage. He came slowly, holding the frosted rail with a gloved hand, and stopped three steps from the bottom so his face was level with Nathaniel's, and put himself in the way.

"Sir," said Byron. "Don't go up."

Nathaniel went up.

Past Byron, who didn't stop him and didn't try very hard to, and up the stair two at a time with the frost crackling under his palm on the rail, *crk, crk, crk*. On the landing it was on the walls too, in long feathered sheets across the wallpaper his mother had chosen. The landing lamps were lit and their glass had frosted over, so they gave a strange furred light, white and green. His breath smoked. The carpet crunched.

The study door was open. Not three inches, either. All the way.

Cold came out of it. January cold, straight through the cloth of his coat. The window on the far side of the room was gone. Not open: gone, frame and all, burst outward over the street, and rain was coming in through the hole and turning to sleet as it crossed the sill. Papers lay frozen to the carpet. The decanter on the side table had split down its length and the brandy inside had frozen in the shape of the glass.

On the desk the brass cage that had held the ornament all his life was broken open, its bars bent outward, empty. Shards of green glass lay across the blotter, still faintly glowing. One went out while he stood there.

So that was what it was, Nat. All those years. You were right at nine. It was a heart.

Jamie was on the rug in front of the desk.

On his side, one arm flung out. The big Works spanner lay by his open hand. His shirt front had gone dark and his face had gone white, and frost had grown up out of his collar over his jaw and throat in fine branching lines like the veins of a leaf. Something cold had gone in through him and out the other side, and left the shape of itself behind.

His chest moved. Just. Up, and down.

Someone made a sound. Probably him.

He crossed the room. The frost on the rug cracked under his knees as he went down beside his brother, and he put his hands on him without knowing where to put them, on his shoulder, his face, the wet dark front of his shirt. It was warm. It was the only warm thing in the room. He pressed down hard on it with both palms, as if he could hold it all in by main force, as if this were a machine and you just had to find where the pressure was getting out.

Jamie's eyes opened and found him, and Nathaniel saw his brother know him.

"Nat," said Jamie. His lips barely moved. "I let it - "

"Don't talk. Don't. Byron's sending for - Byron! *Byron!*"

Jamie's hand came up off the rug, slowly, and found Nathaniel's shoulder. Two fingers and a thumb. It didn't have the strength to squeeze.

Do you want out?

Nathaniel covered it with his own and held it there, hard. No. I'll stay. Bent over his brother on the frozen rug, and shouted for help until his voice went.
