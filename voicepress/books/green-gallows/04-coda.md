# Coda: Blackwood Bridge

*Miriam*

The packet office in Wapping shut at six. Miriam got there at ten to, in the blue dress, with rain down the back of her neck and the Lambrick purse stuffed inside her mitten, where anyone who wanted it would have to take the hand as well. Let them try.

A narrow room, wet wool and lamp oil, a counter, and behind the counter a bald clerk with a pen behind each ear, presumably in case one of them ran off. He didn't look up.

"We're closing, miss."

"Then you'll want to be quick. Three berths on the dawn boat to Southampton."

That got his eyes up. They went to the dress, which was good, then to the mittens, which weren't, and she could watch him do the sum and come out at housemaid who's robbed her mistress. Close. Wrong mistress.

"Three berths is four pound ten. In advance. And I'll need to see - "

She opened the purse on the counter and counted out five sovereigns, *tink, tink*, new as eggs, every one of them warm from her palm and every one of them Lord Lambrick's. The clerk stopped needing to see anything.

"Names?"

"Miriam Bounds."

He wrote it. Neat hand. Probably the best thing about him.

"James..." Careful, girl. Harding was a name that got remembered in a shipping office; Hardings owned half the shipping. "James Hardy."

The pen hovered. "Your husband, miss?"

"My business, mister."

He wrote Hardy. The ones who lasted in this job had learnt to stop at the second question, and he'd lasted.

"And the third?"

"Alice Bounds." It caught on something halfway out, and Miriam coughed and blamed the damp. Twenty today. Asleep by now, if she had any sense, with an orange in her and no idea she was going to sea. Alice would hate the sea. She'd be sick over the rail all the way to Southampton and blame Miriam for every heave of it, and Miriam was going to stand there and hold her hair and let her. "With an I."

"Most Alices are, miss."

"Then you'll get it right."

He blotted the tickets and slid them over. Three slips of yellow card, ink still shining. She put them in the mitten with the purse, flat against her palm, where the edges could dig in. Good. She wanted to feel them every minute till dawn.

Outside it was nearly dark and the rain had settled in for the night. A girl in a laundry shawl was keeping dry under the chandler's awning across the street, and Miriam's eye went over her as it would over a lamp post. Wet shawls in Wapping: there were a thousand of them. She whistled up a cab and gave the driver Paradise Row. The wheels went *shhhk* through the standing water at the corner, and back by the awning somebody else whistled for a cab too. Wet night. Everybody wanted one.

* * *

The coach was where Jamie had said, at the bottom of Paradise Row by the bridge stairs: a hired box with a tired horse and the blinds down. She paid off the cab and climbed in and sat in the dark for the best part of an hour with the tickets in her fist, listening to the horse work at its nosebag and telling herself he wasn't coming. Practice. Say it enough beforehand and it didn't hurt as much after. That was the theory.

Then the door opened, and Jamie Harding folded himself in out of the rain, and the theory went in the river.

He was big. She always forgot that between times, and then he'd come through a door and use up all the air. Broad in the shoulder, fair hair gone dark brass with wet, and a face built for being liked: wide mouth, straight brows, a cleft in the chin he was vain of and pretended not to be. Evening dress under a plain greatcoat that wasn't his, the sleeves a hand too short. Wet shoes. No gloves.

No gloves. Jamie Harding, who had once gone back up four flights for his gloves while she stood in the street holding a bag of somebody's candlesticks. So it was bad, then. Whatever it was, it was very bad, and he'd come anyway.

"You smell of the Works," she said.

"I gave the speech." He sat down opposite, his knees knocking hers. "They clapped. Father didn't. That's how you know it went well."

"Did you look at the lamps?"

"I never look at the lamps."

"Liar."

That got half a smile. Only half. She held the tickets up between two fingers, a sharp showing the queen.

"Dawn boat to Southampton. You're Mr Hardy now, God help you. It's a better name. Suits the chin."

He took them and tilted them to the crack of light at the blind. His eyes went down the names. First, second... and on the third he stopped and looked up at her, and some of whatever had been eating him since the door opened let go its grip.

"You're bringing her."

"I'm bringing her. I'm telling her tomorrow, all of it." She took the tickets back before he could do anything soppy with them. "Then she'll push me in the Solent and you'll have to fish me out."

"She won't."

"You've met her twice."

"Three times. She called me a ponce."

"That's her being fond."

He laughed, properly, and then stopped laughing in the middle, as if somebody had laid a hand on his chest. Leaned his head back against the cushion. Shut his eyes. There it was again, that face. She'd seen it on men along the Ratcliff Highway the night before they turned informer: somebody doing a sum and hating the answer.

"I have to go home first," he said.

And there it was.

"No, you don't."

"An hour. Less. If I don't - " He stopped. "An hour. They're all at the Exhibition till past one. Ada goes to bed at eleven. Ada's gone to bed at eleven since the old king."

"Jamie. Whatever it is, it'll still be there tomorrow, and you'll be in Southampton, and it won't be your problem."

"That's the trouble," he said. "It's been my problem since I was born. I just never looked at it."

Rain on the roof. The horse shifted and the whole box rocked. Miriam waited for the joke. There was always a joke; he couldn't help himself. It didn't come, and that frightened her more than the gloves had.

"There's a thing on my father's desk," Jamie said. "Glass, in a brass cage, the size of an orange. People take it for a paperweight. I took it for a paperweight, for twenty-five years." He opened his eyes. "Three nights ago he walked me down under the Works. There's a woman down there, Miri, in a tank full of green... she's not from here. She's not anything like us. And she talked to me. Not out loud." He touched his temple. "In here. She asked me where her child was."

Right. Miriam had seen a man knifed on the Ratcliff Highway for a shilling. She'd seen worse than that, and done some of it. She had never before had the hair on her arms stand up in a hired coach because a man with a cleft chin tapped the side of his own head.

"You've been drinking."

"Not enough."

"You heard a voice in a cellar." Even to her it sounded thin. "People in cellars hear all sorts, Mr Hardy. Mostly rats."

"It moves, Miri. The thing in the box. When I'm in the room, it moves toward me."

"Then stay out of the - "

"I can't."

"It's been in that box as long as she's been in the tank." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, close enough that she could smell the brandy and the rain, and under them the burnt-penny smell of the Works that got into everything north of the river. "I'm going to break it. Let it out. Then I'm coming to the bridge at midnight, and we're getting on that boat, and I'm never setting foot in that house again as long as I live."

Every sensible bone she had said no. Get him on the boat tonight and let his father keep his paperweight.

"What is it?" she said instead. "In the box."

He went quiet. Long enough that she gave up on an answer.

"A child," said Jamie. "I think it's a child."

Well. That was that, then.

Because there had been a bed once, in a room in Limehouse, and under the bed, in the dust, a little girl with her hands over her ears, counting fence posts out loud so she wouldn't hear the belt. Eight posts and he's done. And beside her on the floor, the big sister who'd promised nothing would touch her while she was there. Who lay there, and listened, and never once moved. Not once. Not one finger.

Miriam had been making up for that finger for fourteen years. Boxes, she understood.

"Then go and let it out," she said. "And if you're late to the bridge I'll come and fetch you, and I'll bring Alice, and she'll call you a ponce in front of your mother."

He kissed her. Cold all over, and his mouth warm, and she held on to the lapels of the borrowed coat a breath longer than was clever. Then he was out in the rain with his hat in his hand, and she sat in the dark with three tickets cutting into her palm and listened to him run, actually run, up Paradise Row toward Mayfair.

Idiot. Lovely idiot. She'd known it from the first night, when she'd had his pocketbook and he'd asked if she'd like the watch chain too, since she'd gone to all the trouble.

* * *

Blackwood Bridge at midnight was black stone, rain, and a long way down.

Narrow and old, with a parapet as high as her ribs and a lamp at each end. The Southwark lamp was out. Hers hissed green in the wet and threw her shadow halfway across the span, which was a stupid place to stand if you didn't want to be seen, and she stood there anyway. Let him spot her from the end of the street. Let him not have to look for her, for once.

Under her the Thames ran fast and black, smelling of coal smoke and the sea, pulling round the piers with a long *shhhooo*. She'd known that river all her life. She'd been in it once, at sixteen, waist-deep at the Limehouse stairs with her hands full of a man. Counting.

Don't. Not tonight. Eight years' practice at not thinking about it; use them.

The church clocks started for midnight all over London, none of them together. St Mary's went first, as usual, because St Mary's thought it was in charge. She counted them anyway. Twelve, then the stragglers, then nothing but rain.

Her lamp sank.

Just hers, for a heartbeat; then she looked across the water and saw the whole Embankment going with it, the long necklace of green dropping to brown, a city with its wick turned down. Three breaths. Four. Then back up, all of it, as if nothing had happened.

Miriam found she was grinning at the river. He's done it. Mad, brave, gloveless idiot, he's gone and done it.

Wheels on the cobbles. A good black town carriage, a matched pair, lamps unlit, coming over from the Southwark side at a walk. It stopped at the far end under the dead lamp.

Not Jamie. Jamie would come on foot, running, hat in his hand. She'd have bet both her own hands on it.

The carriage door opened. A woman got down, tall, in a long dark hooded cloak, and came along the middle of the bridge neither fast nor slow, as if the cobbles had been laid for her. As she crossed the edge of the lamplight Miriam's eyes went where they always went first. Hands. Grey kid gloves, buttoned at the wrist, fine as skin. Twelve shillings a pair if they were a penny. Better than any she'd ever lifted.

Gloves like that didn't come out on bridges at midnight. Gloves like that sent someone.

"Miss Bounds." Low and pleasant, a voice for drawing rooms. "I wonder if we might have a word. About my son."

Oh.

Oh, hell.

She had a second to think it, and a second was a long time on a job. Long enough to know she'd put the rail at her back, which was wrong. Long enough to see the hand come out from under the cloak and the thing in it catch the green. She turned and got one mitten up. Not enough. All those years of being quick, and it turned out quick was never enough.

It went in under her ribs, a punch that didn't stop. The breath went out of her in one long *hhhhah*. Hands at her waist and shoulder, strong, much stronger than drawing rooms had any right to make them, and the parapet against the backs of her thighs, and then not. She grabbed at the cloak. Got a fistful. Heard it tear.

Then there was nothing under her at all.

A long way down. Long enough to watch the bridge go up and away and the hooded shape lean over the rail once, looking, then step back into the dark. Long enough for one clear thought: three tickets, dawn boat. Oh, Ali. I was going to tell you tomorrow.

The river hit her like a floor.

* * *

Cold.

In everywhere at once, through the dress, through the skin, into her teeth and the hole in her side. She opened her mouth to swear and the Thames came in instead. Black. Up was gone, and down with it. The current had her and rolled her, and her boots filled and dragged, and when she kicked, her legs had stopped taking orders.

Something warm left her side in a slow ribbon. She couldn't see it go. Filed it anyway, for later, like a cut in a fight.

So this was how. She'd always reckoned on a knife in an alley, or a rope, if she got unlucky with a magistrate. Never water. Funny, that. She'd held a man under at the Limehouse stairs and counted to a hundred and twenty while he kicked, and told herself all the way home that she'd done it for Alice, and never once been sure. Well, here's your answer, Miri. The river keeps accounts.

She stopped kicking.

And there was light.

Far above, through all that black, something was coming down. A thread of it, pale green and colder than the river, turning as it came. Looking for something. It found her. It slid down to her side where the blood was and stopped, close enough to touch, and hung in the water beside her face. Shivering. Small.

Hello, you. Out of your box, then. Good lad. Good girl. Whichever you are.

A child, Jamie had said. Jamie, who was supposed to be on the bridge.

Too late for her, of course. She had a notion it knew that too. It didn't go away.

Her lips moved. There was no air in them. Only the last thing she had left to spend, and she spent it on the one person who'd be lying awake in Gin Lane, facing the door, with half an orange going dry on the next pillow.

Go back for my sister.

The light went into her.

Miriam Bounds sank through the black water of the Thames with her red mittens open and three yellow tickets floating up out of them one by one, toward a surface she was never going to see again, and for a while nothing happened at all.

Then, at the bottom of the river, in the dark, her eyes opened.
