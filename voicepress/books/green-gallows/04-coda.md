# Coda: Blackwood Bridge

*Miriam*

The packet office in Wapping shut at six, and Miriam got there at ten to, in the blue dress, with rain on her shoulders and the Lambrick purse inside her mitten where nobody could take it without taking the hand.

It was a narrow room smelling of wet wool and lamp oil, with a counter across it and a clerk behind the counter. Bald, fifty, a pen behind each ear. He didn't look up.

"We're closing, miss."

"Then I'll be quick. Three berths on the dawn boat to Southampton."

Now he looked up. He looked at the dress, which was good, and at the red mittens, which weren't, and made his choice about her somewhere between the two.

"Three berths is four pound ten. In advance."

Miriam took the purse out of her mitten and opened it on the counter. She counted out five sovereigns. They rang on the wood, *tink, tink*, bright and new, every one of them stamped with the old king's head and every one of them Lord Lambrick's, though only she knew that. The clerk's eyebrows went up and stayed up.

"Names?"

"Miriam Bounds." She watched him write it.

"James... " She stopped. The pen waited. "James Hardy."

"Hardy."

"With a Y."

He wrote that too. If he thought anything about a girl in red mittens booking a berth for a gentleman, he'd been in the job too long to show it.

"And the third?"

"Alice Bounds," said Miriam, and her voice did something on it that she hadn't expected and didn't like. She cleared her throat. "Alice. With an I."

"Most Alices are, miss."

He blotted the tickets and pushed them across. Three slips of yellow card, the ink still shining. Miriam picked them up and held them a moment. Then she put them inside her mitten with the purse, against her palm, where she could feel the edges.

Outside it was nearly dark and the rain had settled in. Across the street a girl in a laundry shawl was standing under the chandler's awning, out of the wet, the way anybody would. Miriam didn't look at her twice. She whistled up a cab and gave the driver an address near Blackwood Bridge and climbed in, and the cab pulled off into the traffic on the High Street, wheels hissing, *shhhk*, through the standing water.

Under the awning, the girl in the shawl waited till it had gone. Then she stepped out into the rain and caught the next one.

* * *

The coach was where Jamie had said it would be, on the corner of Paradise Row by the bridge stairs, a hired thing with a tired horse and the blinds down. Miriam paid off her cab and got in out of the rain.

She'd been sitting in the dark with the tickets in her hand for most of an hour when the door opened again and Jamie climbed in, shaking water off his hat.

He was a big man, Jamie Harding. She always forgot that between times. Broad in the shoulder, fair-haired, with a face built for being liked: wide mouth, straight brows, a cleft in the chin he was vain of and pretended not to be. Tonight he was in evening dress under a plain dark greatcoat that didn't belong to him, and his shoes were soaked, and his gloves were in his pocket instead of on his hands. That was how she knew he was frightened. Jamie never forgot his gloves.

"You've been to the Works," she said. "You smell of it."

"I gave the speech." He sat down opposite her, knee to knee in the little box. "They clapped. Father didn't. That's how you know it went well."

"Did you look at the lamps?"

"No."

"Good." She held up the tickets. "Dawn boat to Southampton. Three berths."

Jamie took them from her and turned them to the light from the crack in the blind. He read each name. When he got to the third he stopped, and looked up at her, and some of the fear went out of his face.

"You're bringing her."

"I'm bringing her. I'm telling her tomorrow, all of it." Miriam took the tickets back. "If she doesn't push me in the river first."

"She won't. She'll... no. She won't."

"You don't know Alice."

"I know you." He leaned his head back against the cushion and shut his eyes. He looked very tired. "I have to go home first."

"Jamie."

"An hour. Less. If I don't - no. An hour. The house is empty, they're all at the Exhibition till past one. Nobody's there but Ada, and Ada goes to bed at eleven like a clock." He opened his eyes. "There's something in my father's study, Miri. On his desk. I've been looking at it all my life and I never once saw it. Not till three nights ago."

She waited. Outside, a cart went by. The horse shifted in the shafts and the coach rocked.

"It's a glass box," said Jamie. "Green glass, in a brass cage. People think it's a paperweight. It's been on that desk since before I was born. And there's something alive in it."

"Alive."

"He took me down under the Works on my birthday. There's a woman down there, Miri. In a sort of... tank, like the box, only big. He calls it the pod. She's been there for years, I don't know how many, and she's not - she isn't from here. She isn't like us. And she spoke to me." He touched his temple. "In here. She asked me where her child was."

Miriam didn't say anything. She'd seen a man knifed on the Ratcliff Highway for a shilling, and she'd seen worse than that, done some of it, and she sat very still in the dark of the coach and felt the hair stand up on her arms.

"So I'm going to break it," said Jamie. "The box. Let it out. Let it go home, or wherever it goes. And then I'm coming to the bridge, and we're getting on that boat, and I'm never going back to that house again."

"What is it?" said Miriam. "In the box."

Jamie was quiet for a while.

"A child," he said. "I think it's a child."

She thought about a bed in a room in Limehouse, years ago, and the space underneath it, and the dust, and a little girl lying in the dust with her hands over her ears, counting fence posts out loud so she wouldn't hear the belt. Eight posts, and he's done. She thought about her own hands, which hadn't moved. Not once.

"Then you'd better let it out," she said.

He kissed her. Cold all over, and his mouth warm, and she held on to the lapels of the borrowed coat a moment longer than she meant to. Then he was gone into the rain, hat in his hand, and she sat back in the coach with three yellow tickets against her palm and listened to his footsteps go away up Paradise Row toward Mayfair.

* * *

Blackwood Bridge at midnight was black stone, rain, and a long way down.

It was an old bridge, narrow, with a parapet as high as her ribs and a lamp at each end. The lamp at the Southwark end was dark. The one at her end burned green, hissing a little in the wet, and threw her shadow long across the cobbles toward the middle of the span, where she stood with her red mittens on the rail.

Below her the Thames went by fast and black, smelling of coal smoke and the sea. She could hear it under the rain, a deep pull of water around the piers. *Shhhooo.* She'd known that river all her life. She'd been in it once, when she was sixteen, up to her waist at the Limehouse stairs, counting. She didn't think about that. She'd spent eight years getting good at not thinking about that.

The church clocks went for midnight, all over London, out of step with each other. She counted them.

Something happened to the lamp.

It sank. Just the one, at her end of the bridge, the light going down out of green into a dull brown ember, and the hiss of it fading. And across the river every lamp she could see along the Embankment went with it, all at once, a long necklace of light going dim. For three breaths the city was nearly dark. Then it came back.

Miriam stood with her hands on the wet stone and found she was smiling. *He's done it.* She didn't know how she knew, but she knew.

Wheels on the cobbles. A carriage was coming over from the Southwark end, slow, its lamps unlit, a good black town carriage with a pair. It stopped at the far end of the bridge, under the dark lamp.

It wasn't Jamie. Jamie would have come on foot, running. She knew that too.

The carriage door opened. Someone got down. A woman, tall, in a long dark cloak with the hood up, and she came along the bridge toward Miriam not quickly and not slowly, walking in the middle of the cobbles as though she owned the whole of it. As she passed under the edge of the lamplight Miriam saw her gloves. Grey kid, very fine, buttoned at the wrist. Good gloves. Better than any she'd ever stolen.

"Miss Bounds," said the woman. Her voice was low and pleasant. "I wonder if we might have a word. About my son."

Under the arch of the bridge, at the foot of the stone stair down to the water, Jennie stood with her back against the wet wall, shawl over her head, one hand pressed over her own mouth. Didn't move.

It was very quick, after.

Miriam saw the hand come out from under the cloak. Saw what was in it, and knew, and turned and got one mitten up, and it wasn't enough. Something went into her side under the ribs like a punch, only it didn't stop. The breath went out of her in one long *hhhhah*. There were hands on her, strong, at her waist and shoulder, and the parapet was against the backs of her thighs, and then it wasn't. She caught at the cloak and got a fistful, and it tore.

Then there was nothing under her at all.

She fell a long way. Long enough to see the bridge go up away from her, and the shape at the rail lean over once, looking down, and then step back into the dark and be gone. Long enough to think, quite clearly, of three yellow tickets in her mitten.

The river hit her like a floor.

* * *

Cold.

It went in everywhere at once. Through the blue dress, through her skin, into her teeth, her chest, the hole in her side, and she opened her mouth to scream and the Thames came in. Black. Everything black. The current had her. Turned her over, over again, till she didn't know which way was up. Her boots were full and dragging. When she kicked, her legs didn't do much.

Something warm left her side in a slow ribbon. She couldn't see it. She felt it go.

So this is how. She'd always wondered. She'd thought it would be a knife in an alley, or a rope. Not water. Never water. She'd held a man under at the Limehouse stairs eight years ago and counted to a hundred and twenty while he kicked, and she'd told herself she did it for Alice, and she'd never once been sure that was true.

*I was going to tell her tomorrow.*

She stopped kicking. It didn't seem to matter now.

And there was light.

Above her, far above, through all that black water, something was coming down. A streak of it, pale green and cold, colder than the river, turning as it came, like a thread being pulled through cloth. Down through the dark it came, looking for something. It found her. It came to her side where the blood was and stopped there, close enough to touch, and hung in the water beside her face. Shivering. Small.

*A child,* she thought. *I think it's a child.*

It was too late. She knew that, and she thought perhaps it knew it too. It didn't go away.

Her lips moved. No air came out of them. Only the last thing, the only thing she had left.

*Go back for my sister.*

The light went into her.

Miriam Bounds sank through the black water of the Thames with her red mittens open and three yellow tickets floating up out of them one by one toward a surface she would never see again, and for a while nothing happened at all.

Then, in the dark, in the cold, at the bottom of the river, her eyes opened.
