# The Wrong Chair

*Nathaniel, long afterward*

People who have never sat with the dying believe there are last words. They picture a hand lifted from the sheet, a breath gathered, something said that can be carved over a door. I believed it myself at twenty-one. I'd read the same novels as everyone else.

There are no last words. There are only the last ones you happened to hear, and you spend the rest of your life trying to make them mean something.

I should say at the start that my memory of that night is a poor witness. It's careful about the wrong things. The brandy in my father's decanter, for instance, had frozen solid in the shape of the glass, and that the glass had split down one side and left the brandy standing there on the side table like an amber ghost of itself. The wallpaper on the landing was a green stripe my mother had chosen in 1881 and that the frost had come up it in feathered sheets, so that the stripes showed through underneath. Could draw you the frost, too. Have done, more than once, in the margins of things I was meant to be working on.

I cannot tell you, with any certainty, what my brother's face looked like. I've tried. It comes to me as Jamie at the dining table, raising his glass, or Jamie on the roof of the Works with my ruined gauntlet in his hands. It does not come to me as Jamie on the rug. Some kindness in the machinery of the mind won't let it. Or some cowardice in me. After this many years I've stopped trying to tell those two apart.

So I'll give you what I have, and you'll forgive the gaps.

* * *

There were four constables in the hall, and one of them was crying. That's the first thing. A boy, younger than me, with his helmet in his hands, turning it round and round. I remember being angry with him for it. It seemed to me, standing in my own front door with the rain behind me and my case in my hand, that he had no right to cry in our hall. He didn't know us. Whatever had happened upstairs, it wasn't his.

I was wrong about that, as it turned out. He was the first one through the study door. He'd seen more of it than I ever would.

I put my hand on the banister, as I always did coming in. The wood was ice. It took a little of my palm when I pulled away, and for a week afterward I carried a patch of raw skin in the middle of my hand, and I would press on it, at dinner, under the table, to make it sting. I never told anyone that. I'm not sure why I'm telling you.

Ada was at the back of the hall in her crooked cap, saying something about a spanner. Byron was on the stair. He came down three steps and stopped with his face level with mine, which he had not done since I was nine and had set fire to the potting shed, and he said, "Sir. Don't go up."

He called me sir. He'd called me Master Nat all my life. That told me, really. Not the constables, not the frost. Byron, getting the word wrong on purpose so that I'd hear it.

I went up anyway. He let me. I've asked him since whether he could have stopped me, and he said, "Oh, easily, sir," in the voice he uses when he's telling the truth and would rather not be, and then he poured me a drink and we didn't speak of it again.

* * *

The study I've described elsewhere, and I won't do it twice. The window was gone. The cage on the desk was open. The thing my father kept in it all my life was gone, and I'd like to say that registered, that I drew conclusions and was clever about it. I wasn't. An empty cage, a teacup on the floor of a house that's burned down. Of all the things in that room, it seemed to me the least important. Went on seeming so for a whole winter.

Jamie was on the rug.

What I did next does me no great credit, so I'll set it down plainly. I went down on my knees beside him and the first thing in my head, the very first, before grief or fear or anything a brother ought to feel, was: where is it getting out?

Because he was a machine to me, just then. God forgive me, he was. He was a system losing pressure, and somewhere in him was the leak, and if I could find the leak and put my hand over it hard enough, the gauges would come back up. I put both palms on his shirt where it was darkest and leaned my weight on them. I was an engineer. I'd been an engineer since I could hold a screwdriver. It was the only way I had of loving anything.

"Nat," said my brother.

His eyes were open. I'd like to tell you they were full of meaning. They were mostly full of being cold. His lips had gone the colour of slate and there was frost in his eyebrows, little white crystals, and he looked absurd, like a man who'd fallen asleep in a snowdrift for a bet. I wanted to laugh. That's another thing I've never told anyone. Never told Tamara, even, and I've told Tamara most things, eventually, usually because she'd already worked them out.

"Don't talk," I said. "Byron's sent for - Byron! *Byron!*"

"Nat." Very faint. "You're kneeling on my hand."

I was. My left knee was square on his fingers. I moved it, and he made a small noise, *hhh*, that I think, I truly think, was meant to be a laugh.

"Sorry," I said. "Sorry. I didn't - I'm sorry."

"Did they laugh?"

"What?"

"At the Exhibition. Your gauntlet." He was looking past me, at the ceiling, or at the hole where the window had been. "Did they laugh at you?"

Why it mattered, I couldn't have said. I've had a great many years to work on it since, and I think I understand it now, but at the time I only knelt there with my hands full of my brother's blood and said, stupidly, "Yes."

"Good," said Jamie. "Means they'll remember you."

That was the last thing he said that made sense. I've had it engraved on nothing. I've never once repeated it to anyone until now, because the first person I told would say how kind it was, I was sure of it, how like him, and I didn't want it to be kind. I wanted it to be his.

He tried to say something else after that. "I let it - " and then something I couldn't catch, and then, quite clearly, "so cold, in there, Nat. Somebody should've..." He didn't finish. I've filled in the end of that sentence a hundred different ways over the years. Every one of them has been wrong. Every one has been mine. I've had to learn to live with both.

His hand came up off the rug. It found my shoulder. Two fingers and a thumb.

We'd had that signal since we were boys. Do you want out? It came from the receiving lines at our mother's parties, when one of us was about to say something unforgivable to a bishop, and the other would reach across and squeeze, and if you wanted rescuing you covered his hand, and he'd invent a fever or a fire or a horse that needed seeing to. Jamie rescued me, I suppose, forty or fifty times. I never once had to rescue him. I always assumed that was because he was better at bishops.

His fingers had no strength in them. They only rested there.

I covered them with mine. No. I'll stay.

I don't know how long we were like that. Byron tells me eleven minutes, and Byron has never in his life been wrong about the time, but it didn't feel like any number of minutes. It felt like the coach house at three in the morning, when the lamp is low and you're halfway through a drawing and you can't remember starting it and can't see how it ends.

At some point his hand had gone heavier on my shoulder. That's all. Nothing else changed. The rain came in through the window and turned to sleet on the sill. The shards on the desk had all gone dark. I kept my hand over his, because I'd said I'd stay, and it seemed important to keep my word to him for at least as long as it took me to understand he couldn't hold me to it.

Then there was another hand on my other shoulder.

Two fingers and a thumb.

I looked round, and it was Byron. He was kneeling on the frozen carpet in his good trousers, which he would never in a thousand years have done. His face was the face it always is, smooth and closed as a good watch, except that it wasn't. He'd learned the signal. Of course he had. He'd stood at the back of every receiving line we'd ever fidgeted through, holding a tray, watching everything, and he'd never once let on.

Do you want out?

I could have covered his hand. He'd have taken me down to the kitchen and put me by the range and made Ada find me something to eat, and he'd have dealt with the constables, my father, the long white business of the rest of the night, and he'd have done all of it beautifully. That was what he was offering. It was plain then, and it's plain now.

I didn't cover his hand. Didn't push it off, either. Left it where it was, and stayed where I was, and he stayed with me.

* * *

Three weeks after the funeral I came down late to breakfast, as I always did, and found a footman holding out the chair at my mother's right hand. Jamie's chair. Nobody said anything. My father went on reading his letters. My mother poured my tea herself, which she had not done since I was small, and set it at that place, and did not look at me while she did it.

I sat down. It was the wrong chair, and it always would be, and I sat in it for a great many years.

But I want to set down, here at the end, that I didn't sit in it alone that first morning. Byron was at the sideboard with the coffee, as he always was, and as I pulled the chair in he came past behind me and set down a plate of the burnt ends of the bacon, which Ada saved for me because I liked them, and which nobody ever served at that end of the table.

His hand touched my shoulder on the way past, the old signal, so light I might have imagined it.

I picked up my fork. I ate every piece.
