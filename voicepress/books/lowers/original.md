# The Lowers, After Dark

The lantern above the pawnbroker's door swung on a rusted chain, throwing arcs of amber light across the wet cobblestones like a drunk trying to find his way home. *Creak-creak-creak.* Jonah Wren leaned against the alley wall opposite and watched the shop's window, where a hand-painted sign read MORATH & SONS — FAIR DEALINGS in letters so crooked they looked like they'd been written during an earthquake. The rain had stopped twenty minutes ago, but Veranesse never really dried. The city sweated. Stone and salt air and the low iron tang of the railsteam yards three blocks east, where the midnight shifts were changing over — he could hear the *clang-clang-clang* of the switching bells even here.

He shouldn't be in the Lowers at this hour. He knew that. Jessamine had told him as much when he'd pulled his coat on, her voice carrying that particular tone she reserved for moments when she wanted to call him a fool but was too well-bred to say it plainly.

*You'll get robbed.*

*I've got nothing worth robbing.*

*Then you'll get stabbed for the inconvenience of it.*

She wasn't wrong. The Lowers after dark were a different animal than the Lowers by day — during working hours the docks had a rhythm, a logic, crates moving and men shouting and the whole machine of commerce grinding forward, and a man who looked like he belonged to that machine could walk through without trouble. But after the last shift whistle, the streets belonged to people who operated outside of machines entirely. Fences. Cutpurses. The kind of women who leaned out of second-story windows and called down offers that were technically illegal in three of Veranesse's four quarters. The Lowers didn't care about technically.

Jonah cared about one thing: Sable hadn't come back.

She'd left that morning — slipped out before anyone was awake, which for a Felinara meant she'd been quiet on purpose, because those people could move through a house like smoke when they wanted to but Sable specifically chose to be loud. Doors slamming. Cabinets banging. That throaty little *mrrrr* she made when she was annoyed, which was most of the time. For her to leave silently meant she didn't want to be stopped, and for Sable not to want to be stopped meant she was doing something stupid.

Twelve hours. No word.

Mary Grieve had been the one to find out where she'd gone. Mary always found out — she had a network of dock kids and alley cats and former associates from her thieving days who owed her either favors or fear, and the information had cost Jonah only a half-silver and the promise that he'd stop asking Mary how she knew things.

*She went to Morath's. Alone.*

*What the hell for?*

*What do you think? The same thing she's been chewing on for weeks.*

Jonah had stared at her, and Mary had stared back — dark eyes flat and patient in that narrow face of hers, her lips pressed into the line that meant she'd already figured out the shape of the problem and was waiting for him to catch up. Mary Grieve stood five-foot-nothing with the posture of a coiled spring, her rust-brown hair scraped back and held with a pin that was definitely also a weapon. She wore a man's coat three sizes too big, the sleeves rolled to her wrists, and boots that had been resoled so many times the leather was more patch than original. Everything about her said *dockside* — the calluses, the economy of movement, the way her eyes swept a room's exits before she sat down.

*The collar registry,* Jonah said.

*Yeah.*

The collar registry. Because the Crescent had abolished beast-kin slavery eleven years ago — officially, formally, with a ceremony and speeches and probably little cakes — but the underground trade had never stopped, it just moved down into the Lowers where the law's lantern didn't swing. And every beast-kin who'd been sold through the black market had a record somewhere, because slavers were meticulous bastards who kept books the way legitimate businessmen kept books, with dates and prices and descriptions of the merchandise. Sable wanted those records. Specifically, she wanted *her* record — the one that would list who'd sold her into the pens in the first place, before Jonah had stumbled across her in that shit-smelling cellar off Brewer's Alley and paid fifteen silvers to a man with filed teeth for the privilege of telling a Felinara she was free.

Sable hadn't taken it well. Freedom, that is. Not the way Jonah had expected.

The lock on Morath's back door was a Vessen three-tumbler, which meant exactly nothing to Jonah because he didn't pick locks — that was Mary's department, and Mary wasn't here. Mary was two streets over, watching the alley's south exit, because they'd agreed that if this went sideways they needed a way out that didn't involve the front door and whatever muscle Morath kept on payroll.

Jonah pulled the iron pry bar from inside his coat and wedged it into the doorframe.

Subtlety had never been his strongest quality. Jessamine would have reminded him of that, too, if she were here — but Jessamine was in the Crescent, in the townhouse that smelled like lavender and old money, probably reading by lamplight with her stockinged feet tucked beneath her on the settee, and she had *specifically asked him not to do this.* The memory of her face when he'd told her about Sable's disappearance — the way her expression had shifted from annoyance to something harder, something that looked like calculation — sat in his chest like a stone.

*Crrrack.*

The frame splintered. The door swung inward and hit something metal — a bucket, from the sound — which hit something else, which crashed, and then the whole back room of Morath & Sons was alive with the sound of Jonah Wren making an entrance.

"Shit," he said, to no one.

The room smelled like dust and tallow and something underneath both — something animal and sharp, like a kennel that hadn't been cleaned. Shelves lined every wall, floor to ceiling, packed with the detritus of other people's desperation: silverware sets missing pieces, water-stained paintings in gilded frames, instruments with broken strings, a cavalry saber with a notched blade. The pawnbroker's hoard. Jonah moved through it with his shoulders hunched, pry bar still in hand, toward the narrow staircase at the back.

The stairs groaned under his weight. He was not a small man — six-two, broad through the shoulders and arms from years of ironwork, with hands that looked like they'd been designed for the specific purpose of gripping things and not letting go. His dark hair was damp from the earlier rain, pushed back from a face that Jessamine had once described as *having character,* which he'd understood to mean *not handsome but interesting to look at.* He'd take it.

At the top of the stairs: a hallway. Two doors, both closed. And beneath the second one, a thin line of lamplight.

He heard her before he opened it.

"—don't give a damn what it costs, you greasy little man, I want the *name.*"

Sable's voice. That particular frequency of hers — lower than you'd expect from someone her size, with a rasp in it like she'd been gargling gravel. Angry Sable was loud. This wasn't angry Sable. This was something past angry, something that had burned through fury and come out the other side into a cold, flat place where things got broken.

Jonah opened the door.

The room was small and windowless, lit by a single oil lamp on a desk that was buried under papers. Behind the desk sat a man who had to be Morath — sixtyish, bald, with spectacles perched on a nose that had been broken more than once and a mouth that naturally settled into an expression of permanent disapproval. He wore a shopkeeper's apron over a stained shirt and his fingers were ink-black to the second knuckle.

Sable stood in front of the desk with her back to the door. Her tail — dark as the rest of her, the fur sleek and bristling at the tip the way it did when she was agitated — was lashing in slow arcs behind her. Her ears were flat. She wore the same clothes she'd left in that morning: a boy's shirt, leather vest, trousers tucked into calf-high boots. A knife on her hip that she shouldn't have. Her dark hair, shot through with the same near-black as her fur, fell past her shoulders in a tangle she hadn't bothered to brush.

She didn't turn around.

"I told you not to come," she said.

"You didn't tell me anything. You snuck out like a cat burglar." He paused. "No offense."

"Full offense taken."

Morath looked between them with the expression of a man who had just realized his evening was about to get significantly worse. His hand was moving — slowly, carefully — toward the edge of the desk, where a bell cord hung from a hook on the wall.

"Touch that and I'll break every finger on that hand," Jonah said. Not loudly. Not with particular menace. Just a statement of fact, delivered in the same tone he might use to say *it looks like rain.*

Morath's hand stopped.

"Now." Jonah stepped into the room and closed the door behind him. The pry bar hung at his side, dripping rust-water onto the floorboards. "Someone's going to tell me what the hell is going on, and then we're all going to leave this building before whatever muscle you've got downstairs figures out I came through the back door like a goddamn battering ram."

Sable finally turned. Her eyes — amber, slit-pupiled, reflecting the lamplight like coins at the bottom of a well — met his, and in them he saw something he'd only seen once before: the same look she'd had in that cellar, the night he'd bought her freedom. Not gratitude. Not relief.

*Terror.*

Morath cleared his throat. "Your pet—"

"Finish that sentence," Sable said, "and I'll finish *you.*"

The pawnbroker swallowed. Adjusted his spectacles. Started again.

"Your... associate... came to me requesting access to records I may or may not possess regarding the pre-abolition trade in beast-kin labor contracts."

"Slavery," Sable said. "The word is slavery, you dusty prick."

"The records," Morath continued, as if she hadn't spoken, "are not mine to share. They belong to parties who would be... displeased to learn I'd allowed access."

Jonah looked at Sable. Sable looked at the floor. Her tail had gone still — completely still, which was worse than the lashing.

"He has them," she said quietly. "He has the whole ledger. Names, dates, buyers, sellers. Everything. He just showed me the page." Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, a hairline fracture in that armor she wore. "I saw who sold me, Jonah."

The room got very small.

"Who?" he said.

Sable's jaw worked. Her hands were fists at her sides, the short claws pressing crescents into her palms.

"My mother."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere outside, the switching bells rang out again across the Lowers — *clang-clang-clang* — and the city breathed its wet iron breath, indifferent to what had just broken open in this windowless room above a pawnbroker's shop.

Jonah put the pry bar on the desk. Morath flinched.

"The ledger," Jonah said. "All of it. Now."

He reached for his coin purse with one hand and took Sable's arm with the other — gently, the way you'd touch something that might shatter — and felt her trembling through the leather of her vest, fine and constant, like a wire pulled too tight.

The night wasn't over. It was just getting started.
