# The Lowers, After Dark

The lantern over the pawnbroker's door swung on a rusted chain, slopping amber light across the wet cobbles like a drunk looking for his own front step. *Creak-creak-creak.* Jonah Wren leaned on the alley wall opposite and watched the shop window, where a hand-painted sign read MORATH & SONS, FAIR DEALINGS, in letters so crooked the painter must have been working through an earthquake. Fair dealings. Sure. And he was the Archduke of Veranesse.

The rain had stopped twenty minutes ago, but Veranesse never really dried. The city sweated. Stone and salt air, and under them the low iron tang of the railsteam yards three blocks east, where the midnight shift was changing over. The switching bells carried even here, *clang-clang-clang*, flat and tired.

He shouldn't be in the Lowers at this hour. Jessamine had told him so while he was pulling his coat on, in the voice she kept for calling him a fool without ever using the word.

"You'll get robbed."

"I've got nothing worth robbing."

"Then you'll get stabbed for the inconvenience."

Fair point, Jess. Hard to argue with, standing in an alley that smelled of fish heads and somebody's bad decisions. The Lowers by day had a rhythm to them, crates moving, men shouting, the whole great engine of commerce grinding along, and a big man in a work coat could walk through the middle of it and pass for one of the moving parts. After the last shift whistle the engine stopped. The streets went to people who'd never been parts of anything. Fences. Cutpurses. Women leaning out of second-floor windows calling down offers that were illegal in three of the city's four quarters. The Lowers had never much cared about technically.

Jonah cared about one thing. Sable hadn't come back.

She'd slipped out before dawn, and quietly, which was how he'd known it was bad. A Felinara could cross a house like smoke if she wanted to. Sable never wanted to. Sable slammed doors. Sable banged cabinets hunting for the good cheese and made that throaty little *mrrrr* of hers when she couldn't find it, which was always, because Jessamine hid the good cheese. Jonah had never asked where. Some marriages ran on mystery. Sable going silent meant she didn't want to be - well. She didn't want to be stopped. For Sable not to want to be stopped meant she was about to do something stupid. Probably brave. Almost certainly both.

Twelve hours. Not a word.

Mary Grieve had found out where. Mary always found out. She had a web of dock kids and alley cats and old associates from her thieving days, every one of them owing her a favour or a scar, and the answer had cost Jonah a half-silver and a promise to stop asking how she knew things.

"Morath's," Mary had said. "Alone."

"What the hell for?"

"What do you think for? Same thing she's been chewing on for a month."

He'd stared at her. She'd stared back, dark eyes flat and patient in that narrow face, mouth pressed into the line that meant she'd already worked out the shape of the problem and was waiting, with no great hope, for him to catch up. Mary Grieve stood five foot nothing and held herself wound tight, ready to go off. Rust-brown hair scraped back under a pin that was certainly also a weapon. A man's coat three sizes too big with the sleeves rolled to her wrists, boots resoled so often they were more patch than boot. Calluses. No wasted movement. She counted a room's exits before she sat down in it, and she didn't sit down often.

"The collar registry," Jonah said.

"Yeah."

The collar registry. The Crescent had abolished beast-kin slavery eleven years ago, officially and formally, with a ceremony and speeches and very probably little cakes. The trade hadn't stopped. It had only gone downhill into the Lowers, where the law's lantern didn't reach. And slavers were meticulous bastards. They kept books the way honest merchants kept books, dates and prices and descriptions of the goods, so every beast-kin ever sold through the pens had a page somewhere. Sable wanted hers. She wanted the line that said who'd sold her in the first place, before Jonah went down into that shit-smelling cellar off Brewer's Alley and paid fifteen silvers to a man with filed teeth for the privilege of telling a Felinara she was free.

She hadn't taken it well. Freedom. Not the way he'd pictured, anyway. He'd pictured, God help him, something like thanks. What he'd got was a cat who slept with a knife and wouldn't eat with the door shut.

Morath's back door had a Vessen three-tumbler lock, which meant precisely nothing to Jonah, because Jonah didn't pick locks. Locks were Mary's department, and Mary was two streets over, watching the south mouth of the alley, because they'd agreed that if this went sideways they'd need a way out that didn't run through the front door and whatever Morath kept on the payroll.

He took the iron pry bar out of his coat and set it in the frame.

Subtlety had never been his gift. Jessamine would have pointed that out too, if she were here. But Jessamine was up in the Crescent, in the townhouse that smelled of lavender and old money, likely reading by the lamp with her stockinged feet tucked under her on the settee, and she had asked him, specifically and twice, not to do this. He kept seeing her face when he'd told her Sable was gone. Annoyed first. Then still. Then she'd looked past him at the dark window and started, he'd have sworn it, adding something up.

He hadn't liked the sum. He liked it less now.

*Crrrack.*

The frame gave. The door swung in and hit a bucket, which hit something else, which went over with a crash you could have heard in the Crescent, and the whole back room of Morath & Sons announced at the top of its lungs that Jonah Wren had arrived.

"Shit," he told the dark.

Dust and tallow. Under both, something sharp and animal that caught at the back of his throat: a kennel nobody had mucked out. Shelves ran floor to ceiling on every wall, packed with the leavings of other people's worst weeks. Silver sets missing a spoon. Water-stained portraits in gilt frames. A fiddle with no strings, a cavalry sabre with a notch out of the blade. Jonah went through it hunched, the bar in his fist, toward the narrow stair at the back.

The stairs complained about him all the way up. He wasn't a small man, six foot two and broad across the back from twenty years at the forge, with hands built for taking hold of things and not letting go. Wet dark hair pushed back off a face Jessamine had once called "full of character", which he'd translated without much effort as not handsome, but you'd look twice. He'd take it. Most days he'd take it.

At the top, a hallway. Two doors, both shut, and under the second a thin line of lamplight.

And her voice, through the wood.

"I don't give a damn what it costs, you greasy little man. I want the *name*."

Sable. Lower than you'd ever guess from the size of her, with a rasp in it like a file on soft iron. Angry Sable was loud. He'd met angry Sable, he'd had a plate thrown at him by angry Sable. This was quieter than that. This was the voice that came after fury had burned all the way through and left something cold and level at the bottom, the voice of somebody about to break a thing on purpose.

He opened the door.

A small room, no window. One oil lamp on a desk buried under paper. Behind the desk, a man who had to be Morath: sixty or so, bald, spectacles on a nose that had been broken more than once and set badly every time, a mouth that sagged by long habit into disapproval. Shopkeeper's apron over a stained shirt. Fingers inked black to the second knuckle. A man who wrote a great deal down.

Sable stood in front of the desk with her back to the door. Her tail, dark as the rest of her, the fur sleek and bristling at the tip, swept in slow arcs behind her. Ears flat to her skull. The same clothes she'd left in: boy's shirt, leather vest, trousers tucked into calf-high boots. A knife on her hip that Jonah had definitely not given her. Her hair, near black like her fur, fell past her shoulders in a tangle she hadn't touched since yesterday.

She didn't turn round.

"I told you not to come," she said.

"You didn't tell me anything. You snuck out like a cat burglar." Oh, well done, Jonah. Really. "No offence."

"Full offence taken."

Morath was looking from one of them to the other and plainly revising his plans for the evening. His right hand had begun to travel, slow and careful, toward the corner of the desk, where a bell cord hung from a hook on the wall.

"Touch that and I'll break every finger on the hand," Jonah said, in the voice he'd use to mention it might rain later.

The hand stopped.

"Now." He stepped in and shut the door behind him. Rust-water dripped off the end of the bar onto the boards, *tap*, *tap*. "Somebody's going to tell me what the hell is going on. Then we're all going to leave this building, before whoever you've got downstairs works out I came in the back like a battering ram."

Sable turned round at last.

Amber eyes, slit-pupiled, the lamplight sitting in them like coins at the bottom of a well. He'd seen that look on her once before. In the cellar, the night he'd paid for her. He'd told himself at the time it was shock. He'd told himself a lot of things, that night, walking her home through the rain with his coat round her shoulders while she flinched every time he moved his hand.

It hadn't been shock. It was this. She was frightened half out of her skin, and she was standing there anyway with her claws out, and Jonah's heart did something slow and painful under his ribs.

Morath cleared his throat. "Your pet - "

"Finish that," said Sable, "and I'll finish *you*."

The pawnbroker swallowed. Adjusted his spectacles. Began again, more carefully.

"Your associate came to me requesting access to certain records I may or may not hold, regarding the pre-abolition trade in beast-kin labour contracts."

"Slavery," said Sable. "The word is slavery, you dusty prick."

"Those records," Morath went on, as if she were weather, "are not mine to share. They belong to parties who would be... displeased... to learn I'd allowed access."

Jonah looked at Sable. Sable looked at the floor. The tail had stopped. Gone completely still, which was a great deal worse than the lashing. He'd learned that in the first month, along with where not to put his hands.

"He's got them," she said. "The whole ledger. Names, dates. Who bought, who sold. He showed me the page, Jonah. Just now." Her voice went on the last word, a hairline crack, there and gone. "I saw who sold me."

Jonah's mouth had gone dry. Funny, how fast that happened. He'd faced down a press-gang once with less trouble.

"Who?"

Her jaw worked. Her hands were fists at her sides, the short claws dug into her palms in little crescents. Blood in one of them.

"My mother."

The lamp guttered. Out across the Lowers the switching bells went again, *clang-clang-clang*, and the city breathed out its wet iron breath and went on about its business, and in a windowless room over a pawnshop a girl who'd survived the pens stood with blood in her fist and did not cry. She'd have hated him for noticing she didn't.

So he didn't notice. He set the pry bar down on the desk instead, and Morath flinched anyway.

"The ledger," Jonah said. "All of it. Now. And the bell cord stays where it is, or so help me I'll hang you from it."

He reached for his purse with one hand. The other he laid on Sable's arm, light as he could make it. Through the leather of her vest she was trembling, fine and constant, a wire strung too tight.

She didn't pull away. First time in a year.

Morath opened the bottom drawer of the desk, and Jonah heard boots start up the stairs behind them.
