"A legitimate - hold on." She set the cup down hard enough to slop liquor over the rim. "You want me to walk you into a company yard. Guild inspectors, foremen who owe me favours I've spent three years collecting. So you can ask for a *job*."

"The boiler in your front room's going to blow in about two weeks."

She blinked at him. "What?"

"That whistle it's doing. The high one." He tipped his head toward the curtain. "Listen."

She didn't want to. He watched her decide that refusing to listen would count as losing, which she would never allow, and they both went quiet. Through the curtain it came again, thin and reedy, almost too high to catch.

*Fweeeee.* Two seconds of it, then nothing at all, which was worse.

Jonah had heard that note before, on a cold morning at the Aldworth when the frost was still on the yard cobbles and the night shift was stamping its feet by the gate, about an hour before they carried what was left of Davey Pratt out of the furnace shed in a tarpaulin that wasn't quite big enough. Somebody had bought cheap and fitted it badly. You could always tell the ones that had been fitted in a hurry by men paid by the job, because the joints were too pretty and the brackets were an afterthought.

"That's your upper manifold venting through a hairline crack," he said. "When the crack opens, and it will, you lose the back wall. Most of the alley, too."

Mary hadn't moved. Hadn't wiped her fingers either, still wet with spilled liquor, resting on the table beside the cup like she'd forgotten they were hers.

"You walked into my shop," she said slowly, "to tell me my boiler's dying."

"I walked into your shop for a railyard contact. The boiler's free."

"Nothing's free," she said, and he could see her pricing it already, the back wall, the alley, what a guild inspector would make of either.

"That one is. The next one costs you a contact."

She studied him the way a fence studies a watch somebody swears is gold. Picked up the cup and drank what was left in it, then set it down very gently this time, square in its own wet ring.

Not a fool, then. Or not only a fool. She could work with that, probably, and she hated that she was already working out how.

"Two weeks?" she said.

"If you're lucky. If somebody stokes it hard on a cold night, less."

"And you could fix it."

"I could tell you who not to hire to fix it," said Jonah. "Then... well. Then I could fix it."

*Fweeeee.*

Mary closed her eyes. "Fuck," she said, with feeling, and reached for the bottle.
