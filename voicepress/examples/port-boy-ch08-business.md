# Chapter Eight: Business

The Kettle stank of boiled cabbage and juniper, and under that of the harbor, which got into everything in the Lowers sooner or later. Jonah ducked beneath a sign that had been a copper kettle once, before thirty winters of salt air turned it into a green lump that was more growth than pot by now. Shouldered the door open.

Light the color of weak tea leaked through windows nobody had washed since the Reclamation. A dozen drinkers hunched over a dozen cups, and not one of them looked up. In the Lowers you never looked up when a gin house door opened. Look up and you'd seen who came in, and a man who'd seen who came in could be asked about it later, by the Watch or by someone worse. The only ones who looked were the ones paid to. There were two of those by the bar, one cleaning his nails with a gutting knife, and they watched Jonah all the way to the back.

Mary had the corner table. Of course she did. Back to the wall, both doors and the bar in plain view, the sort of table she'd have paid the landlord to keep empty.

She wasn't alone.

Marks the Big filled the booth across from her the way a dropped anchor fills a rowboat. Six and a half feet of man going comfortably soft over a frame that had been built for breaking things, head shaved to stubble so the scars showed: a knife-line above one ear, a pink rumple of old burn at the back of the skull, and on the right temple a neat crescent that could only be human teeth. Jonah decided not to wonder. Gold rings, two to a hand, heavy enough to count as weapons in any Communion court. An ebony walking stick leaned against the booth with a silver head that had been weighted by somebody who knew exactly what they were about.

Markus Delaine, technically. Nobody called him that twice. He ran the south docks. Smuggling, mostly, with fencing on the side, and a loan book with interest rates Mary had once described as theological. He wasn't the biggest fish in Veranesse, and nowhere near the most dangerous. Just the one you could find.

Mary had brought Jonah to him on day four. Marks had looked him over the way a man looks over a new hammer, weighing it, and must have liked the heft, because the cargo intercept had been a Marks job. The intercept, and then the storm. And then the tent.

Jonah's stomach turned over.

A flash of memory: canvas cracking in the wind, rain hammering a foot above their faces, her breath hot on his throat and her voice gone small and fierce at the same time. *I want to be the one you think about.*

Last night. That had been last night.

She didn't look up when he reached the table. She was bent over a charcoal map on brown paper, auburn hair knotted up with the bent nail through it, the man's linen shirt sliding off one shoulder above the corset. Same as every day. As though nothing at all had happened.

Marks drew on his cigarillo until the coal glowed. *Fffhhh.* Let the smoke out through his nose in two slow grey ropes.

"Wren." His voice came up from somewhere around the cellar. "Sit."

Jonah sat. The bench complained. He spared a thought for whatever miracle of carpentry kept it standing under Marks every afternoon.

"Mary's been telling me about the Concord haul," said Marks, and tapped ash onto the floorboards. "Forty-two silvers after her cut. Not bad, for a first-timer. Means you can walk the Crescent without pissing yourself."

"Thanks."

"Wasn't a compliment. It's a qualification." Small dark eyes, sunk deep under a heavy brow, studied him without hurry. "Men who take compliments get comfortable. Comfortable men get caught."

"Then I'll try to stay miserable."

"Do. It suits you. You look like a man whose dog died, then got up and bit him."

"Close," said Jonah. "The dog's fine. It just won't look at me."

Mary's charcoal didn't so much as pause.

Marks grunted, which in a smaller man might have been a laugh, and looked from one of them to the other with no expression at all. Jonah revised his opinion. He'd had Marks down as muscle that had learned to count. Wrong way round. This was a man who counted for a living and had grown the muscle on the side, the way other men grew a paunch.

"Business," said Marks. He leaned forward, and the table groaned, and one thick finger came down on the map. "The Meridian Exchange. Hub of the northern rail. Every freight manifest, every customs declaration... and, more to the point, the payroll vault of the Communion Rail Authority."

Jonah looked. Three storeys, several wings, a rail platform down the east side. In the basement, a square marked in heavy charcoal, with two doors and a guard station drawn in front of it.

"Payroll for the whole northern network comes through once a month," Marks went on. "Gold. Not paper, not promissory notes. Coin. Forty thousand marks, give or take. It sits in that vault seventy-two hours before it goes out to the regional stations."

"You want to rob a government vault," said Jonah.

"I want to rob a government vault," Marks agreed, in the tone of a man ordering lunch. "Mary does the planning and the inside work. You do the Crescent end. The suit, the manners, walking into a room full of officials without looking like you crawled out of a bilge. I'm sourcing the rest."

"When?"

"The seventeenth. Three weeks." He glanced at Mary. Without looking up, she held up four fingers. "Your cut's four thousand."

Four thousand marks.

The foundry had paid nine coppers a day, and docked two of them for the bunk. Call it fifty marks in a good year, if a man didn't drink. Eighty years at the furnaces, then, and he'd still come up short. A house in the merchant quarter with glass in every window. Passage to anywhere the rails ran. Real papers for Sable instead of forged ones, and maybe enough left over that Mary would -

No.

Against that, set two doors and a guard station. The Rail Authority hanged thieves from their own trestles and left them for the gulls; he'd seen three of them on the walk in from the docks his first day. Call it one chance in three of the rope. One in four, with Mary planning it. One in five if she was planning it and still speaking to him.

"I'm in," he heard himself say. Hadn't finished deciding. Said it anyway. "Mary, can we talk?"

Her charcoal was tracing a thin line from the ground floor down into the basement. A ventilation shaft.

"About what?"

"About -" He glanced at Marks, who was regarding the ceiling with polite interest. "Privately."

"I'm busy."

"Mary."

"The east wing vent's too narrow for you," she said, still not looking at him. Her voice was flat and perfectly pleasant, the voice she used on landlords. "We'll want somebody smaller for the interior. Marks, the Calleni kid? The one who did the Fenwick job last spring?"

"Mary, five minutes."

"The Calleni's good," Marks rumbled, managing to ignore Jonah entirely from three feet away, which took real skill. "But he talks. On a vault job, talking is dying."

"So we gag him. Five coppers and a rag. Problem solved."

"*Mary.*"

She looked up then.

Her eyes met his, green and perfectly empty. Whatever had been in them in the tent, the hunger, the terrible honesty of *don't you break my heart*, she'd taken it all and put it somewhere he wasn't allowed to follow.

"Did you sleep?" he asked.

"The plans go back to Marks's friend in an hour," said Mary. "We can talk after."

She looked back down at the map.

Jonah sat through the next forty-five minutes with his jaw clamped so hard his back teeth ached. Guard rotations, shift changes, the rail timetable that left the east platform empty for nineteen minutes after the night mail. Mary asked about lock types, hinge pins, the thickness of the inner door, which way the hinges were hung, short clean questions, and wrote the answers in a hand so small he couldn't have read it upside down if he'd tried. He tried. She addressed every word to Marks, or to the map, or to the cigarillo smoke curling under the lamp. She didn't look at Jonah once. He might as well have been the bench.

When it was done, Marks rolled the map and slid it inside his coat, where it vanished into the black leather without a bulge. He stood, and the booth let out something close to a sigh. Dropped two coppers for his gin. Then he stopped, one hand on the silver head of his stick.

"Whatever's broken between you two," he said to nobody in particular, "it stays broken till the eighteenth. After that you can bleed on each other all you like. Before that, you bleed on my time, I take it out of your cut." He considered. "Both your cuts."

*Tap. Tap. Tap.* The stick carried him through the room, and the drinkers made room for it without once looking up.

The door shut behind him. Somebody coughed. The barman went back to smearing a glass with a rag that had been grey since the Revolution.

Mary stood. Pocketed a half-eaten roll without ceremony, slung her satchel, and walked out.

"Mary."

She kept walking.

He followed her into the street. The Lowers at dusk: purple sky above the rail trestles, the gaslamps coughing alight one after another along Tanner's Row, coal smoke and frying fish from a cart two alleys over. She turned north toward Bent Street, walking fast. Those heeled boots.

*Tak-tak-tak-tak.* He'd have known that rhythm blind.

"Mary, stop."

"I'm going home."

"You don't have a home. You sleep in four different places depending on who owes you."

"Then I'm going to one of the four. Goodnight, Jonah."

He caught up and fell in beside her. She didn't speed up. Didn't slow down either. They passed a fishmonger hosing down his slab, and a boy hawking the evening sheets, and a drunk pissing on a wall with the cheerful aim of a man who'd given up on precision hours ago.

"About the storm," he said.

"What storm?"

"The tent, Mary. You and me. Last night."

"Last night I got caught in bad weather on a job that went sideways. I was cold. Body heat was exchanged." Every word clipped and sealed, read off to him as flat as a manifest. "Operational necessity. It happens."

"That's not what happened and you know it."

"Do I?"

She swung into an alley so narrow he could have touched both walls. Stopped. Turned on him.

And the blankness cracked. Only a hairline, but he could see through it to what was underneath: the same thing he'd seen in the tent, except it had now spent a whole day being ignored, and had decided along the way that everything it ever suspected about itself was true.

"You went to Lina," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Mary -"

"This morning. You didn't go to work, and you didn't come and find me. You walked your blue balls straight down to the Burnt Rose and paid two coppers for somebody who actually knows how to suck a cock." Her voice was dead steady. Her hands weren't. They were balled at her sides, knuckles white, nails biting her palms. "Did you ask her to wear the shirt?"

He didn't answer. Couldn't think of a single thing that would help.

"Sable told you," he said finally.

"Sable said you came home smelling of two women and a brothel. I didn't need the details. I've an imagination." She laughed, short and hard, the sound of a glass going over. "My shirt. The linen one. And a wig, auburn, I'd bet my cut on it, because Lina's a professional, she's got a whole box of wigs, she'll be whoever you want for two coppers an hour. So you dressed her up as me and you fucked her face the way you couldn't fuck mine, because I *gagged*, because I didn't -"

"That's not -"

"DON'T."

It cracked off the walls. A cat shot out from behind a stack of crates. Mary's chest was heaving under the oversized shirt, and her eyes had gone bright. Mary Grieve didn't cry. Whatever this was, it burned instead of ran.

"Don't you dare stand there and tell me that's not what happened. I'm no Crescent lady and I can't take a cock down my throat like some trained courtesan, but I am not stupid, Jonah. Don't insult me by pretending I am."

He said nothing, because every word of it was true. He had dressed Lina up as her, and fucked a stand-in because Mary's mouth hadn't been enough and his patience hadn't been enough and neither had whatever passed for his self-control. Standing here in this alley with the stink of the drains and her nails in her palms, he felt something he hadn't felt since the foundry burned with every man he knew inside it.

Shame. Real shame, the heavy sort that settles in the gut like swallowed iron and won't shift.

"It was a one-time thing," Mary said. Quieter now. Banking the fire, not putting it out, pushing it down somewhere it could burn slow and only hurt her. "The tent. I was cold, you were there, it happened. Operational necessity." She squared her shoulders. Lifted her chin. "It won't happen again."

"Mary -"

"It *won't happen again*." Each word set down like a brick. He could watch the wall going back up, course by course. "We work together. Marks has a job, and it's a big job, and the money's real, and I am not letting whatever this was..." a sharp flick of the hand, between his chest and hers, "...cost me four thousand marks. So we're professionals. Partners. What happened in the tent stays in the tent."

"And if I don't want it to stay there?"

"Then you should have come to me this morning, instead of walking to a whorehouse." Her voice broke on the last word. Only for a breath, and then the crack sealed over. "Forget it."

She walked out of the alley. *Tak-tak-tak-tak.* Narrow shoulders squared, chin up, disappearing into the gaslit dusk the way she always walked, as though she had somewhere to be and you weren't invited.

Jonah stayed where he was. The cat crept back onto its crates. Overhead, a rail-coach clattered over the trestles trailing white steam across the purple.

*Then you should have come to me.*

She was right. She was always right, and he hated her for it, and hated it worse tonight because the rightness of it sat on his chest, heavy as a slab of pig iron. And the worst of it, the part that made the shame climb from his gut into his throat, was that even now, with her words still bouncing around the bricks, some low and stubborn part of him was already wondering when he'd next see Jessamine.

He walked home. The Lowers took him the way they took everything, without interest, without judgment, without the faintest sign that the city cared about one more man who'd fucked up the only good thing to happen to him since he came down a gangplank with eleven coppers and no plan.

* * *

Sable was awake when he got in, cross-legged beside the door, eating an apple. Where she kept finding apples he'd no idea. Her amber eyes followed him across the room to the mattress, and kept following when he fell on it.

"The angry one came back," she said. "While you were at the gin house. Stood outside for six minutes this time. Then she left."

"Six minutes."

"I counted. I count everything." Her ears tipped forward, the curious angle rather than the cross one. "She smelled of salt. The kind from eyes. Not the sea."

He lay very still.

Six minutes, outside his door, before she'd walked into the Kettle and looked at him as though he were furniture. He turned it over and couldn't make it fit, and then it fit perfectly, and that was worse.

"Go to sleep, Sable."

"It's barely dusk." She licked apple juice off her thumb. "I've only just woken up properly."

"Then be quiet."

"Physiologically impossible. I'm part cat. We're crepuscular. Dusk is when we're *most* awake."

He put the pillow over his face. It smelled of straw and tallow and, very faintly, of cedar, which was Mary, because she'd lain right here the night she planned the ball job and the pillow had never let go of her.

*Crunch.*

"You're terrible at this," said Sable, with her mouth full. "Women. Choices. Being a person in general. Really impressively terrible."

He didn't argue. Lay under the pillow breathing cedar and listening to her chew, and somewhere north of Bent Street, in one of four borrowed beds, Mary Grieve was not crying. He'd have bet his whole four thousand on it, and he'd have lost.
