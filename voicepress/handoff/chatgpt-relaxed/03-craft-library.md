# Craft Library

General fiction craft the writer and the story editor use for every voice. The voice bible says
how THIS author sounds; this says how scenes, chapters and people work. Where they conflict, the
voice bible wins on sentences and this wins on structure. Every rule here is a strong default, not
a law: great writers break them on purpose, once, for effect. Breaking them by accident is the fault.

---

## 1. Scene architecture

- **Every scene changes something.** A value turns: safe to endangered, trusted to suspected, secret
  to known. If nothing has turned by the end, cut or merge the scene.
- **Scene and sequel.** An action scene runs *goal, conflict, disaster*. Its aftermath (the sequel)
  runs *reaction, dilemma, decision*, and the decision is the next scene's goal. Chapters alternate
  between them, which gives the book its breathing rhythm.
- **Try-fail cycles.** Outcomes are rarely "yes". Prefer **"yes, but"** (he gets the seal, but the
  guard saw his face) and **"no, and"** (he doesn't get it, and now the vault is double-locked). A
  plain "no" stalls; a plain "yes" deflates. Real victory comes late and costs something.
- **Arrive late, leave early.** Start a scene after the throat-clearing and leave before the
  wrap-up. If the first paragraph is someone waking, travelling or arriving, ask what happens next
  and start there.
- **The plan rule.** If a plan is shown in full on the page, it must go wrong. If it will go right,
  hide it, so the reader watches it unfold as a surprise. (Weeks's dock job is the first kind.)

## 2. Promises, progress, payoff

- The opening of a book, chapter or scene makes promises about genre, tone and the problem. Keep them,
  or break them deliberately and visibly.
- **Progress** has to be felt: a ledger of clues found, allies won, wounds taken, days left.
- **Plant early, pay late.** Anything that will matter at the climax (an object, skill, weakness or
  rule of the magic) appears earlier, doing an ordinary job. Chekhov's gun can hang on the wall as a
  coat hook in act one.
- Foreshadow through practical detail, not ominous narration. "The paperweight Byron was never allowed
  to pick up" beats "little did he know".

## 3. Suspense, mystery and surprise

- **Mystery:** the character and the reader both lack information. It runs on curiosity, and works.
- **Suspense:** the reader knows something the character doesn't (the bomb under the table). It runs
  on dread, and it's the strongest of the three. Use the second POV, the overheard plan, or the figure
  behind someone.
- **Surprise:** nobody knew. It gives one jolt, then it's spent. Earn it by making it inevitable in
  hindsight.
- Most great scenes run suspense for the length of the scene and spend one surprise at the end.

## 4. Stakes

- **Personal before public.** "The city will fall" means little until it's "and Kaldrosa will be sold
  to the man who blacked her eye".
- **Specific beats large:** a crown for a black eye, or four hundred units for trials.
- Stakes rise in steps through the book: money, then safety, then a person, then identity, then the
  world. Never top out early.
