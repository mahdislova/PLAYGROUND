# Voice A: the author's own pages

<!-- OWN-07: Waiting, ambush, combat with Reaver summoning, capture -->

Hugh sat within the blind, wrestling with doubt and frustration. One week they'd been waiting, watching, with no success. No sign of interest from the fae, not even a hint of their having noticed the setup.

A tent was erected in a goblet canyon a mile up the slope of the mountain. It ostensibly served as the base of operations for a small scouting party led by Hogweed, which would loop around the forest before returning each night to rest. As camping spots went it was a good one; the small canyon, really a glorified gulley, hid the fire from sight, and protected the camp from direct assault on three sides.

The scouts, however, made sure to leave trails leading back to the camp each day, and spent their evenings and nights watched by an entire regiment of bandits led by a Poison Sister and Hugh, who'd created a series of blinds and nooks in the rocky canyon walls from which to watch.

The plan was simple. The scouts would draw the fae to this lonely outpost, the fae would attack, and the regiment would spring its counter-ambush.

Simple.

Yet nothing had happened for a week. Seven days during which Hugh had replayed every facet of their plan over and over again. Had the fae noticed the hidden men and women? Had they scented them, perhaps, or noticed them filtering down to the main camp and back every couple of days?

They'd used Zarja's advice to best effect: the fae, apparently, were creatures of dawn and dusk, and the least likely to be encountered or exploring at midday, which was when they'd effected their movements.

But who was to say the fae hadn't noticed their tracks? Weren't aware of their ploy, and were planning an even bigger ambush to kill all of them at once?

Zarja had counseled them to not overthink the matter. "My people are not fond of careful planning or structured patrols. They go where they will, when they will, and should they discover something of interest, they investigate on the spot. Few are wise enough or display sufficient restraint to think matters through. If they come across your tracks, they will follow them. If they espy your camp, they will attack. They are as elemental as they are dangerous, as prone to impulsive action as they are mercurial in their favors. You're not laying a trap for trained human soldiers. Remember that."

And yet.

Seven days in, and nothing.

Hugh shifted his weight. He was wedged behind a screen of rubble and gray canvas through which he could burst with ease, but behind which he lurked in increasing discomfort and cold. The rocky walls of the crack pressed in around him on all sides, and boredom and frustration were his only companions.

How much longer would they wait before trying another approach? Another week? A day?

Dusk had fallen, the sun having dipped behind the far western peaks, and the goblet canyon was a morass of gloom but for the small fire burning outside the sole tent. The 'scouts' had returned but half an hour ago and now sat around the flames, eating their meal, fighting to appear relaxed but flinching at every sound. The trees that loomed over the walls of the canyon were dark and impenetrable, menacing in their somber solidity. As if the very woods were on the side of the fae.

Hugh shifted and fought the urge to shiver. Peered through the crack in his blind. He and the regiment were hidden at the back of the goblet, the dead end; on the far side of the camp from him the canyon ran forth between two narrow walls, growing ever more shallow as it rose to finally merge with the mountain slope, its length choked with fallen rocks and toppled trees.

Not an ideal approach, even for fae. But perhaps it would serve as an emergency escape route when the trap was sprung.

Or so he hoped.

Wait - was that - ? Hugh froze, peered up at the lip of the canyon, where a sharp boulder he'd taken to calling 'the Nose' extended out a yard into the air. Movement?

Holding his breath, Hugh studied the Nose, the rocks and rubble around its flanks, the shadows at the base of the trees that leaned out in their desperate bid to capture the sunlight that fell upon the canyon during the day.

No. Hugh swallowed his disappointment. Nothing. Just his imagination, perhaps the flickering leap of shadows cast by the fire below -

Wait.

There.

Hugh lowered his hand to the hilt of his blade. A slender figure had detached itself from the gloomy depths and moved sufficiently forward to peer down at the tent. Wild vines were wreathed in the fae's hair, his skin daubed with swirling dark patterns, a slender, serpentine blade clutched in his hand.

Hugh's heart began to hammer. A scout? The first of an attack party? The fae was utterly silent, hard to keep track of even though Hugh was staring right at him. Still as a branch, inhuman in his poise and the fixity of his attention on the scouts below.

Hugh didn't even dare breathe a whisper to himself. Watched, locked in place, until the fae raised a hand and twitched two fingers, as if summoning others forward.

Yes.

A half-dozen shadows resolved themselves into other attackers, each distinct from the other, some with horns, one with a head of feathers instead of hair, some with bestial features, others inhumanly beautiful. All, however, were adorned with the swirling marks that Zarja had explained indicated they were on the war path, and all clutched sylvan weapons in their fists.

Six or seven of their foes, staring down at the tent with eyes that glittered with hate.

Hugh slowed his breathing. Calmed the storm in his center, and reached out to the slumbering spirits in his soul: Acipa, he called, summoning one of the Reaver lieutenants he'd not reached for often, a woman who'd intimidated him with her focus, efficiency, intuitive wisdom and deep sense of the truth. Akilina, her brash counterpart, forceful and inconstant, disdainful of rules and eager for the fight. Foughtash for his strength and ferocity, and Evassier for his precision and talent with blades.

The four Reavers slipped into his body, filling him with feverish strength and might, making the world seem to move a fraction slower, his weapon all the more familiar in his grip.

Now he was ready.

The lead of the fae party still had his fist raised, crouched as he was by the Nose, and then dropped his hand to point at the tent and the seven fae leaped over the chasm's edge to fall upon the scouts.

"Now!" roared Hugh, bursting forth from his blind, tearing through the canvas as he threw himself forward to meet the attack.

The scouts seated by the campfire scrabbled back in panic, drawing their weapons, even as the walls of the goblet canyon exploded in a mess of ruined screens and rubble, twenty or so warriors bursting forth with bows raised, arrows already nocked.

The fae realized their predicament even before they hit the ground. Hugh saw panic and fury in their leader's eyes, heard him cry out a command in their unintelligible language, and the attackers turned to flee, not even bothering to throw weapons at their targets.

Turned to race down the canyon's choked length, the sole boulder and log-filled chute that led out to the mountainside, whose obstacles should have proven impossible for the clumsy humans to cross and give chase - but not so for them.

Their leader leaped, soaring forward over the first rocks into the scree of slender branches that crisscrossed the escape route - and crashed back off the wiry shoots as if he'd run straight into a marble wall.

The others encountered equal difficulty. Half of them made it through, smashing their way through branches and tendrils of ivy that were unnaturally rigid, but two others rebounded in like manner.

Shouts of excitement, bandits racing forward, and the three fallen fae leaped to their feet and turned, teeth bared as they met the onslaught.

Their leader hurled a handful of slender darts at the oncoming bandits, dropping three of them, and shouted in his incomprehensible language; one of the fae, a woman with curling ram's horns, ducked her head and charged right at Hugh who was leading the attack even as the third - his head filled with feathers instead of hair - hurled twin blades blurring toward Hugh's neck.

Who only laughed, a gladness, a madness, tearing through him like a flash fire. With a sweep of his blade he knocked the two daggers out of the sky and with his free hand caught hold of the curling ram's horn of the charging girl, sidestepping her so that she rushed past him, only to dig in his heels and haul back, tearing her head back, arching her back, her momentum carrying her up into the air, so that for a moment she hung, suspended, two yards above the ground, before he slammed her down onto the dirt.

The bandits' own charge had faltered, and now they stepped back, giving Hugh room as he turned to face the other two fae, his blade spinning lazily in his off hand, a dangerous smile playing across his face.

The feathered man and the leader stared at him, reflections of the camp fire burning deep in their alien eyes, their faces almost human, their movements unnaturally smooth, their breath coming in deep, terrified jerks.

"Come on," said Hugh, raising his hand to beckon at them with two fingers. "The only way out of here is through me."

The horned girl was stirring at his feet, curling onto her side, the breath driven explosively out of her lungs. Not a problem for a few moments more.

The feathered man let out a scream of rage, the cry of a hawk descending into its plunge, and leaped straight up, even as the leader whipped both hands forward, unleashing a storm of daggers he couldn't possibly have been holding.

Time slowed. Hugh spun to the side, pivoting on the ball of his foot, bringing his blade down in an oblique slash, cutting six of the knives out of the air. The metallic impacts rang out, and then the hawk fae came rushing down on the diagonal, his taloned feet straining for Hugh's flesh, shooting down like a flung spear, faster than thought.

Hugh braced himself, took the strike full on even as pain blossomed in his side from the few daggers that had slipped past his guard. Talons latched onto his shoulder, dug into his chest, the fae's impact as tremendous as that of a falling boulder.

Hugh grunted and gave no ground, flexed his knees to take the assault, and stopped the fae cold.

Who remained latched onto him, standing atop Hugh as a mountain climber might a perilous crag, his eyes wide in shock, clearly having expected to drive his prey crashing into the ground.

The fae leader leaped forward, sinuous as an eel, twin curved blades in hand now, swirling so that he was a dervish of sinuous steel.

This was a language Hugh could understand.

Ignoring the pain that tore into his shoulder and the breadth of his chest from the fae's talons, fighting even as the man remained poised above him, Hugh engaged the leader, laughing as he stepped into the fae's storm.

He could have summoned more Reavers. Could have overwhelmed his opposition with his might, become so powerful they'd have been as gnats before the breath of a furnace, but no - he wanted to push himself, to bleed, to give release to the tension and frustration of the past week.

To bleed, and bleed others in turn.

The fae leader would have cut him down in moments if Hugh had faced him alone, so preternaturally skilled was he.

But Hugh didn't fight alone. Acipa, Akilina, and Evassier gave him speed. Foughtash gave him might. And together, they were the fae's match; Hugh's longsword met the fae's every darting stab, turned every flickering flash, broke apart the leader's assault, tore the tapestry of his sword work into shreds, and when an opening appeared he slammed the flat of his blade against the fae's head, sending him reeling.

The feathered fae bunched his legs, leaped off Hugh, relinquishing his cruel grasp, would have leaped free, but Hugh surged up after him, caught him by a scaled ankle.

Yanked him back down, eliciting a squawk of surprise from the man, and when he was level Hugh hammered a punch into the man's face that broke bone and drove the fae crashing back into the dirt at the leader's feet.

An avalanche slammed into Hugh from behind, bending him backward, drove him six yards forward in the blink of the eye, and hurled him straight into the cliff face.

Hugh turned his shoulder at the last moment, impacted, brought down a shelf of rock, crashed to one knee.

His spine should have shattered from that blow.

Almost had.

Grimacing, he raised his head, rubble and dust cascading down his shoulders, and saw the horned fae panting, half crouched, just before him, her eyes sparking fire and defiance.

By Fortuna she was beautiful in her rage and hatred.

Her ram's horns sprouted directly from her forehead, an inch above each upswept eyebrow, ribbed and curling almost a foot wide on either side. Her nose was flattened, her ears like those of a faun, but she was young, or seemed so - if she'd been human, Hugh would have pegged her as no older than eighteen. For despite the might of her horns, there was a delicacy to her features, to her pert lips, her slender jaw, that belied the strength of her attack.

Hugh rose to his feet, a few more rocks rolling off his shoulders. "Ow," he said.

Her eyes flared open wide in shock. Subtle paint made her hazel eyes all the more dramatic, flaring wings of yellow, burgundy, and light purple that swept up under her brow.

She wore a delicate necklace of what looked like cowrie shells around her neck, and Hugh saw her pulse racing there, her chest rising and falling in despair.

And in the face of her fear his joy in the battle burned away, leaving ashes in his mouth.

This was no dread foe for him to crush. This was but a young woman, no matter her shape or form, and the fear in her eyes was all too real.

The fae leader had regained his wits. He cried out a command, voice as piercing as the scream of a dying animal, and turned to dive into the boulder-choked passage, ducking under the stiff foliage that had rebuffed him before.

The feathered fae leaped high and then executed his oblique spearing down, clearing the foliage in like manner, to disappear into the shadows beyond.

The ram girl hesitated, eyes darting from Hugh to the wall of bandits that faced her, then betrayed her intent with a subtle flexing of her knees.

Hugh was on her before she could do more than turn and begin her flight.

One arm wrapped around her slender neck, the other around her slender waist, lifting her clear off the ground before she could flee.

"Irons!" roared Hugh as she screamed and bucked her head back, twisting it from side to side in a bid to crash her horns into his face. "Irons!"

Men came running up, seized her wrists and clamped manacles about them. The fae screamed at the iron's touch, and when the collar was closed about her neck, all the strength fled her frame, so that she fell limp in Hugh's embrace, writhing in helpless agony.

<!-- OWN-08: Aftermath, mercy, the prisoner, the march down, a second escape attempt -->

"Well done, milord," said Jarvis, one of the scouts who'd evinced enough nerve to play bait over the course of this long week.

Hogweed stood to the side, her face twisted into pity and disgust.

A pity and disgust that was mirrored in Hugh's heart. The ram girl was mewling in distress, twisting and turning gently in his arms as if clutched by a nightmare.

For long, uncertain moments he simply held her, and then he crouched and set her down on the ground.

"Back," he commanded, and the ring of torches and curious flames retreated a few paces.

"Back!" snapped Hugh, glaring at the bandits, who retreated further, finally clearing the area.

"Can you hear me?" Hugh stared down into the ram girl's face. Her painted eyelids were flickering, her eyes rolling, trying to turn her head from side to side but prevented from doing so by her horns.

"Damnit," he hissed. "Hogweed. A rope."

To her credit, the Poison Sister didn't question or simply repeat his words back to him, but nodded and ducked into the scout's tent, emerging a second later with a coil of tarred, slender cord.

"Fashion it into a slipknot," said Hugh. "Hurry."

Welts were forming around the ram girl's wrists and about her neck, as if the metal were actually burning her.

Hogweed's fingers moved rapidly, and she extended a noose to Hugh. Who slipped it around the ram girl's neck, tightened it, and then moved his fingers to her iron collar.

"Wait," said Jarvis, voice taut with panic. "If you release her, won't her, you know, her powers… how can we be sure…?"

Hugh fixed the man with a glare that drove him back a few paces. Pointed at the moaning girl. "This isn't who we are, no matter what war we wage."

And so saying, he drew the pin from the collar, pulled it free, and cast it aside. A moment later the manacles followed suit.

The fae's face contorted into a deep frown, but she ceased her moaning. A moment later her eyelids flickered, and then her hazel eyes locked on Hugh who knelt beside her.

She froze, like a deer startled by a hunter stepping out from behind a tree.

Hugh spread his hands wide. "No iron. I didn't know the effect it would have. I'm sorry for using it."

Her faun's ears twitched, flicked, rotated, but she didn't otherwise move, her entire frame betraying terrible tension.

"I'm going to use this rope instead," said Hugh, lifting the cord. "Because you're still technically our prisoner. We're taking you down to Erro. I promise on my honor to not harm you if you don't seek to escape."

"You have no honor," spat the girl, her voice surprisingly rich and mellifluous.

"I don't need to convince you of that. I'll let my actions speak for me. Will you promise to not try to escape if I only bind you with rope?"

"No," she said, shifting now to sit, turning her head from side to side as she examined the bandits who ringed them both.

Hugh sighed. "Really? You want me to place the manacles and collar back on?"

She darted a glance at where the iron lay, and looked away as if the very sight seared her eyes.

"Never mind. Even without your word I'll not put those on you. But I promise you this: try to escape, and I will hunt you down." His words rang with grim certainty. "You cannot escape me, no matter how fleet-footed you think you are."

She turned back to him, the authority in his words capturing and holding her attention. Her ear flicked again, but her brows lowered. "What are you?"

"The name's Hugh of Stasiek." He rose to his feet, back twinging in pain from where she'd blasted him. Blood had caused his tunic to cling to his chest. The thrown knives had fallen free, and had fortunately been small, but pain yet radiated from where they'd stabbed into his hip, his thigh, his side.

"My lord?" Hogweed. "You maybe want those bandaged before we set off?"

Hugh considered it. "No. I want us gone before the fae return with reinforcements. The sooner the better. Good work, everybody. Hogweed, let's quit this camp and take everything of value with us."

The Poison Sister took control, her commands sharp and on point, and in moments men were collapsing the tent, dousing the fire, collecting supplies, climbing out of the goblet canyon, bows at the ready.

"Your name?" asked Hugh, staring down at the ram girl.

She returned his gaze with a defiance that couldn't quite mask her fear.

"Very well," said Hugh. "We'll go with Goat Girl for now."

Anger flickered across her features, her flattened nose wrinkling in distaste, and he thought for a second she'd contradict him, her pride forcing her to give up her true name, but instead she looked sharply away.

"Up," he said. "I don't want to, but I can drag you all the way down the mountain if you insist."

Reluctantly, she rose to her feet. She was of medium height, just a little taller than Morwyn, but her willowy frame was impossibly feminine; he could have encircled her waist with both hands, but her bosom was large, her hips wide. Her crimson hair fell down past her shoulders nearly to her waist, and only then did he realize he was staring; the ram girl was all too aware, her expression having hardened, and with an apologetic grimace Hugh looked away.

Their flight down the mountain was uninterrupted; they made good time, the ram girl clearly contemplating escape the whole way but unable to formulate a plan, not with Hugh walking behind her and Hogweed's men encircling them.

It was just shy of midnight when they broke free of the trees and Erro came into view, the Mandroga a pewter line beneath the waning moon, the houses dark but for the lit windows of the tavern.

Hugh looked to where Hogweed marched just ahead. "Who's on patrol?"

"Snowdrop."

"Her men better intercept us," he said. "We're already closer than -"

"My lord." A slender figure stepped out of the shadows beside an outcropping of rock, her bow held down by her side.

"Ah. Snowdrop." Hugh gave her a wolfish grin. "I was just complimenting your vigilance."

The young Poison Sister inclined her head with obvious wry amusement, but her gaze was fixed on the ram girl. "Success at last?"

"We're about to find out. I'm going to send out another patrol to reinforce yours. Expect retribution."

"Very well." She stepped back. "I'll pass word on to the men."

The closer they drew to Erro the more frantic the ram girl became. She began stalling, forcing Hugh to push her forward, sending her stumbling down the path, her feet seeming to have turned to blocks of wood. When they were close enough to hear the rushing whisper of the falls, she let out a pained cry and threw herself to the side, charging toward the path's embankment.

Hugh cursed and pulled back on the rope; he'd forgotten just how powerful her charge could be, and was dragged forward, heels skidding across the rocky path as she lowered her head to bull her way up the side of the bank.

Hogweed's men cried out in alarm, and more than one raised a nocked arrow in her direction.

Foughtash yet rode within Hugh, along with three other Reavers, but it wasn't enough; terror gave the girl unnatural strength, and Hugh staggered and slid after her, unable to arrest her flight.

For a moment he considered summoning others, but then in a flash he leaned into the skills of those who already rode him; calling on Evassier and Akilina's dexterity, he ceased hauling back and sprang forward instead, leaping onto the girl's back, wrapping an arm about her neck once more, and bore her to the ground.

She screamed in frustration and horror, reared up onto her knees, but Hugh regained his balance and hauled back, holding her tight, arching her back like a bow.

"Stop!" he hissed into her ear as she clawed at his forearm, her crimson hair in disarray, her head twisting from side to side once more in an effort to gouge him. "You cannot escape me, stop!"

She gave one last, heart-rending cry of desolate fear, and then sagged in his arms, the strength seeping out of her.

"On my honor," panted Hugh, holding her tight against him. "On my honor, you will not be harmed." He didn't even know what he was saying, the words tumbling free from his lips as if on their own accord. "You won't be harmed. You won't be tortured. I swear it."

But for her trembling she remained still, her back yet arched, her one horn pressed into his bloody shoulder, her hands at his forearm, her chest rising and falling as she panted.

Hugh relaxed his grip slowly. "I swear it," he whispered, allowing his arm to fall away. "I swear it."

She curled forwards, as if bowing to the distant forest, and her shoulders shook with silent tears. Hugh rose to his feet and stared down at her, feeling monstrous, but unable to interrupt her grief. Watched and waited as she shuddered and wept, the bandits and Hogweed watching in wonder and silence, until at last the girl mastered herself and rose, wiping an arm across her eyes.

"Very well," she whispered. "Let us see what your honor is worth, Hugh of Stasiek."

Throat tight, Hugh nodded. What use more words? Hogweed signaled, her men resumed their descent, and their group marched the remaining distance to Erro proper.

"Send word to whomever is in camp to go on patrol with Snowdrop," he commanded Hogweed as they drew close to his estate. "And put the rest of the camp on alert. I want two patrols in Erro proper, one on the rooftops, the second watching the entrances, both southern and north. We're to remain on war footing till well past dawn."

"Yes, my lord," said Hogweed, voice curt. She turned to leave, taking a branching path away from his own toward the bandit camp, but stopped when he called to her.

"And Hogweed?"

She looked back, suspicious.

"Well done."

If anything, his praise seemed to deepen her suspicion, so that with a dubious nod she led her men away to Floriana's camp.

"This way," he said to the ram girl, leading her along the path to the imperial estate.

Light shone out from the cracks between the shutters. Hugh reached the entrance, and paused. Took a deep breath, glanced at the quiescent fae, and then pushed the door open and entered.

<!-- OWN-09: Ensemble dialogue, the earned speech, the litany, the knife at the throat, the cliffhanger -->

"Hugh!" said Zarja, looking up from where she was reading a book on the dining table, a candle set close by. "You're -"

Morwyn sat in another chair, leaning back on the two rear legs, carefully rebinding the hilt of her blade. Anastasia was in the open kitchen, in the act of pouring steaming water into a teapot.

All three froze as the ram girl entered behind Hugh, her horns nearly brushing the doorframe on either side.

"- back," finished Zarja, rising to her feet, dropping her Elena guise immediately to appear in her lisica form, her golden hair rising into curls, her ears slipping forth, her eyes gleaming as if with their own inner fire.

The ram girl seemed no less shocked to see her, eyes widening, ears going rigid, her whole frame tensing in surprise.

For a moment neither spoke, and their silence bound everyone else's lips. Then the ram girl said a phrase in the native fae tongue, her voice betraying confusion and query.

Zarja responded, tone guarded.

Her words hit the ram girl like a fist, causing her to actually step back. She fired back a question, her tone rife with incredulity.

Zarja spread her hands, expression solemn, dignified, and responded with calm and certainty.

Silence, and then the ram girl spat on the floor.

"Well, that seems to have gone well," said Morwyn.

Sorrow flickered across Zarja's face, but it never touched the resolve in her eyes. "It's hard to explain," she said, speaking still to the ram girl. "But I sense the hand of fate behind Hugh. Where he goes, the world will change and our people shall be changed with it. I have chosen to stand by his side in the hopes of helping him understand our people, and in doing so ameliorate the cruelty of our fate."

The ram girl laughed, the sound derisive and mocking. "I see. So your influence leads to my being captured with ropes instead of iron. You change the decorations, but not the outcome."

"No," said Hugh, dropping the rope and moving to the closest bottle of wine. "Her influence leads me to capturing you instead of simply leading five hundred men armed with fire and iron into the mountains to slay every living fae."

The ram girl shuddered, darted a look at Zarja, who gave her a grim nod.

"You wish to make another traitor of me," spat the girl. "For me to betray my people so you can march up there with not only fire and iron, but with information to make your battle all the easier."

"I don't want you to betray your people," said Hugh, pulling the cork from the wine bottle with his teeth and spitting it aside. "I want you to betray those corrupted by Arasim. Your people, the true fae, I wish…"

He paused. Foughtash and the others yet lurked within him. Could they hear his words? How close could he come to saying what was in his heart without Jacinia learning of his intent?

Without giving her sufficient cause to pluck his life and cast him into the Ashen Gardens along with the other gladsome Reavers?

"…yes?" prompted the ram girl.

"To be more judicious," he allowed at last.

The ram girl sneered. "Judicious. A fine word that does not mask your true intent."

Hugh filled a goblet, drained it, refilled it.

"Be easier to just drink from the bottle," said Morwyn.

"That would be uncouth."

Anastasia moved forward, a steaming cup in hand. "Please, for better or worse, you are our prisoner for now. Sit. Have some tea."

The ram girl tossed her head proudly, sweeping her hair back from her shoulders. "I may be your prisoner, but I will never act as your guest."

"Fine," said Hugh. "So let us get to it. I swore to you on my honor you would not be hurt. I will hold to that pledge. Come dawn, if you desire it, I will set you free. This I also swear on the love Fortuna has for me."

"Free?" asked Morwyn.

"Free," said Hugh firmly. "I'll kill anyone who moves to harm me and mine, strike them down in combat, but this? No. My memories of how I was treated by Katharzina are too fresh. I'll not torture or imprison."

"Hard to argue with that," said Morwyn, and resumed winding leather around the bare wood of the hilt. "Though it's probably the least effective start to an interrogation I've ever heard."

Anastasia set the cup of tea before an empty chair. "My name is Anastasia, a disciplus of Duke Annaro and currently in service to Lord Hugh. This is Morwyn, the captain of the duke's personal guard, and this is Zarja, our friend and companion."

"Zarja Lythgraefen el-Abeian," said the lisica softly.

The ram girl stared down her flattened nose at them all. "I've no interest in your names. To me you are merely the enemy."

"That so." Hugh sat on the edge of the table, placing his boot on the closest chair, leaning forward with one elbow on a knee. "Interesting. So you're all for demon worship, for perverting the true nature of the fae, for doing whatever it takes to bring a new war upon humanity, even if it means drowning the world in fire and blood so that everyone loses in the end?"

"Humanity must perish!" The ram girl's voice rang out, fierce and proud. "This village, this valley, was once my home! Before you split the trees, polluted the streams, before you tore the stones from the earth with which to build your barrows, we fae lived here, harming nobody, cherishing the wild, nurturing beast and bird! This valley, and the valley beside it, the rolling lowlands, the plains beyond! This world was ours, and wherever the eye fell one saw marvels, one saw beauty, one saw harmony as nature flourished under our stewardship! Now?"

Hugh sipped from his wine, not bothering to answer her clearly rhetorical comment.

"Now? The land is covered in wheat and barley, a single crop that has supplanted the abundance that once grew everywhere. Forests are leveled, animals slaughtered, richness replaced by an endless herd of cattle to feed your hunger! The sky chokes with smoke, your cities reek of filth and excrement, and you - you don't even live in harmony with one another! All you desire is power and money, and never are you sated, never your ambitions quenched! You tread on each other in your vaulting quest for ever more, more than you can consume in your short lives, sitting your barrows of stone, hoarding glittering gold like petty dragons, glorying in the misery of your fellow humans as they groan and die of disease in the streets!"

Her eyes flashed, her chest heaved, and she lashed all of them with her glare.

"Your world is one of filth and death, blood and slaughter, greed and hatred! In three short centuries you have perverted our land beyond measure, and worse! You have driven us from our ancestral homes, have thrown us into ever remote corners of the world, yet even that is insufficient! It is not enough that we live out of your sight, but you must hunt us down, you must kill us, and why?"

Her hands were clenched, and now pain cracked through her fury. "Why do you do this? Even today, long after our bravest warriors are dead, after our cruelest leaders killed, when all that remains of our people wish for nothing but peace and to try and scrape by on the scraps you have left us, why do you still hunt us? Do we offend you so with our very existence? Can you not sleep atop your beds of gold in peace, knowing that out there, somewhere, the rightful owners of this land yet draw breath?"

Hugh finished his cup of wine, but the power of her words stayed his hand. He made no move to refill it, nor did Morwyn work on her hilt. Everyone listened, mesmerized.

"I know why. We know why," said the ram girl, looking to Zarja with stony anger. "Because we are the embodiment of your guilt. You cannot enjoy your stolen goods in peace as long as we live. You must kill us off, even the most gentle of our kind, the most innocent, so that you need no longer be reminded of your crimes. Only then will you be able to sink into your perfidy without any pangs of conscience. Only then will you be able to smile at your bloody hands without horror. Only when every fae lies dead will you be able to lie to yourselves and say this land was always yours, its riches always yours to reap, and never need fear being contradicted."

Hugh pursed his lips and nodded thoughtfully, finally refilling his goblet. "You're right."

The ram girl's face, which had grown flush with the passion of her words, turned pale. "You admit it, then? You've so little shame?"

"I do." Hugh sipped from the wine, not tasting anything but ashes in his mouth. "I've known that blind hatred myself. Blind, I say, because when I killed your kind it was because I was taught that this was right, this was good, and the world would be a safer place without you."

The ram girl went to interject but Hugh spoke on.

"But I no longer feel that hate. I've parted company with those who do. But I still understand that hatred, and disagree with it. I will have no part in it. As I said before, my quarrel here is not with fae such as yourself. It is with those Vispathia has led into the shadow of Arasim. Those who have taken demon taint into their spirits so as to be able to strike back against my people."

The ram girl sneered. "I am to believe this?"

"Yes." And though the Reavers yet rode within him, Hugh felt a weariness descend upon him that had nothing to do with his physical labors. "I can't claim to understand your pain. No human can. But I am a man of no small power. I have given you my word that you shall go free come dawn. One way or another I will kill the demon-tainted, and then, Erro secured, I shall return to Stasiek to confront my brother. And I shall demand that he lift the ban on fae who reside in these valleys, allowing them to return to their homes if they are willing to share the land with the people who already live here."

"Hugh!" Anastasia's shock was palpable. "You can't be serious."

"The Fate Makers will have your head," said Morwyn quietly. "And your brother's, if he defies them."

"They can try and take it," said Hugh. "I'm not an easy man to stop."

"You cannot defy the empire's entire religion," said Anastasia, speaking slowly with shocked wonder as if she were trying to wrap her mind around what he was proposing. "Every baron, every duke, the imperial army itself, would march upon Stasiek."

"Then we'll die," said Hugh. "It's no great thing, death. I may die for saying these words alone. But I grow tired."

"Tired?" asked Anastasia, voice rich with disbelief once more.

"Tired of committing crimes simply because I'm ordered to do so."

Anastasia ran her hands through her hair, disrupting her chignon without care. "And so you will set yourself against the Fate Makers? The Emperor? The Disciplus Academy - all of Mendev will rise."

"Then let them."

"You can't be serious," said Morwyn, voice flat.

"I am." Hugh drained his goblet, tossed it aside, rose and strode to the shuttered window. Turned. "Because the more time I spend in your company - all of you, the three of you - the more I come to value integrity. Laugh if you will. I care not. But Zarja is teaching me to look beyond appearances. Anastasia, you are teaching me the value of independence from the institutions that have molded my life. And Morwyn, from you I am learning how to be true to myself, no matter what that self may look like. How can I take those lessons, the love you have given me, and return to life in the empire after this, with all its blind cruelties and stupidities?"

Nobody spoke.

"The answer is, I can't. Nor will I live in the wild like a bandit. Nor can I simply walk away, as I once planned, to explore the boundaries of the maps, in so doing abdicate my responsibilities to this empire, to its peoples, to the crimes I myself committed. No. I will defy the heavens themselves if I must, but I'll put a stop to the injustices that rule the day, and if I die for it, then - well. I die. But I will die myself, Hugh of Stasiek, a man who was worthy, if only for a few short years, of your love."

Zarja's smile was tremulous as she clasped her hands together over her heart. Anastasia opened her mouth and then snapped it shut again. Morwyn was frowning at him as if he were a problem she couldn't solve with the edge of her blade.

"Think on it. None of us can return to life as it was. Anastasia. You and I have concocted plans to escape, to escape the Academy, to escape Mendev itself. Morwyn, can you honestly say you can return to Annaro's service as his captain, and content yourself to a life of executing his commands with bloodless efficiency?"

Nobody spoke. Anastasia chewed her bottom lip and looked away. Morwyn continued to stare darkly at him.

"So why not? Why not plant our flag and defy the world to come crush us? We'll die, I know we will, but what of it? I'd rather die for a cause I believe in, die beside the women I love, than bend knee to corruption, greed, everything Goat Girl here just spoke of. Because everything she said about humanity was correct, or close enough, and I'll have no part of it any longer."

"Oreanna," said the ram girl, voice soft.

Hugh stared blankly at her.

"Oreanna," she said again, then blushed. "Not Goat Girl."

"Oreanna, then, thank you." Hugh took up the near empty bottle of wine, went to drain it, but turned to Morwyn and Anastasia instead. "Well? What say you?"

"Death," said Morwyn, voice sepulchral. "It's a guarantee. We've no chance of surviving this madness. Our bodies will be feeding the crows before the year is out."

"Agreed," said Anastasia. "I can attest firsthand how the Academy will react. You're asking us to die, Hugh."

"Fine. Die with me." He felt wild, liberated, uncontainable. "But for however long we live, we four shall burn so brightly that the sun itself, the moon and stars, shall stare down at us in envy. Because I can tell you this: most of the years I've lived thus far have been wasted, drowned in alcohol and blood. I've felt such misery for most of my life that I'm not glad to have lived those years at all, and would trade them all, trade each sordid, pitiful moment, for months by your side. Months where I can stand tall as a man, where I can stare at my own reflection and know that I am my own person, that I own my fate, and that nobody can break me. Kill me, yes. But break me? With you by my side? With your love in my heart? Never. So yes. Come die with me. Come live with me. For a few months, maybe a year, we shall defy the odds, and for as long as we can we will celebrate what it means to be good, to be true, to be in love, and when that ax falls, as I know it must, then I will go to the Ashen Gardens with a glad heart, with no regrets and a smile upon my bloodied lips."

The silence that followed was so intense that it practically ached.

"And that," said Zarja, voice soft, "is why I am with him. That is why I am willing to risk all to stand by his side."

Anastasia stepped forward, placed her arms upon his shoulders, and eyes gleaming, smiled up at him. "Your madness is… is utterly infectious," she whispered, voice husky, and then rose to her tiptoes to kiss him on the lips.

"Madness indeed," said Morwyn, voice sullen. "But fuck it. It's not as if I had much to look forward to. A life serving Annaro's whims seems desperately boring at this point. So fine. Let's tell the world to go burn and see how many souls we can send to the Ashen Gardens as our vanguard."

Anastasia's lips quirked into a smile against his own, and then she pulled away to turn and stare at Morwyn, one arm still around Hugh's neck. "Go on. Say you love us, that you'll die for us, and can't imagine being happy anywhere else."

Zarja laughed, and even Hugh couldn't repress a smile when Morwyn scowled and looked away.

But then Zarja's smile slipped away. "What about your… responsibilities?"

Hugh grimaced. "I'm going to have to figure that out."

"What responsibilities?" asked Oreanna.

Anastasia smoothed down her slate blue and gold-trimmed uniform. "To his brother, I imagine. The duke."

"I'll deal with my brother when the time comes," said Hugh. "And if he doesn't see matters as I do, then I'll react accordingly."

"What of Floriana's people?" asked Morwyn. "We need them to fight against Arasim's corruption."

"And Niestor," added Anastasia. "We're going to need his alliance if we're to succeed. None of them will fight alongside you if they know the truth."

"Then we'll have to tell them when the moment is right," said Hugh. "There's idealism and then there's idiocy. We need to show them the truth of the matter first, let them see that the fae are not our enemies. Once they have seen this for themselves, only then will I tell them what we intend."

Nobody seemed convinced.

"And if they don't agree? We'll just kill them all, won't we, my Lord?" asked Morwyn with feigned cheer.

Hugh snorted. "It won't come to that. But no. Telling everyone right now what we intend before they can even understand it is foolishness. Just as Zarja only revealed herself to you two once you'd grown to trust and love Elena."

"Elena?" asked Oreanna.

Zarja responded with a fluid stream of fae, and the ram girl nodded when she was done.

"Very well," said Anastasia. "I feel almost light-headed. Perhaps I need stronger stuff than tea."

"Never drink tea when you can have wine," said Hugh.

"But where do we go from here?" The disciplus took Hugh's empty bottle from his fist, inspected it with a frown, then stepped toward the kitchen. "Our next step?"

"That depends on Oreanna," said Hugh, sitting on the edge of the table once more. "And what she's willing to share with us about Arasim's corruption."

The ram girl hesitated. Which was a huge improvement, thought Hugh. He watched her. Watched her uncertainty, how her hazel eyes flicked from one woman to another, resting on him like a restless bird before flickering away again.

To rest, at last, as he'd known it would, on Zarja.

Oreanna said something in her mellifluous native tongue, something that sounded halfway between the call of a bird at dawn and the trill of a stream over rocks.

Zarja replied in kind, her grace, her gravity, giving her an almost royal aura.

Oreanna pondered, asked something else.

What Hugh wouldn't have given to understand fae.

Zarja's response was immediate, authoritative, and clearly was about Hugh, for both women turned to stare at him.

"Whatever it was, I didn't do it," said Hugh, raising his goblet to his lips and finding it empty. "Most like."

"I don't understand," said Oreanna. "I've never met - never heard - of a human like yourself."

"He's unique in more ways than one," said Anastasia, her smile warm.

Morwyn snorted.

"Look, as much as I like being the center of a compliment party, there's no need. I'm fully aware of how wonderful I am -"

Oreanna raised one of her upswept brows.

"Oh, prepare yourself," said Morwyn. "He's nigh insufferable once he gets going."

"- but this is about trust. Words are pretty, but we've all heard orators who promise gold and deliver poison. So you tell me, Oreanna. What more can I do to prove myself? To prove I only mean ill for those who are demon-tainted?"

Her faun-like ears flicked as she considered him. Despite her young appearance she was manifesting just the same gravity and poise as Zarja; perhaps all fae were able to appear so dignified and poised when they wished.

"Very well." Her tone grew sharp. "You ask me to trust you with the lives of my friends and family. Let me see you trust me in turn."

"All right. How?"

"Remove this rope from my neck, give me a knife, and let me place it at your neck."

"No," said Morwyn. "Absolutely not."

"Done," said Hugh.

Zarja clearly wanted to say something, but at the last bit her lower lip and remained silent.

"Come here," said Hugh.

Oreanna frowned, not liking his peremptory tone, perhaps. So Hugh sighed. "Come here, if you please."

The ram girl stepped forward. Almost Hugh expected to hear the clip clop of delicate hooves, but glancing down, he saw two bare, human feet.

He drew his dagger and with his free hand pressed two fingers under her chin. Her skin was smooth, warm, and this close he could smell her scent, a verdant smell like that of the deep forest. Slowly, gently, he tipped her chin up, elongating her throat, and slid his blade under the cord, the flat of the dagger easing across her skin.

Goosebumps broke out across her neck, down her neckline. She stopped breathing, and for a moment he simply kept the blade against her neck, fingers under her chin, till with a savage slash he cut the cord away and the noose fell to the ground.

She startled, stepped back, to be confronted by the hilt of his dagger.

"Here," he said. "I'm strong, but there's not much I can do with a slashed throat."

"Hugh," said Morwyn, rising to her feet and sending her chair crashing to the ground. "This is utter madness. You've known her for less than half an hour. I understand what you're trying to do, but this is too dangerous -"

"Same goes for her," said Hugh, keeping his voice steady, his gaze locked on Oreanna's hazel eyes. "We're asking her to risk everything. If I can't do the same, I'm not worthy of her trust."

He reached out, wrapped his fingers around her slender wrist, and raised the dagger to his throat.

Felt the blade scritch over his stubble.

"Right here," he said, voice soft. "Press and pull. You'll slash open my artery. I'll bleed out over the floor and be dead in less than a minute."

Oreanna's eyes were wide. Her youthful face pale, her beauty made all the more striking for possibly being the last thing he'd see. Her chest rose and fell rapidly, and he felt her put pressure on the blade, felt it press into his skin.

"I kill you, your attack on Vispathia founders," she whispered, moving closer. "I kill you, this camp falls into chaos. Buying us time until someone comes to replace you."

A second blade insinuated itself through her hair, its point emerging as it slid along her neck.

"You kill him," said Morwyn, "and you'll not draw another breath."

"I was prepared to die for the court when I took up my bow," whispered Oreanna. "Prepared to give my life for my people."

"Morwyn," said Hugh. "Back down."

"No," said Morwyn.

"That's a direct order from your commanding officer," said Hugh. "Back down, captain."

Morwyn's expression crumpled into helpless rage and fury, but her blade retracted, barely disturbing the crimson locks through which it had appeared.

"Now," said Hugh, breathing in deep, slow breaths. "We've come to it. Strike for Vispathia and Arasim, or pull that blade from my neck and help us cleanse the taint from your people."

"You are mad," said Oreanna, searching his face with something akin to dawning wonder. "Or the most manipulative bastard I have ever had the misfortune to meet."

"I'd attest for his honesty," said Anastasia, "but you've no reason to trust me, either."

"I'll attest to his idiocy," said Morwyn, "and know you'd agree with me."

"I vouch for him," said Zarja, moving around the table to stand beside Hugh. Her tail waved hypnotically from side to side, and her eyes fair blazed with gold light. "I, Zarja Lythgraefen el-Abeian, who stood beside White Pelleas at the battle of Golden Ford; I, Zarja Lythgraefen el-Abeian, who was loved by the flame incarnation Amarinth and loved him in turn; I, Zarja Lythgraefen el-Abeian, who walked the Inner Glade before it was burned, who danced at al'Thaneaos before it was toppled, who bathed in the Emerald Pool before it was fouled."

She reached out, slowly, oh so slowly, and laid her hand on Oreanna's wrist. Exerted no pressure, but simply touched her.

"I, Zarja Lythgraefen el-Abeian, who now loves Hugh of Stasiek, and believe in his heart, in his dreams, and his future. I vouch for him."

Oreanna's eyes swam with tears. The blade trembled at Hugh's throat. Her lips trembled, and then with a cry she pulled the dagger away, allowed it to fall from her nerveless fingers, and covered her face as she turned away, nearly taking Hugh's throat out with the tip of one horn.

Zarja moved smoothly to slide an arm around the ram girl's shoulders, to pull her into an embrace. She spoke softly to her in the language of the fae as more sobs wracked the young woman, held her and nodded to Hugh, indicating he should give them space.

Hugh's heart felt like an anvil upon which Fortuna herself was smiting ore, each blow of her hammer resonating through his body and soul. Eyes glazed, mouth dry, he walked backward into the kitchen, to pull a bottle of wine from a crate. Tore the cork free with just his fingers, plucking it forth with a loud pop, and placed the mouth to his lips.

Drank, drank with long, powerful, deliberate pulls, until his stomach was filled with wine.

Watched and made no complaint as Zarja led Oreanna into the large bedroom at the back of the estate.

As Morwyn righted her chair and sat back down, shaking her head in wonder.

Anastasia rubbed at her face, inhaling sharply, and then followed Hugh into the kitchen. Made to take the bottle, thought twice, and plucked one of her own forth.

"One day, Hugh of Stasiek," said Morwyn, voice low, tone hard. "That kind of shit is going to get you killed."

Hugh finished the last of the bottle and pulled it away with a sigh. "Probably. But this is not that day."

Wrong, whispered a voice in his ear.

His blood ran cold. Was that Akilina?

"Hugh?" Morwyn's attention was total, and she was already half out of her chair. "What is it?"

Come, my lord. Akilina's voice in truth. Come answer to your crimes.

And like that his mind was plucked forth, his consciousness dragged down into his core, deeper, into the very nadir of his soul, and the world went dark as it fell away.

# The March to Nazarovo

They marched at dawn, Hugh leading the way on foot before a hundred armed soldiers, their tromp echoing off the northern bridge, the people of Erro turning out to watch them go.

Anastasia rode just behind him in the back of the wagon pulled by Muleheart with Zarja in her Elena guise holding the reins. Morwyn was by his side, dressed in studded leather, blade at her hip, hair pulled back into a business-like ponytail. She’d regained her poise, and marched with perfect composure, betraying no sign of the events from the night before.

Branka moved to the fore of the crowd, her hair pale in the dawn light, raising a hand in parting as they turned north up the mountain road. She’d promised to bring Medved, the massive, haunted guardian bear of Erro close to town, and task him with watching over their village while Hugh was gone. Hugh raised a hand in acknowledgement, then with a deep breath, turned toward the peaks.

It had been years since he’d led an armed force. More years than he could remember, and a tremor of uncertainty filled him. Uncertainty borne from the failures of his past, the massacres, the deaths that he’d caused. A hundred souls, a hundred men and women who were trusting him, trusting in his leadership, his wisdom to keep them alive.

But he’d made his peace with his decisions. The time to equivocate was over. Two patrols to remain and defend Erro. Two more to be left at the Fystov Keep. Two more to follow him into Niestor’s barony, enough to display his strength but insufficient to warrant being treated as an invasion.

Or so he hoped.

Up they marched, the bandits finding their stride, but no songs echoed out down the line, and though the Poison Sisters kept their units tight, their faces were closed with displeasure. The valley revealed itself below on their right, the path ever rising, the mists burning away before the sunlight, the canopy that swathed the valley turning from a deep fir green to a more vivacious emerald with every passing hour.

On they marched, the men in dour spirits, scouts returning every quarter hour to report on the road ahead, others flanking them fifty yards out to watch for ambushes.

Discipline was good, though morale was low; the fact that most of these soldiers had fought Niestor five years ago was an ugly, simmering truth he couldn’t deny. But nobody revolted, nobody refused to move forward, and despite the confrontation he’d had with Floriana over her orders before leaving, the bandit queen led her men with grim determination, so Hugh counted his blessings. The keep hoved into view just before midday, the sound of the masons betraying its presence before the road curved around to reveal the dour edifice, its twin gate towers, the blocky walls mottled with fresh stone. The portcullis hadn’t been delivered yet, and so a stout, make-shift gate had been installed, banded in black iron and made of boards thick enough to withstand the attentions of a battering ram.

Well, withstand one for at least half an hour, at any rate.

An hour passed as they broke for lunch. Hugh toured the keep with the master mason, who reviewed his progress, denied being molested by fae, and was obviously pleased with his work: the walls were of solid construction, and with his amendments were hale and doughty once more.

The keep was cleared of all furniture, swept free of dust, and fresh furnishings were slowly being carted in from Erro and surrounding townships. Foxglove and the half-elf Celandine were to hold the fort, with Oreanna as their guest, and they toured it with him, pleasantly surprised at how quickly the carpenters had replaced doors and even the ruined floors in the gate towers.

Hugh chose not to mention the ghost they’d liberated, nor the history of the keep.

Fifty men and women would remain to guard the pass. If Fortuna blessed them, they’d return this way within the week with Niestor’s alliance secured. If not? Then at least the fort would protect the people of treachery.

Hugh stepped aside as their party reached the front gate, and met Oreanna’s eyes. “A moment?”

The ram-kin hesitated, flashing a glance to the others, then dipped her horned head and moved to join him. Floriana gave a brief salute and marched out into the road where the troops were waiting.

“You going to be all right waiting for us here?” asked Hugh.

The fae tossed her red hair back over a slender shoulder and stared at him defiantly. “You suggesting I might not be safe?”

“Oh, you’ll be safe enough, Celandine will see to that.” He couldn’t help but smile. “But it’s a massive stone fort filled with cold iron. Won’t be comfortable.”

“Very little of this adventure has been comfortable, my lord,” said Oreanna. But a glint of amusement had entered her hazel eyes. “Fortunately, I’m past the age where comfort is of paramount importance.”

“Good to hear. Thank you.” Following an impulse, he reached out and took her hand. “I want to thank you. For your trust. Your faith in what we’re trying to do here. If folks like you and I can’t find common ground, then there’s no hope for our kind ever doing so.”

She startled as his fingers touched her own, but didn’t pull her hand away. “You’re right. That’s what I’ve been telling myself. If I can’t find it within myself to trust you, how can I ask any other to do the same? So I will. I will trust you, Hugh of Staziek. I will remain here in your house of stone, surrounded by strangers, waiting for your return. And I shall pray for you, pray to the moon and stars that you return to us safely.”

“Thank you,” said Hugh. There was something about her. A delicacy, something just shy of skittishness, intertwined with steely determination. She was nervous and somehow confident at the same time, resolved yet very much on edge. Trying to convince herself she wasn’t opting to remain in an enemy’s camp.

Trying to trust him.

He squeezed her hand gently. “I promise I will return. I promise your faith in me is not misplaced. I will do everything I can to make things right, or as right as this twisted, wicked world will allow.”

She searched his face, and her eyes were large, her expression without guile. “I believe you. By my love for the wilderness and the wildness within me, I really do.” A surprised smile came to her lips. “Never did I think I might place my trust in a mortal man again, but you have surprised me, my lord. I shall await you here, and look forward to your return.”

“Thank you,” said Hugh, smiling back. “This could be the start of something special, Oreanna. Your trust, my efforts. Who knows what changes we might bring about?”

“One way to find out,” she said, squeezing his hand before letting go. “And I’m excited to do so.”

“As am I. Take care. I will see you soon.”

She touched her fingers to her brow and bowed. Hugh returned the gesture, then, heart beating strongly, wonder in his heart, he strode out the front gate.

Floriana gathered her Poison Sisters together to speak in private, and upon Hugh was gratified to see her lieutenants salute her with appropriate precision when their meeting was done. Striding out through the main gate to join Hugh, the bandit queen - no, his captain - gave him a salute in turn, and called out for everyone to march.

They crossed the Fystov Pass. Hugh could help but muse over the betrayal that had taken place here only weeks ago, how he and his companions had been ambushed by Katharzina and her men, taken prisoner and begun the endgame that had culminated in Aleksandr’s death.

All for the better.

Across the broad bridge they marched, over the perilous chasm, the wind plucking at their clothing and armor, and back into the narrow pass which peaked several hundred yards on only to begin its sharp descent down into Niestor’s lands.

A series of steep switchbacks illustrated the impregnability of Fystov Fort: it would take a truly herculean effort to bring siege machinery up the narrow trail. These zigzagged down the near cliff-like slope before leveling out by late evening into a merely sharp descending road which whose outset was flanked by twin towers; Niestor had chosen to eschew an actual fort for two squat Martello towers with perfect view of the road and a killing field between them.

And, as it turned out, a perfect view of the switchbacks descending to them.

The tower rooftops were bristling with archers, but the sight of a delegation awaiting in the road between them was reassuring; clearly Hugh’s message had gotten through, and their approach was expected.

Raising a hand, Hugh waited for the column to cease marching, and strode forth with Morwyn and Floriana by his side.

It was hard not to glance up at the arrow-slit windows that riddled the Martello tower’s cylindrical sides, to show no unease as he marched into arrow range from the archers at the top.

Instead, he focused on the group of men awaiting him, a small contingent wearing expensive half-plate and black chainmail, burgundy surcoats falling to their knees with Niestor’s single rose emblazoned across their chests.

Their leader was mounted on a stalwart horse that was more mountain pony than charger, and for that Hugh gave him credit; it would have been foolish in the extreme to risk a warhorse up on these trails.

The man was squat, bald, and seemed to have no neck. A fringe of brown hair ringed his scalp just above his protuberant ears, and instead of armor he wore court finery, a complex and clashing assemblage of scalloped yellow sleeves, a tunic of black puffery adorned with a pattern of miniature gold daggers, along with a half cloak trimmed in wolf fur.

Yet despite his appearance there was a cool calculation to his gaze that prevented Hugh from dismissing him; the envoy watched him approach with tight-lipped patience, hands quiet on the horse’s pommel.

Hugh stopped when he was a dozen yards from the party, and inclined his head. Morwyn then took a step forward, and pitched her voice to carry.

“From Hugh of Stasiek, by the grace of the Fate Maker Lord of Erro and its environs, brother of Duke Annaro of Stasiek, in whose name he ventures forth to treat with you: greetings.”

The envoy touched his heels to his mount’s flanks, urging it forth a few steps, the clop of its hooves echoing off the tower walls.

“In the name of the inestimable Baron Niestor, lord of these lands and bastion of all that is good, civilized, and humane: welcome. Willingly and gratefully I do receive you to his domain, for well have we become aware of the odor of your good reputation, which has spread far and wide like sweet perfume. I, personally, have longed to make myself known to you at some favorable opportunity, and this occasion having made it so, do cherish the moment and give thanks to the Fate Maker for having brought it about. I am Sir Everec, a humble knight in my lord’s service, and do most humbly, oh, so most exceedingly humbly, greet you in his name.”

“Greetings, Sir Everec,” said Hugh. “I thank you for your charitable welcome. We come as promised to confer with Baron Niestor on a matter that I believe is to both our advantage. May we proceed with safe passage to Nazarovo?”

“The road is yours, my lord,” said Everec. “I shall be delighted to escort you to Nazarovo, where the baron himself awaits breathlessly to learn of your tidings. Indeed, he would punish me severely if we were to delay; every moment without you is an agony to him, I am sure, and thus I will not risk his displeasure by offering you sweet meats and wine within the tower, but instead urge all haste.”

“That suits me well,” said Hugh. “Lead on, good sir. We shall follow.”

“Ah, your words are as music to my ears. But alas, if you can find it in your bold heart to forgive me: why does a Lord of Stasiek travel into my baron’s lands in the company of - well, shall we say - ‘infamous’ bandits?”
