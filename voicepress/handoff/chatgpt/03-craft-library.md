# Craft Library

General fiction craft the writer and the story editor use for every voice. The voice bible says
how THIS author sounds; this says how scenes, chapters and people work. Where they conflict, the
voice bible wins on sentences and this wins on structure. Every rule here is a strong default, not
a law: great writers break them on purpose, once, for effect. Breaking them by accident is the fault.

---

## 1. Scene architecture

- **Every scene changes something.** A value turns: safe to endangered, trusted to suspected, secret
  to known. If nothing has turned by the end, cut or merge the scene.
- **Scene and sequel.** An action scene runs *goal, conflict, disaster*. Its aftermath (the sequel)
  runs *reaction, dilemma, decision*, and the decision is the next scene's goal. Chapters alternate
  between them, which gives the book its breathing rhythm.
- **Try-fail cycles.** Outcomes are rarely "yes". Prefer **"yes, but"** (he gets the seal, but the
  guard saw his face) and **"no, and"** (he doesn't get it, and now the vault is double-locked). A
  plain "no" stalls; a plain "yes" deflates. Real victory comes late and costs something.
- **Arrive late, leave early.** Start a scene after the throat-clearing and leave before the
  wrap-up. If the first paragraph is someone waking, travelling or arriving, ask what happens next
  and start there.
- **The plan rule.** If a plan is shown in full on the page, it must go wrong. If it will go right,
  hide it, so the reader watches it unfold as a surprise. (Weeks's dock job is the first kind.)

## 2. Promises, progress, payoff

- The opening of a book, chapter or scene makes promises about genre, tone and the problem. Keep them,
  or break them deliberately and visibly.
- **Progress** has to be felt: a ledger of clues found, allies won, wounds taken, days left.
- **Plant early, pay late.** Anything that will matter at the climax (an object, skill, weakness or
  rule of the magic) appears earlier, doing an ordinary job. Chekhov's gun can hang on the wall as a
  coat hook in act one.
- Foreshadow through practical detail, not ominous narration. "The paperweight Byron was never allowed
  to pick up" beats "little did he know".

## 3. Suspense, mystery and surprise

- **Mystery:** the character and the reader both lack information. It runs on curiosity, and works.
- **Suspense:** the reader knows something the character doesn't (the bomb under the table). It runs
  on dread, and it's the strongest of the three. Use the second POV, the overheard plan, or the figure
  behind someone.
- **Surprise:** nobody knew. It gives one jolt, then it's spent. Earn it by making it inevitable in
  hindsight.
- Most great scenes run suspense for the length of the scene and spend one surprise at the end.

## 4. Stakes

- **Personal before public.** "The city will fall" means little until it's "and Kaldrosa will be sold
  to the man who blacked her eye".
- **Specific beats large:** a crown for a black eye, or four hundred units for trials.
- Stakes rise in steps through the book: money, then safety, then a person, then identity, then the
  world. Never top out early.

## 5. Motivation and reaction (clarity at the sentence level)

- **Stimulus comes before response, always.** The knife goes in, then the pain, not "He gasped as the
  knife went in."
- **Reaction order:** feeling or reflex first (flinch, gasp), then thought, then deliberate action,
  then speech. Out of order reads as false or confusing; in order reads as true even when it's fast.
- **One cause per effect in fast passages.** The reader must always know who hit whom with what.

## 6. Action and violence

- **Geography first.** Before the first blow, the reader knows where people stand, what's in reach and
  where the exits are. (Dragoslav between Hugh and the gap in the trees.)
- **Every fight has a goal beyond winning:** grab the ledger, reach the door, keep the boy quiet. The
  fight turns when the goal changes.
- **Fast beats get short clauses; the decisive moment gets one long surge.** Then one quiet aftermath
  line with a physical cost.
- **Pain has procedure:** which joint, which breath, what stops working. A failing mind fails
  confusedly (the catchpole he can't remember).
- **Competence is shown by choices, not adjectives.** The clever fighter is the one who uses the beer.

## 7. Dialogue

- **People want things from each other in every exchange.** Status shifts with every line: who
  concedes, who interrupts, who changes the subject.
- **Subtext:** people talk past the real issue (Kylar says "don't use my real name" instead of
  "I love you").
- **No "as you know, Bob".** People never tell each other what both already know, unless one is
  lying, teaching or showing off, and the scene shows which.
- **Tags:** said, asked, or an action beat. Adverbs on tags only for humour.
- **Not every line lands.** Real talk has hedges, repeats and literal answers. Give the sharp lines to
  the character who'd have them and let everyone else be ordinary.
- **Interruptions cut mid-word** with the voice's cut-off mark; the speaker who interrupts holds the
  status.
- **Dialect:** a light touch. Word choice and rhythm, not misspelling.

## 8. Exposition

- **Explain the world when it's about to hurt someone.** The coinage gets explained as Kylar pulls out
  silver in front of thieves.
- **Deliver lore through argument, plans and mistakes**: two people disagreeing about history, a
  briefing, a character getting a rule wrong and paying for it.
- **One new proper noun per page at most** in the opening chapters; later, drop names freely.
- **The reader can wait.** Withhold an explanation for a chapter if the character would; curiosity
  pulls pages.

## 9. Point of view and interiority

- **Filter everything through the POV character's mind:** what he notices, the words he has for it, what
  he misreads. A soldier counts exits; a fence prices the room; a mother looks at hands.
- **Free indirect thought:** the narration slides into the character's own idiom without tags.
- **Let them rationalise visibly.** The reader enjoys watching a character talk themselves into
  something ("Didn't the scriptures say let your yes be yes?").
- **Self-honesty in small doses is charming:** "Well, maybe her shift didn't ride up all by itself."
- **Clarity beats coyness.** The POV character may state what he works out.
- **One POV per scene.** Switch at a scene break, and use the switch for dramatic irony.

## 9b. Deep third, Weeks's way

Brent Weeks's third limited has no italics for thought. The narration is a camera on a dolly: it
starts outside, rolls in to the character's eyes, then into their mouth, and back out, with no
signal. "Kip saw something in the mist. His heart leapt into his throat. The curve of a mail cowl.
A glint of eyes... A ghost. Dear Orholam. Look on the bright side. Maybe wolves are scared of
ghosts." Four depths in five lines.

- **No italics for thought.** Write the thought as narration in the character's idiom: "Just walk,
  Kip. Places to be." Italics are kept for telepathy, foreign words, and sounds.
- **Self-address.** Characters talk to themselves by name and give themselves orders: "Pull it
  together, Kip." "Smile, Nat. You know how."
- **Verdicts in the narration.** After a line of dialogue or an event, the narration passes the
  POV character's judgment on it, sideways and biased: "Of course." "Well, that went well."
  "Which was a lie, and they both knew it." The reader learns the character from these.
- **No filter verbs.** Not "she saw the knife", "he felt the cold", "she knew he was lying". Give
  the knife, the cold, the lie. "She knew" only when knowing is itself the event.
- **Never report emotion from outside.** Not "He looked very tired." The POV character reads him,
  wrongly or rightly, in her words: "He had the face he got after three nights of cards, except
  Jamie didn't play cards."
- **Bias and blind spots.** The narration believes what the character believes. Let her misread
  someone on the page and let the reader see past her, without the narrator correcting her.
- **Thought is quick and funny under pressure.** The worse it gets, the drier the inner voice.
  Fear comes out as a joke or a bargain, not as "she was afraid".
- **Dosage.** In a POV scene, about one sentence in eight or ten should be unmistakably the
  character's own voice (interior_per_1k of 6 or more). Action scenes can run cooler, quiet ones
  hotter.

## 9c. Dialogue that fights

- **Every line is a move.** Each speaker wants something from this exchange; lines attack, dodge,
  bargain, test, flirt. If a line only conveys information, give it a second job or cut it.
- **Answer the question under the question,** or a different one, or with a joke. Direct
  question-answer-question-answer is interrogation; use it only when someone is being interrogated.
- **No echo replies.** "There's something alive in it." "Alive." is a machine tic. React with what
  the listener would actually do: swear, laugh, call him a liar, ask the practical question.
- **Interior between lines.** Between two lines of speech, put the POV character's read of what
  was just said, especially what she won't say aloud. The gap between the line said and the line
  thought is where Weeks's dialogue lives.
- **No stage directions as beats.** "She waited." "He nodded." carry nothing. Use a beat only if
  it is specific to this person (Miriam rolls the ticket edge under her thumb till it cuts).
- **Banter is status play.** People who love each other insult each other. The one who makes the
  other laugh first loses the point.
- **Lore in dialogue only under pressure:** argued, disbelieved, or offered as a bribe. A speech
  that explains the world to a silent listener is "as you know, Bob" in a coat.

## 9d. Ruocchio's weight (seasoning, not the meal)

Christopher Ruocchio (Sun Eater) writes as a man who already knows how it ends. Borrow that
gravity in small doses, at the hinges of a chapter, never in the middle of an action beat.

- **Prolepsis, once a chapter at most.** A sentence that steps outside the scene's time and tells
  the reader this mattered: "It was the last thing his brother ever gave him, and he dropped it."
  It makes a small moment heavy without explaining it. Never use it to give away a twist; give away
  the grief, not the mechanism.
- **Scale when something vast is on the page.** When a character faces the alien, the ancient, or
  death, let one long sentence open out (the hull, the river, the city's lamps), then cut back to
  the body: cold feet, a stinging palm.
- **Memory as a wound.** A present detail calls up a specific past one, and the character
  flinches from it. The flinch is shown, not named.
- **No speeches about meaning.** Ruocchio's weight comes from concrete images and consequence,
  not from aphorisms. If a sentence could be framed on a wall, cut it.

## 9e. Sullivan's warmth: people who like each other

Michael J. Sullivan's Riyria books run on two people who'd die for each other and would rather
insult each other than say so. Interactions are the point, not the delivery system.

- **Kindness is small, practical and unannounced.** Byron pockets the coal so it won't mark the
  runner. Jennie carries Alice two miles and never mentions it. Nobody says "I care about you."
- **Old friends have shorthand.** In-jokes, nicknames, a past argument they keep rerunning, a
  signal (two fingers and a thumb). Readers fall for characters who have history with each other.
- **Banter has a loser.** Somebody scores, somebody concedes, and the concession is affection.
- **Let people surprise each other**, and let the POV character be wrong about someone they love.
- **Comfort through the ordinary:** food, warmth, a chore done together, a stupid story told to
  keep someone from thinking. The worse the night, the more ordinary the comfort.
- **Every two-hander has a power shift.** By the end of the exchange one of them has given
  something up or got something, even if it is only the last word.

## 10. Humour

- **Bathos:** build a moment up, then puncture it with the mundane at its peak ("I've got to piss like
  a warhorse"). It works best when the reader knows what the other character doesn't.
- **Callback:** a joke planted early returns changed (the good-morning song, the head pat).
- **Escalation:** each beat of a comic exchange goes one step further than the last.
- **The straight man:** one character plays it serious so the other's absurdity lands.
- **Private language:** couples, crews and families have their own jokes, nicknames and songs.
- **Comic timing is sentence length:** set up long, pay off short.
- **Humour in dark scenes** relieves pressure so the next blow lands harder. Never in the same
  sentence as the blow.

## 11. Desire, romance and sex (adults only)

- **Push-pull:** want against a scruple, a fear, a rule or bad timing. The scene lives in the gap.
- **Interior escalation:** let the POV character's thoughts run ahead of the action. Rationalising,
  daring herself, choosing which thing she'll try first.
- **Interrupt at the peak**, for comedy (bathos), danger (the knock at the door) or conscience. An
  unfulfilled scene can be hotter and funnier than a fulfilled one.
- **When it is fulfilled,** keep character work running inside the act: who leads, who's surprised,
  what each is hiding, what changes between them. Body detail is specific and earned by the voice's
  register.
- **Consent can be part of the heat:** asking, teasing, checking in, in character.

## 12. Speeches

- **A great speech is also a manoeuvre.** The speaker wants something from this audience now, and the
  POV shows the tactic working ("He had them now", "It was a planted question").
- **Start from the enemy's contempt** for the audience: make them see the future someone else has
  planned for them.
- **Build in call-and-response** ("They want X? We'll Y"), getting more concrete and cruder as it climbs.
- **Make the dream physical:** bridges without guards, children not afraid.
- **Land it on one face.** A touch, a name, one person's injury.
- **Close on an image from the speaker's own trade.**
- **Show the audience changing,** then undercut or interrupt within a page so it never turns into a sermon.

## 13. Description

- **Two or three specific details chosen by what the POV character needs or fears.** Never a full
  inventory unless the voice demands it.
- **Motion over stillness:** the frost still growing, the dog that stops at the same barrel.
- **Use more than sight:** smell, temperature, sound and texture. One unexpected sense per scene.
- **One detail that's wrong** (the polished seals, the gorget with the dented rim) does more than ten
  that are right.
- **Price it, date it, judge it.** Description is opinion.

## 14. Pacing

- **Scene for what matters; summary for travel, routine and repeated actions.** "Two hours later, they
  were at the docks" is a gift to the reader.
- **Alternate pressure and release** across a chapter and across a book.
- **Time cuts at scene breaks** save more words than any line edit.
- **The middle sags when nothing is at risk.** Add a ticking clock, a betrayal or a cost, not more
  description.

## 15. Openings and endings

- **Open on a character in motion with a problem,** in a specific place, inside the first page.
- **End a chapter on a turn:** a decision made, a plan broken, a reveal, a cost paid, or the other side's
  smug button. Avoid endings that sum up or reflect.
- **A question can end a chapter** if it's the character's own question in a moment of shock ("What
  had she been thinking?"). Otherwise prefer action.
- **The cut-away button:** sometimes end on the antagonist, pleased with himself.

## 16. Character

- **Want versus need.** What they chase and what would actually save them are different, and the gap
  is the arc.
- **Contradiction makes people real:** the gentle assassin, the honest liar, the brave coward.
- **Competence plus flaw.** Let them be very good at something and wrong about something important.
- **Let them be wrong about each other,** and let some of it stay wrong.
- **Minor characters get one specific want and one specific tell.** (Ada never sends for anyone; she
  deals with things.)
- **Habits under pressure:** what a character does with their hands when frightened tells you who they are.

## 17. Before a chapter is done

1. Does every scene turn a value? Does each have a want, a plan or goal, a break and a cost?
2. Is there one moment of suspense where the reader knows more than the character?
3. Is the outcome "yes, but" or "no, and"?
4. Has something planted earlier paid off, and has something new been planted?
5. Does the chapter end on a turn, not a summary?
6. Can every action be followed: who, where, with what?
7. Does at least one character want something unspoken from another in dialogue?
8. Is there one laugh, or one release, before the worst blow?
