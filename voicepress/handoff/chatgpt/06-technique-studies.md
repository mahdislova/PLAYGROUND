Rain had found its way down the back of Kessa's collar an hour ago and was now somewhere around the small of her back, making itself at home. She sat her horse at the near end of the toll bridge at Harrowgate and studied the gatehouse.

Somebody had paid a fortune for that gatehouse. Dressed stone, fresh tar on the portcullis chains, the Margrave's boar painted over the arch in gold leaf that had never seen a winter. Then they had paid nothing at all for the bridge itself, which sagged in the middle and was missing planks on the downstream side. New money, and frightened of something.

Interesting.

The guard on the gate was perhaps twenty, all elbows inside a coat of borrowed mail, and he held his halberd too tight, with both hands, like he'd been told it might run off.

"Toll's a silver," he said.

"It was a copper last spring."

"Last spring there wasn't a war on," he said, and shifted his grip on the halberd, which didn't help.

"There isn't one now."

"There will be." He said it with enormous satisfaction, as though he had arranged it personally. "Silver, or you swim. River's high, mind."

Kessa considered the river, which was brown and fast, full of branches, and then considered the boy.

"Tell me," she said. "Does the Margrave know you're charging a silver? Or does he think it's still a copper, and you've a very nice pair of boots on the way?"

He went red to the ears and glanced down at his boots before he could stop himself. They were very nice boots.

"A silver," he said again, but quieter.

So he wasn't a thief, only a boy who'd discovered arithmetic. She could work with that.

"Here's a copper," said Kessa, and flicked it to him. "And here's another, for you to forget you ever saw me. If anyone comes asking after a woman with a sword and a bad temper -"

"Never seen one," he said quickly, and swung the gate wide.

Tam lay flat on the counting-house roof with the slates cold under his belly and did his sums.

Two watchmen. The one on the front door was asleep on a stool with his chin on his chest, which was a gift from the gods. The one walking the yard was awake, and had a dog, and the dog stopped to piss on the same rain barrel every circuit as though it were paying a toll. Forty paces round, a minute and a bit each time.

So he'd go down the drainpipe while the dog was busy at the barrel, then twelve feet across to the shutter. Pry it, slip in, and the strongroom would be the second door on the left, if the clerk hadn't lied. The clerk had been far too drunk to lie well.

Except the drainpipe was iron. Iron rang.

He could see it already: his boot on the bracket and the whole length of the pipe humming, the dog's head coming up, and about six seconds after that he'd be wearing the dog.

Not the pipe, then.

The ivy was older than the building and thick as his wrist at the root, and it ran right past the shutter. It would hold him, probably. It had held the clerk's cat, and the cat was enormous.

He thought about the four marks he owed Sallow, and what Sallow had done to Ned Pike's hands over a good deal less. Went on thinking about it for exactly as long as it took the dog to finish at the barrel.

Then he swung his legs over the edge and went down the ivy.

A strand tore loose under his left hand with a soft wet *rrrip*, and for one long sick moment he hung by three fingers over the yard with the dog not twenty paces off and turning his way -

It sneezed. Trotted on.

Tam breathed again, found the shutter and worked his blade into the crack. Easiest four marks of his life, he told himself, and didn't believe a word of it.

The first of them came over the bar, and Brennic broke his wrist on the beer tap and put him back behind it.

The second had a cudgel and more confidence than reach. Brennic stepped inside the swing and set a forearm under his chin, and the man sat down hard on a stool that hadn't been expecting him and went over backward with it. The other two came together, which was clever of them, so he threw the nearer one's own pint in his friend's face, and while one was blinking and the other was mourning his ale he put them both through the front window.

The glass held. They didn't.

After that it was quiet in the taproom, apart from the fire popping and somebody's dog barking two streets over. The fifth man stood alone in the middle of the floor with a knife in his hand and the expression of someone doing sums and not liking the total.

"Well?" said Brennic. "I haven't got all night, or I have, but I'd rather not spend it on you."

The man looked at his friends in the street, then at the knife, as though it had let him down personally.

"Who sent you?"

Nothing. The man's eyes went to the door and back, measuring the distance and not liking that sum either.

"Who?"

"Nobody sent us." He licked his lips. "We just... heard there was a reward."

That should have been funny. Brennic had been worth a reward for years and it had never once kept him up at night, but the man wasn't looking at him. He was looking past him, at the stairs, at the room at the top of them where the girl was supposed to be asleep.

Oh, gods. No.

Behind the bar the publican rose from where he'd been crouching, set down the cleaver he hadn't used, and picked up a glass and a rag.

"You'll be paying for that window," he said.

Orsolya was mending a net on her doorstep when he came up the path, and she didn't stop mending it.

"You've got fat," she said.

"You've got old."

"I was always old. You were always going to get fat. It's nice when things work out." She bit off a length of twine. "You'll want tea."

"I want a word."

"Tea first. Words go down better with something hot behind them, and I'm not having one on my doorstep where Mother Dunn can hear. She's deaf as a post and she'll still hear it."

He followed her in. The cottage was smaller than he remembered, or he had grown, and it smelled the way it always had, of tar and woodsmoke, and of the lavender she had sworn for thirty years she couldn't stand and had never once stopped buying.

She set the kettle on the hook. Didn't look at him.

"Pell's dead," he said.

The kettle didn't care. It ticked as it warmed.

"Is he."

"Six days ago, in Harrowgate. Someone opened his throat in an alley and took his boots. He was looking for you, Orsa."

She took two cups down from the shelf and turned one over to check it for spiders, the way she always had, and found one, and tipped it gently out of the window.

"Well," she said. "He found Harrowgate."

He watched her pour. Steady hands, not so much as a ripple in either cup, and he had seen those same hands shake so badly once that she couldn't span a crossbow. The night they'd left Pell behind on the quay at Vossmouth with the Watch coming up the steps and his leg broken under him, calling their names...

She knew. She had known before he came up the path, and had sat on that step mending a net nobody needed mended so she would have something to do with her hands when he arrived.

"Milk?" she said. "I've got goat's, if you'll have it, though you used to hate goat's."

"Orsa -"

"There's no sugar. I've given it up. Doctor's orders." She handed him the cup and met his eyes for the first time, and held them until he looked away. "Drink your tea, Daven."

He drank his tea.

There were seven of them when they went down the stair into the crypt. Vasko counted them again at the bottom out of habit. Counted twice, because the light was bad and he was nervous, and he'd learned long ago that the night you didn't count was the night somebody was missing. Old Mabb with the lantern, muttering to it the way she always did, as though it were a dog that might wander off. The Pellow brothers, who had never once in Vasko's hearing agreed on anything except money. Thin Ilse with her crossbow wound and ready, which made everyone else walk a little to one side of her. The priest. Vasko himself.

And Dorran, at the back, carrying the rope and saying nothing at all. Dorran talked in his sleep. Dorran talked at funerals.

The crypt was older than the chapel above it by a few hundred years, and whoever had built it had wanted the dead to stay put. Iron bands across every niche. Lead seals on the iron. Somebody had gone to the expense of having the seals stamped, and somebody else, much later, had gone to the trouble of cutting every one of them open.

"Well, that's encouraging," said the younger Pellow.

"It's a good sign," said the elder. "Means somebody's been in and out already. Means it's -"

"Means somebody's been *in*," said Ilse, and nobody laughed.

The priest knelt by the nearest niche and held his hand over the cut seal without touching it. Vasko watched the old man's lips move. Watched him stand, too, and take a slow step back from the iron, then another, until he was standing a good deal closer to the stair than anyone else. Ilse saw it as well. Her crossbow drifted a little toward him.

"Something you'd like to share, Father?" Vasko said mildly.

"No," said the priest. "Nothing at all."

"Only you've moved."

"My knees are old," said the priest, "and the floor is... well. Cold," and every one of them knew it for a lie, and nobody said so, because they needed him to read the inscriptions further in.

They went on, Mabb and her lantern first. The light swung away from the niches and the dark came back to fill them.

In the third niche from the stair, something that had been lying very still for a very long time turned its head to watch them go.

Twenty paces further in, the passage opened into a round chamber, and in the middle of the floor somebody had dug a hole, neat and square, and lined it with the stamped lead seals laid edge to edge, each polished bright as though whoever did it had knelt there for days with a rag and nothing else in the world to do. The younger Pellow looked at it for a long time.

"Who," he said at last, "tiles a fucking hole?"

Nobody wanted to sleep, so they sat around the fire and argued about the dagger instead. It lay on a square of oilcloth between them where Vasko had set it down, and the firelight ran along it like water. Long and narrow, the blade fullered down two-thirds of its length, a crossguard that swept forward in two little horns. The grip was bound in silver wire so fine it looked like thread.

"Thirteenth Dynasty," said the priest. "Court work. See the horns on the guard? They stopped making them like that after the Saltmarch wars, because the horns caught in mail and snapped off."

"Or someone made it to look old," said Ilse. She didn't look up from the bolt she was fletching.

"Someone could have," the priest agreed. "But they'd have had to know about the horns. And nobody does, except old men like me, and dealers, and -" He stopped.

"And?"

"And the people who forge for dealers."

The elder Pellow leaned in to look and his brother leaned with him, so that for a moment their heads nearly touched. "Worth what, then? If it's real."

"If it's real," said the priest, "it's worth more than this whole company. If it's real, it belonged to the Margravine Ysolt, who had her husband killed with it at their wedding feast and then wore it on her belt for thirty years, so everyone would remember."

"Charming," said Mabb.

"It was a different time," said the priest.

"It's the same time," said Mabb, poking the fire until it spat. "People just used to be more honest about it."

Vasko picked the dagger up, which made the priest flinch, and turned it this way and that in the firelight, slow and careful, the way the old fence in Coldharbour had taught him to turn anything he was about to be lied to about. Along the base of the blade, half hidden under the guard, was a maker's mark: a little hammer struck once into the steel. He had seen that mark before. Not in a book. Last winter, on a bench in a back room in Coldharbour, beside a jar of acid and a box of fake rust.

Set the dagger back down on the oilcloth very carefully. Smiled at the priest. "Thirteenth Dynasty," he said. "You're sure."

The priest smiled back. Neither of them blinked.

The sluice gate at Morrow Weir had been built to be opened by twelve men on a windlass, and Tobin had three, one of whom was drunk and another of whom was eleven. And a flood an hour upriver, maybe less.

On paper the answer was the windlass, and the windlass was no answer at all. Three men could turn it, slowly, but the chain had rusted into the gear teeth somewhere around the reign of the last king, and every quarter-turn the whole frame shrieked and bucked like it wanted to throw them into the race. Another half hour of that and they might have the gate a foot open. A foot wouldn't drain a bathtub.

Tobin stood on the walkway with the spray in his face and looked at the problem the way old Harrel had taught him at the mill. He looked at what held the gate shut.

Two iron pins, each as thick as his forearm, through the hinges on the downstream side. The whole weight of the river was pressing on that gate. It wanted to open. It had been wanting to open for sixty years. The windlass wasn't there to lift it. It was there to stop it swinging wide all at once and tearing itself off its own hinges.

So stop fighting the river, and let it do the work.

"Pull the pins," said Tobin.

The drunk one stared at him. "It'll go."

"That's rather the idea."

"It'll go all at *once*, it'll take the walkway, it'll take *us* -"

"Not if we're off the walkway first." Tobin was already fitting the long bar under the first pin. Clever, Tobin, very clever. Now be clever and fast.

Upstream, the river had changed its voice, from a roar to something deeper that he felt in his teeth before he heard it.

An hour, he'd thought. Half that now, or less.

Lady of the Ford, he prayed, which he hadn't done since his mother's funeral, I'm doing the work here. All I'm asking is you don't do anything clever of your own. Just this once. Stay out of it.

The first pin came free with a scream of iron and dropped into the race. The gate groaned and leaned, and held on the one remaining hinge. He got the bar under the second pin and heaved, and felt it move.

"Off!" he yelled. "Everybody off!"

The boy went, and the drunk went faster than any sober man Tobin had ever seen. Tobin gave the bar one last heave, and the pin slid, and slid, and stuck.

A sliver of rust, no wider than a fingernail, had wedged between pin and hinge, and the whole weight of the river was leaning on it now, pinning it tight.

Upstream, the trees along the bank began to fall over, one after another, in a long brown wave.

Well, thought Tobin. That's the Lady for you.

There were forty of them crammed into the drying loft over the tannery, and the place stank so badly that half of them were breathing through their sleeves. Mags had picked it for that. No Warden would come up those stairs voluntarily, and nobody who had come up them tonight would forget it, and she let them stand in the stink for a good while before she started.

"The Guild thinks you're finished," she said. "Old Corrin told me so himself, over a very nice dinner. He thinks the river freezes in six weeks, and when it does, there is no work on the barges, and no work means no rent, and by Midwinter half of you will be living under the arches and selling your boots. He thinks you will come crawling to his hiring hall in the spring and take whatever he offers, because you will have to. He thinks you're too tired and too frightened to -" She shrugged. "Well. And do you know what? He is right. That is exactly what you will do."

Nobody said anything. In the corner, somebody's stomach growled.

Mags had them. Could see it in their faces, the thing that comes just before anger, when a man hears his own worst thought said out loud by somebody else. Most bargemen never thought past next Tuesday's wages, because they could not afford to. Make them think about spring, and their children in spring, and you had them by the collar.

"Unless," she said, and let it hang.

"Unless what?" said Benno, at the front, right on cue. She had paid him a shilling to ask it.

"Unless we stop doing what he expects." She started to walk, slowly, along the front of the crowd. "He wants the river frozen? Fine, we'll cut a channel. He wants us hungry? There's four warehouses of Guild grain on Salt Quay, and I know which watchmen drink. He wants us under the arches? We'll be under his arches, the ones holding up his counting house, with our hands on the props. He wants us crawling?" She stopped. "Let him find out how far we'll crawl, and what we'll take with us when we go."

Somebody laughed, short and ugly. Somebody else said "Aye," and then a few more did.

She stopped in front of Tam Hollis, who had lost two fingers to a winch last month and been docked a week's pay for the blood on the deck. Took his bad hand, the wrapped one, and held it up where they could all see it.

"A week's pay," she said. "For this. That's what Corrin thinks a Hollis hand is worth. I say it is worth more. I say it's worth his whole fucking hall."

Tam's face did something complicated, and he looked at the floor.

"So go home," said Mags. "Go to work tomorrow, keep your heads down, say yes, sir. But keep your knives sharp, and keep your mouths shut, and when I send word, you come." She let go of Tam's hand. "Corrin's been loading the dice for thirty years. This time we're cutting the rope while he's still on the barge."

"Get up," said Wenna, and Arlo said "No" and pulled the blanket over his head.

"You promised Mother you'd fix her gate."

"Your mother," said Arlo into the pillow, "is a gate."

Wenna laughed despite herself and sat down on the edge of the bed, and he rolled over and looked up at her with one eye, his hair standing straight up on the left side. She did the thing they always did. Poked him in the ribs and said "Fish," and he said "Fish" back with a perfectly straight face, and neither of them had ever been able to explain to anybody else why that was funny.

Then he pulled her down.

She meant to object. She had a whole objection ready, about her mother's gate, and the shutters being wide open, and somehow it went out of her head when he kissed her. He tasted of nothing, the way he always did since the fever, not even of sleep. The healer had said it was the scorch in his blood and it would pass, and it hadn't passed, and Wenna had stopped minding about the third week.

And why not, she thought, with his hand on her hip. Why not now? They'd said the words at the stone in front of half the village. The priest had only wanted them to wait for the harvest blessing because the priest wanted a fee. And she'd been waiting since spring, and so, she was fairly sure, had he, and the house was empty. Mother was at the market until noon. Well. Probably until noon.

She sat up and reached for the laces of her shift, taking her time about it so he'd see, and her heart was going hard -

"Gods," said Arlo, swinging his legs out of the bed on the other side, "I've got to piss like a plough horse."

He stood and stretched, scratching his belly. Wandered off toward the yard, whistling, and Wenna sat on the bed with her laces in her hand and listened to him go.

"Did you say something about the gate?" he called back through the door. "Is it the hinge again or the post?"

"The post," said Wenna.

"Right," said Arlo. "Fish."
