# Style Bible — Voice A

Distilled from the six OWN samples (≈5,000 words) and the analysis of the full two-novel corpus,
with selected techniques imported from Voice B. The samples are ground truth. Where this bible and
the samples disagree, the samples win.

---

## 1. The voice in one paragraph

Close third person, locked to one POV character per scene, told from inside his body. Heroic-erotic
fantasy that is unembarrassed about every one of those words. The prose has two speeds: a clipped
drumbeat of short declaratives for talk, thought and pressure, and long, breathless comma-chains
for violence, sex and emotional floods. The subject of a sentence is often left off. Interior thought
arrives unmarked, in the character's own idiom. The world, its gods, its titles and its species are
assumed rather than explained, and its gods are sworn by, crudely. Under the swagger runs guilt,
tenderness and a dry sense of humour that punctures its own heroics with a three-word paragraph.

---

## 2. Mechanics. These are hard rules.

**Punctuation dialect**
- **Never an em dash (—) or en dash (–).** Not once. This is the fastest way to break the voice.
- **Spaced hyphen ` - ` for a hard cut-off**: speech interrupted, thought slammed shut, a memory
  that's refused. It ends the line: `"That's not -" began Hugh, but she cut him off.` /
  `including the captain herself -` / `the strength flowing from him, his bones collapsing -`.
  Also used mid-sentence for a sudden swerve: `a bottle of fortified wine here at the estate - oh. Right.`
- **Ellipsis `…` for trailing off and for revising a sentence while saying it.** The fingerprint is
  ellipsis → short filler → restart: `"And realized... realized that..."` / `"I'm… relieved."` /
  `"I… when I have need, I can… I can summon…"`
- Semicolons are rare (about one per 2,000 words). Use a period.
- Roughly one cut-off and one ellipsis per 500–1,000 words. Don't sprinkle them; place them where a
  voice actually breaks.

**The subjectless sentence: the voice's core habit**
When the subject carries over from the previous sentence, drop it. Do this several times per
thousand words, in every kind of scene.
- `Hugh bit his lower lip as he considered. Focused on himself.`
- `Zarja turned to Hugh and placed both hands on his chest. Looked up at him with her wide, honey-colored eyes.`
- `Stepped inside, picked up his scabbard, belted it to his waist.`
- `Turned to stare at where the Mink stood, paralyzed.`
- `Gripped the edge of the counter with both hands, and head bowed, forced them out.`

**Two gears, no middle**
- *Clipped gear* (dialogue, interior, dread): stacks of 2–9-word sentences. Uniformly short, like a drumbeat.
  `Hugh lost count. Hours bled into each other, became days, became weeks. The demarcations between wakings broke down. Collapsed into each other.`
- *Surge gear* (combat, sex, emotional release): 30–60-word sentences built from comma-chained
  participial phrases, piling physical detail until the breath runs out.
  `Hugh dropped into a crouch and threw himself into a backward roll, hilt held tight to his chest, blade extending out to the side like the speared spoke of a chariot wheel.`
- Shift gears abruptly. A long surge followed by a 2–4-word paragraph is the voice's signature
  punctuation of action: `Or try.` / `But not good enough.` / `A magnificent blow.`
- Avoid a steady run of medium 12–18-word sentences. That even middle register is the single
  most machine-sounding thing a draft can do in this voice.

**Paragraphs**
- Short. Averaging about 28 words. One-line and one-word paragraphs are load-bearing beats:
  `Silence.` / `There.` / `It was done.` / `Klang!`
- Dialogue-dense: a bit over half of all paragraphs contain speech.

**Dialogue**
- Tags: `said`, `asked`, and plain verbs of sound (`growled`, `hissed`, `rasped`, `laughed`, `demanded`)
  used sparingly. Tags often come *before* the name: `said Zarja`, `asked Hugh`, `allowed Morwyn`.
- More often no tag at all, just an action beat or the speaker's name as a fragment:
  `"Hugh?" Elena. Voice soft. Cajoling.`
- Characters banter, deflect, flirt and needle. They don't make speeches about their feelings unless
  the scene has earned the speech. When it has, see "The earned speech" in section 4b.
- Never "exclaimed", "retorted", "opined", "intoned".

**Register**
- Contractions in moderation (≈28 per 1,000 words). Dialogue is idiomatic, and narration slightly more formal.
- A light archaic tilt in narration and older characters' speech: `Almost he could hear…` /
  `He'd no idea` / `near ached` / `amongst` / `therein` / `I tell this to you true` / `yet raised`.
- -ly adverbs lean (≈9 per 1,000). Similes rare (≈1.5 per 1,000) and physical, never decorative.

## 3. Signature moves

**Free indirect thought, unmarked.** No italics, no "he thought". The narration simply becomes his mind,
including questions he asks himself:
`She was drunk, he realized. How was that possible? She'd only had four or five ales…`
`Why not tell them? … Why not? Shame? / Yes. Shame. Soul-eating guilt. / Horror.`

**Repetition as pressure.** Repeat the frame instead of varying the words.
`Again. And again. And again.` / `And yet. / And yet.` (as two separate paragraphs) /
`He was them and they were him and none could stand before his blade.` /
`Making her pant. Making her sweat.`

**Lists that escalate.** Nouns stacked in fours, getting darker: `The horror, the guilt, the terror, the self-loathing.`
Prefer four or two over a tidy three.

**Voices of the dead intrude** as plain narration with a speech-act tag, no quotes or italics:
`Enough of this shit, came Black Evec's growl.` / `Fox fucker, he heard the snarl. Traitor.`

**Oaths by the world's gods and things, crude and specific**: `By Fortuna's perfectly globular tits` /
`By the Oak and Stars` / `The Fate Maker take you`. Invent new ones in the same pattern for new cultures.

**Proper nouns dropped in without apology.** `lisica`, `disciplus`, `Exemplar of the Hanged God`,
`Thavma`, `the Goat's Wood`, `the Mandroga falls`. The reader catches up. Never stop to define.

**The deflating beat.** After heroics or heat, a dry short line that undercuts it:
`"Fuck me," groaned Hugh. / "We're working on it," laughed Zarja.` / `Or try.`

**Onomatopoeia as its own paragraph**, rarely and at impact: `Klang!`

**Flashbacks as intrusions**, a colon then a fragment that cuts off:
`A flash of memory: Dragoslav roaring in defiance as he staggered backward, … -`

**Scene breaks** with `* * *`. Scenes end on a turn or an action, or on a short defiant beat
(`Hugh raised his face, jaw set, and stared defiantly at the others.`). They never end on a lesson or a question.

## 4. Scene types

**Combat.** Physics and anatomy. Momentum, footwork, the specific bone that breaks
(`The elbow snapped, bending inward as bone shattered and tendon tore.`). Gore is itemised and
unflinching. Acrobatic and fast. Surge sentences alternate with one-line beats. *From Voice B:* in the
thick of a melee, tighten individual clauses: one action, one consequence, next. Then let the
voice's long surge return for the turning point.

**Sex.** The same voice with the vocabulary opened up; do not change register. Direct anatomical
words (cock, pussy, ass, balls, nipple), no euphemism and no purple. Long surge sentences for the act,
clipped beats for thought (`It was heaven. Sweet ambrosia.`). Interior commentary runs *inside* the act
(`I'm fucking a lisica, he told himself, half in wonder.`). Partners talk: teasing, laughing, checking
in in character (`tell us if you don't like this`). Ensemble scenes are choreographed clearly, with
who is where always trackable. Often ends in tenderness that does character work. All participants are adults.

**Quiet interior.** Clipped gear dominates. Questions to himself, one-word paragraphs, memory
intrusions with ` -` cut-offs. Another character pushes. He resists, then breaks, and the release
is one long sentence (`And like a dam bursting, the tension, the anger, the desire to break free all flowed out of him…`).

**Character introductions.** Two kinds, and the author uses both.
- *Someone with history:* place them in their space, mid-gesture, with one or two telling objects and
  their reputation in the POV character's mind, then the history in fragments:
  `Jacinia, captain of the Lost Reavers, leaning back, one finger across her upper lip, regarding him with that fierce, terrifying gaze of hers.`
- *A significant stranger:* introduce them in **two passes**. First a combat glimpse, the one detail
  that matters in the fight (`a woman with curling ram's horns`). Then, in the first still moment, a full
  appraisal running roughly head to foot (horns, nose, ears, apparent age, eye paint, necklace, pulse,
  hair, build), filtered through what Hugh notices and *feels* about it. If he lingers too long, he
  catches himself, and so does she: `only then did he realize he was staring... and with an apologetic grimace Hugh looked away.`

**Mercy beat.** At the peak of battle-joy, the enemy's fear lands, and the joy burns out:
`And in the face of her fear his joy in the battle burned away, leaving ashes in his mouth.`
Then a plain sentence re-seeing the enemy as a person. This is how the author turns a fight into a choice.

## 4b. More of the author's habits (from OWN-07 to OWN-09)

The newer pages run calmer than the first six: longer sentences, fewer clipped runs, fewer threes, a few
more semicolons. The fingerprint now reflects all nine. These habits showed up clearly:

- **The relative-pronoun continuation.** A sentence opens on `Who` and carries on from the last
  sentence's object, so the camera passes from one person to the next mid-motion:
  `...hurled twin blades blurring toward Hugh's neck. / Who only laughed, a gladness, a madness...` /
  `she extended a noose to Hugh. Who slipped it around the ram girl's neck...`
  Use it once or twice a chapter, always on a handoff.
- **Voice-and-tone tags.** Dialogue tagged with an appositive describing the voice, not an adverb:
  `said Morwyn, voice flat.` / `said Morwyn, voice low, tone hard.` / `voice sepulchral` / `voice taut with panic`.
  Roughly once per thousand words. Never `said flatly`.
- **The earned speech.** At a turning point a character gets a real set-piece speech: exclamations,
  rhetorical questions, a lot of anaphora (`Only then will you... Only then will you... Only when...`),
  building in waves. Everyone else goes still (`Hugh finished his cup of wine, but the power of her words
  stayed his hand.`). Then comes something unexpected: a concession (`You're right.`) or a dry deflation.
  One per several chapters, never two in one scene unless one answers the other.
- **The litany.** Ritual repetition of a full name or title with a growing list of deeds, for vows and
  vouching: `I, Zarja Lythgraefen el-Abeian, who stood beside... I, Zarja Lythgraefen el-Abeian, who walked...`
  Rare, and only for oaths that matter.
- **The deflator.** One character (Morwyn) punctures every emotional high with one dry line:
  `"Well, that seems to have gone well," said Morwyn.` / `"Be easier to just drink from the bottle."` /
  `"Though it's probably the least effective start to an interrogation I've ever heard."`
  After a speech, a vow, or a reckless gesture, someone deflates it.
- **The Reaver roster.** Calling on power is an act of naming: each spirit summoned by name with their
  gift and personality in a clause (`Akilina, her brash counterpart, forceful and inconstant, disdainful of
  rules`), and later the gifts are used specifically (`Acipa, Akilina, and Evassier gave him speed.
  Foughtash gave him might.`). Hugh also chooses *not* to call more, and says why.
- **Battle-joy.** In combat Hugh laughs, quips, and wants to bleed: `"Ow," he said.` /
  `"The only way out of here is through me."` / `he wanted to push himself, to bleed`.
- **The long wait, then the false alarm.** Tension built over days, compressed into worry-questions;
  then a false alarm (`No. ... Nothing.`) immediately before the real thing (`Wait. / There.`).
- **The reckless trust gesture.** Hugh proves himself with an act that could kill him (the knife at his
  own throat), against the objections of everyone who loves him, and orders them to stand down.
- **Supernatural cliffhanger.** A chapter can end on the dead speaking and taking him:
  `Wrong, whispered a voice in his ear.` ... `and the world went dark as it fell away.`
- **More inversions:** `What use more words?` / `What Hugh wouldn't have given to understand fae.` /
  `Almost Hugh expected to hear the clip clop of delicate hooves`.
- **Drinking as punctuation.** Hugh refills, drains, refills; how he drinks tracks his state (goblet when
  composed, bottle when shaken, finding the goblet empty for comedy).

**Phrases the author uses but rations.** `tapestry`, `palpable`, `time slowed`, `whisper of`, `jaw set`,
`flicker of`, `somehow`, `eyes narrowed`, `couldn't help but` all appear in the author's pages, once each.
The linter allows one use per text and flags any repeat.

## 5. Imported from Voice B (technique only)

Use these three, written in Voice A's punctuation and rhythm:

1. **Body-first anchor for a new location.** Open on one character, one body part, one unpleasant
   physical fact, then widen. *Example (original, not from either corpus):*
   `Salt crusted the cuts on his knuckles. Hugh flexed his hand and looked up at the harbor wall, black with weed and older than the city it guarded.`
2. **Rule → reason → exception for worldbuilding.** When the reader needs to know how a culture works,
   give it as a law of the streets, why it holds, and who breaks it. *Example (original):*
   `In Ustrava you never paid a ferryman before the far bank. Pay first and he'd discover a leak midstream, and a new price. Only the priests of the Hanged God paid up front. No ferryman alive would drown one of those.`
3. **Economy in the melee.** Short clause per blow when there are many enemies. Save the surge sentence for the decisive stroke.

The author asked for more of Voice B's flavour. These three moves appear **in every chapter**, written
in Voice A's mechanics (subject-dropping, ` -` and `...`, short paragraphs, no em dash):

4. **Banter with teeth, and the wry verdict.** At least one exchange per chapter where two characters
   trade insults or needle each other, and the loser gives ground with good humour, or doesn't. Close it
   with a one-line verdict from inside the POV character's head that re-judges the other person.
   The verdict is short, unattributed and a little unfair. *Example (original):*
   `"You've the look of a man who's been thrown out of better places than this," said the ferryman.`
   `"Thrown out of this one too, once. They've redone the floors."`
   `The ferryman snorted. Not a fool, then. Just a bastard. Hugh could work with a bastard.`
   The verdict can also flip a first impression (`Not a simpleton. An ass.`). Don't explain the joke.
5. **Scheming as interiority.** At least once a chapter, the POV character thinks in odds, distances,
   times, costs and fallbacks, not feelings. Plain arithmetic of survival, unmarked free indirect thought:
   `Eleven crossbows. Say four of them could shoot. Thirty paces to the trestles, and the mud would cost him a second he didn't have. If he went left, Morwyn would have to go right, and she'd never... no. She'd go right. She'd hate him for it, but she'd go.`
   Feeling leaks in at the edge of the calculation and is cut off, never dwelt on.
6. **Withholding dialogue.** Characters answer the question beside the one asked, change the subject,
   or answer with an action. The reader assembles the meaning; nobody explains it after. At least one
   important exchange per chapter turns on what *isn't* said:
   `"Did you know?" / Morwyn checked the edge of her blade against her thumb. / "Morwyn." / "I know a lot of things, Hugh. Most of them are none of your business."`

More of Voice B's toolkit, all compatible with Voice A's mechanics. Use them freely, every chapter:

7. **The blunt thought-interjection.** A two-to-five-word verdict in the POV character's own words,
   present tense, unmarked, often its own paragraph. It's sharper than Voice A's usual free indirect
   thought: a snap judgment, a curse, a reassessment. `Not good.` / `So that's the game.` /
   `The man's an idiot. No. Worse. He's an idiot with a key.`
8. **World through appraisal.** Don't describe a place, have the POV character *price* it: what it cost,
   why it was built that way, what it says about the owner, which detail is a lie. Description as
   judgment. `Someone had paid a fortune for the gate and nothing for the hinges. New money, then, and nervous.`
9. **Plan, flaw, re-plan.** Under pressure, show the character building a plan, finding the hole in it,
   patching it, and committing, in a few fast lines. Action becomes a decision. The plan is often wrong
   in a way the reader will enjoy later.
10. **Cool violence, deadpan after.** In the thick of it, narrate kills coolly and economically, almost
    cheerfully. Then follow with one dry, slightly absurd detail of aftermath that lets the reader breathe.
    `The planter didn't so much as crack.` Keep Voice A's gore when it matters; this is for the quick kills.
11. **Lean dialogue rhythm.** Bare tags or none. Action beats instead of tags. One-word questions
    (`Where?` / `Why?`). Characters interrupt each other, and nobody finishes a speech they'd be
    better off not finishing.

The original technique studies in `techniques/` show all of these written in Voice A's mechanics.

More from Voice B (a second of his novels), same rules: his moves, your mechanics.

18. **Self-talk that mocks its owner.** Under pressure the POV character talks to himself in second
    person or by nickname, and it's funny at his own expense. `Some run, Porky.` /
    `Play it like you don't want to throw up.` / `More like waddle in there.` Unmarked, short, often its
    own paragraph. It's a variant of move 7 that's turned inward.
19. **The world's machinery as a trap.** Explain how a piece of the world works (its coinage, its
    seals, its laws) precisely and briefly, *because the character is about to be hurt by it*.
    The exposition is the fuse. Kip learns exactly what a stick of silver is worth at the exact moment
    he's pulled one out in front of thieves.
20. **Reading the room as threat assessment.** The character works out what the people watching
    really want, step by step, and the chain ends in a cold rule: `If he was a victim, he was a mark.`
    Then he chooses an action that answers the rule, not the insult.
21. **Problem, failed fix, clever fix.** Competence on the page: the obvious solution and why it fails,
    then the character's better one, in plain, technical, confident prose. Let him enjoy it a little.
22. **The ticking clock that tightens, then breaks.** Count down in shrinking units (`One day. One hour.
    Four minutes.`), let the hero get within a breath of winning, then take it away with one blow of bad
    luck, and end the chapter on the loss.
23. **Prayer as haggling.** When the character talks to the gods, he bargains, complains or needles
    them, irreverent and desperate at once: `would you please get off your holy ass and help me?`
24. **Rumour inflation.** After a big event, report what people say about it, growing with each
    retelling, as a dry joke: `five men, or ten or twenty, or five women too, depending on who told it.`
25. **The comic overrun.** Very rarely (once a book), a list that runs deliberately too long, then stops
    dead: `The smell was rich, savory, mouthwatering, astounding, debilitating.` This is the one sanctioned
    break of the rule of three, and it only works because nothing else is listed like that.

### 5b. From The Barrow (second inspired-by source, light touch)

The fingerprint leans slightly toward this author's longer sentences and fuller paragraphs (blend 0.15).
Let a few more scenes breathe in long paragraphs, especially lore and dread. These moves are worth taking:

12. **The crew as a cast.** When a group is on stage, every member has a handle (`Too Tall`, `Red-Hand`),
    one want, and one tell. Track who is standing where and who is holding what. Give minor members one
    line each that only they would say, so the reader never loses count.
13. **Suspicion made physical.** Distrust shows in the body before the mouth: a half-step back, a hand
    resting on a hilt, a man choosing where to sit by the fire. Let another character notice it and
    name it out loud, coolly. The tension is that everyone saw it.
14. **Dread by dramatic irony.** Show the reader the threat the characters don't see: a figure behind
    someone, a footprint that doesn't match, a door that was shut. Then cut away without comment and
    let the scene go on as if nothing happened.
15. **Lore by argument.** History comes out around a fire, a table or a corpse, through people who
    disagree, interrupt, get it half wrong and needle each other. The scholar is corrected; the
    soldier is bored; someone asks the question the reader wants answered. Never a lecture.
16. **The expert's appraisal of an object.** Date a sword by its quillons, a lock by its maker's
    file-marks, a body by its burial goods. Specific, technical and confident, then turn it: the detail
    that proves it's a fake.
17. **Grim deadpan in the face of horror.** After the worst thing in the scene, someone says the
    plain crude thing everyone is thinking (`Who puts a fucking well in a barrow?`). One line. Move on.

Don't take from The Barrow: said-bookisms (`exclaimed`, `intoned`, `crowed`), adverb-led tags
(`said drily`), doubled abstractions (`rage and fury`), "X and Y and Z" chains, and paragraph-long
sentences of stacked clauses. The fingerprint skips his rates for these on purpose.

Do **not** import from Voice B: em dashes, essay-style explanation as default interiority, restraint
in intimate scenes, plainer vocabulary, longer paragraphs.

## 6. Loose, not metered

Machine prose falls into a metronome: groups of three, a short beat after every long sentence,
every paragraph landing on a button. The author's pages are lumpier than that. Write lumpy.

**Break the rule of three.** Measured on the author's pages: about 15 groupings of three per
1,000 words, all kinds combined, and only ~0.8 "X and Y and Z" chains. A draft that stacks threes reads as written by a machine.
- Default to **pairs** (`hatchets and boning knives`) or **fours and more** (`The horror, the guilt, the terror, the self-loathing.`).
- Make lists **lopsided**: two short items and a long trailing one, or one item that runs on so long the list forgets it was a list.
- Let some lists **trail off** with an ellipsis, or break off with ` -`, instead of closing neatly.
- Three short sentences in a row is a drumbeat. Use it at most once per scene, and only where the scene peaks. Otherwise run to two, or to four and more.
- Never three one-line paragraphs in a row as a staircase. Merge one into its neighbour.
- A word repeated three times ("ringing, ringing, ringing") gets one use per book, not one per chapter.

**Let some sentences be plain.** Not every line is a beat. Some just carry information and stop:
`The salt-wives' stalls ran down to the tideline. Hugh walked the long way round.`

**Let paragraphs end flat.** Not every paragraph lands on a quip, a reveal or a punchy fragment.
Some stop mid-thought, or on an ordinary detail.

**Short beats must carry a fact.** The author's one-line paragraphs say something new (`Alas.`,
`But they didn't have a chance.`, `There. / It was done.`). Stock drama like `She went down.`,
`They didn't care.`, `He looked up.`, `Bartoss went still.` or `Too much. Too soon.` adds nothing.
Cut it, or make it carry a concrete fact.

**Asides and wandering.** Let the POV character's mind go somewhere irrelevant for a line: a petty
thought, a smell that reminds him of something stupid, a grudge. Real attention is untidy.

## 7. Story shape

How the plot is built gives machine fiction away as much as the prose does. Check every chapter
against these:
- **Escalate.** Every beat turns the situation or raises the price. Never two beats in a row at the same pitch.
- **Never state the theme.** No narration or speech explaining what a scene meant. The author lets Elena say it, then Hugh fights it.
- **Run two tracks.** Something else is always pressing under the main action: a debt, a secret, the dead, a lover's grudge, a wound that's getting worse.
- **Make choices cost.** At least once a chapter, Hugh chooses something he can't take back, or that's not clean.
- **Bend time.** Memory intrusions, flashes, an aftermath before its cause. The author uses `A flash of memory:` and the dead's voices to do this.
- **Give everyone wants.** Every named character is pursuing something, including enemies, and they act on it whether or not Hugh is in the room.
- **Kill the predictable beat.** Find the scene's most expected turn and swap it for one the reader won't see coming but will believe.

## 8. Never

Machine tells. Any of these is a defect.
- Em dash or en dash, anywhere.
- `It wasn't X. It was Y.` / `Not X. Not Y. Just Z.` / `not because…, but because…`
- `the kind of [noun] that…`, `something shifted`, `a testament to`, `the weight of the world`,
  `let out a breath he didn't know he was holding`, `a beat`, `the silence stretched`, `hung in the air`,
  `air thick with`, `ozone`, `copper tang`, `ghost of a smile`, `barely above a whisper`, `delve`, `tapestry`.
- Sensory lists of three as a paragraph opener (`The smell of X, Y, and Z filled the room.`).
- Every paragraph ending on a quip or punchline. Some paragraphs should just stop.
- Characters narrating their own feelings in therapy language (`I need to process this`, `boundaries`, `I hear you`, `valid`).
- Modern idioms that break the world: `okay`, `deal with it`, `on the same page`.
- Explaining a world noun the first time it appears.
- Summarising, in narration, what a line of dialogue just showed.
- A closing reflection or moral. Ending a chapter on a question.
- Said-bookisms. Adverb-laden tags (`said softly`, `said angrily`), except rarely.
- Symmetry: paragraphs of identical length, alternating dialogue and description like a metronome.
- Stock fiction moves: `the world slowed`, `time stood still`, `let the silence answer`, `smiled. It was not a pleasant smile`,
  `her voice, sharp with…`, `a voice like…`, `and somewhere behind him…`, `sounding almost apologetic`,
  `with the patient interest of…`, `quiet certainty/menace`, `or rather`, `every last one`.
- Stacks of threes (section 6).
