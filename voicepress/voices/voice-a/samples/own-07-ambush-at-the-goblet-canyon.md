<!-- OWN-07: Waiting, ambush, combat with Reaver summoning, capture -->

Hugh sat within the blind, wrestling with doubt and frustration. One week they'd been waiting, watching, with no success. No sign of interest from the fae, not even a hint of their having noticed the setup.

A tent was erected in a goblet canyon a mile up the slope of the mountain. It ostensibly served as the base of operations for a small scouting party led by Hogweed, which would loop around the forest before returning each night to rest. As camping spots went it was a good one; the small canyon, really a glorified gulley, hid the fire from sight, and protected the camp from direct assault on three sides.

The scouts, however, made sure to leave trails leading back to the camp each day, and spent their evenings and nights watched by an entire regiment of bandits led by a Poison Sister and Hugh, who'd created a series of blinds and nooks in the rocky canyon walls from which to watch.

The plan was simple. The scouts would draw the fae to this lonely outpost, the fae would attack, and the regiment would spring its counter-ambush.

Simple.

Yet nothing had happened for a week. Seven days during which Hugh had replayed every facet of their plan over and over again. Had the fae noticed the hidden men and women? Had they scented them, perhaps, or noticed them filtering down to the main camp and back every couple of days?

They'd used Zarja's advice to best effect: the fae, apparently, were creatures of dawn and dusk, and the least likely to be encountered or exploring at midday, which was when they'd effected their movements.

But who was to say the fae hadn't noticed their tracks? Weren't aware of their ploy, and were planning an even bigger ambush to kill all of them at once?

Zarja had counseled them to not overthink the matter. "My people are not fond of careful planning or structured patrols. They go where they will, when they will, and should they discover something of interest, they investigate on the spot. Few are wise enough or display sufficient restraint to think matters through. If they come across your tracks, they will follow them. If they espy your camp, they will attack. They are as elemental as they are dangerous, as prone to impulsive action as they are mercurial in their favors. You're not laying a trap for trained human soldiers. Remember that."

And yet.

Seven days in, and nothing.

Hugh shifted his weight. He was wedged behind a screen of rubble and gray canvas through which he could burst with ease, but behind which he lurked in increasing discomfort and cold. The rocky walls of the crack pressed in around him on all sides, and boredom and frustration were his only companions.

How much longer would they wait before trying another approach? Another week? A day?

Dusk had fallen, the sun having dipped behind the far western peaks, and the goblet canyon was a morass of gloom but for the small fire burning outside the sole tent. The 'scouts' had returned but half an hour ago and now sat around the flames, eating their meal, fighting to appear relaxed but flinching at every sound. The trees that loomed over the walls of the canyon were dark and impenetrable, menacing in their somber solidity. As if the very woods were on the side of the fae.

Hugh shifted and fought the urge to shiver. Peered through the crack in his blind. He and the regiment were hidden at the back of the goblet, the dead end; on the far side of the camp from him the canyon ran forth between two narrow walls, growing ever more shallow as it rose to finally merge with the mountain slope, its length choked with fallen rocks and toppled trees.

Not an ideal approach, even for fae. But perhaps it would serve as an emergency escape route when the trap was sprung.

Or so he hoped.

Wait - was that - ? Hugh froze, peered up at the lip of the canyon, where a sharp boulder he'd taken to calling 'the Nose' extended out a yard into the air. Movement?

Holding his breath, Hugh studied the Nose, the rocks and rubble around its flanks, the shadows at the base of the trees that leaned out in their desperate bid to capture the sunlight that fell upon the canyon during the day.

No. Hugh swallowed his disappointment. Nothing. Just his imagination, perhaps the flickering leap of shadows cast by the fire below -

Wait.

There.

Hugh lowered his hand to the hilt of his blade. A slender figure had detached itself from the gloomy depths and moved sufficiently forward to peer down at the tent. Wild vines were wreathed in the fae's hair, his skin daubed with swirling dark patterns, a slender, serpentine blade clutched in his hand.

Hugh's heart began to hammer. A scout? The first of an attack party? The fae was utterly silent, hard to keep track of even though Hugh was staring right at him. Still as a branch, inhuman in his poise and the fixity of his attention on the scouts below.

Hugh didn't even dare breathe a whisper to himself. Watched, locked in place, until the fae raised a hand and twitched two fingers, as if summoning others forward.

Yes.

A half-dozen shadows resolved themselves into other attackers, each distinct from the other, some with horns, one with a head of feathers instead of hair, some with bestial features, others inhumanly beautiful. All, however, were adorned with the swirling marks that Zarja had explained indicated they were on the war path, and all clutched sylvan weapons in their fists.

Six or seven of their foes, staring down at the tent with eyes that glittered with hate.

Hugh slowed his breathing. Calmed the storm in his center, and reached out to the slumbering spirits in his soul: Acipa, he called, summoning one of the Reaver lieutenants he'd not reached for often, a woman who'd intimidated him with her focus, efficiency, intuitive wisdom and deep sense of the truth. Akilina, her brash counterpart, forceful and inconstant, disdainful of rules and eager for the fight. Foughtash for his strength and ferocity, and Evassier for his precision and talent with blades.

The four Reavers slipped into his body, filling him with feverish strength and might, making the world seem to move a fraction slower, his weapon all the more familiar in his grip.

Now he was ready.

The lead of the fae party still had his fist raised, crouched as he was by the Nose, and then dropped his hand to point at the tent and the seven fae leaped over the chasm's edge to fall upon the scouts.

"Now!" roared Hugh, bursting forth from his blind, tearing through the canvas as he threw himself forward to meet the attack.

The scouts seated by the campfire scrabbled back in panic, drawing their weapons, even as the walls of the goblet canyon exploded in a mess of ruined screens and rubble, twenty or so warriors bursting forth with bows raised, arrows already nocked.

The fae realized their predicament even before they hit the ground. Hugh saw panic and fury in their leader's eyes, heard him cry out a command in their unintelligible language, and the attackers turned to flee, not even bothering to throw weapons at their targets.

Turned to race down the canyon's choked length, the sole boulder and log-filled chute that led out to the mountainside, whose obstacles should have proven impossible for the clumsy humans to cross and give chase - but not so for them.

Their leader leaped, soaring forward over the first rocks into the scree of slender branches that crisscrossed the escape route - and crashed back off the wiry shoots as if he'd run straight into a marble wall.

The others encountered equal difficulty. Half of them made it through, smashing their way through branches and tendrils of ivy that were unnaturally rigid, but two others rebounded in like manner.

Shouts of excitement, bandits racing forward, and the three fallen fae leaped to their feet and turned, teeth bared as they met the onslaught.

Their leader hurled a handful of slender darts at the oncoming bandits, dropping three of them, and shouted in his incomprehensible language; one of the fae, a woman with curling ram's horns, ducked her head and charged right at Hugh who was leading the attack even as the third - his head filled with feathers instead of hair - hurled twin blades blurring toward Hugh's neck.

Who only laughed, a gladness, a madness, tearing through him like a flash fire. With a sweep of his blade he knocked the two daggers out of the sky and with his free hand caught hold of the curling ram's horn of the charging girl, sidestepping her so that she rushed past him, only to dig in his heels and haul back, tearing her head back, arching her back, her momentum carrying her up into the air, so that for a moment she hung, suspended, two yards above the ground, before he slammed her down onto the dirt.

The bandits' own charge had faltered, and now they stepped back, giving Hugh room as he turned to face the other two fae, his blade spinning lazily in his off hand, a dangerous smile playing across his face.

The feathered man and the leader stared at him, reflections of the camp fire burning deep in their alien eyes, their faces almost human, their movements unnaturally smooth, their breath coming in deep, terrified jerks.

"Come on," said Hugh, raising his hand to beckon at them with two fingers. "The only way out of here is through me."

The horned girl was stirring at his feet, curling onto her side, the breath driven explosively out of her lungs. Not a problem for a few moments more.

The feathered man let out a scream of rage, the cry of a hawk descending into its plunge, and leaped straight up, even as the leader whipped both hands forward, unleashing a storm of daggers he couldn't possibly have been holding.

Time slowed. Hugh spun to the side, pivoting on the ball of his foot, bringing his blade down in an oblique slash, cutting six of the knives out of the air. The metallic impacts rang out, and then the hawk fae came rushing down on the diagonal, his taloned feet straining for Hugh's flesh, shooting down like a flung spear, faster than thought.

Hugh braced himself, took the strike full on even as pain blossomed in his side from the few daggers that had slipped past his guard. Talons latched onto his shoulder, dug into his chest, the fae's impact as tremendous as that of a falling boulder.

Hugh grunted and gave no ground, flexed his knees to take the assault, and stopped the fae cold.

Who remained latched onto him, standing atop Hugh as a mountain climber might a perilous crag, his eyes wide in shock, clearly having expected to drive his prey crashing into the ground.

The fae leader leaped forward, sinuous as an eel, twin curved blades in hand now, swirling so that he was a dervish of sinuous steel.

This was a language Hugh could understand.

Ignoring the pain that tore into his shoulder and the breadth of his chest from the fae's talons, fighting even as the man remained poised above him, Hugh engaged the leader, laughing as he stepped into the fae's storm.

He could have summoned more Reavers. Could have overwhelmed his opposition with his might, become so powerful they'd have been as gnats before the breath of a furnace, but no - he wanted to push himself, to bleed, to give release to the tension and frustration of the past week.

To bleed, and bleed others in turn.

The fae leader would have cut him down in moments if Hugh had faced him alone, so preternaturally skilled was he.

But Hugh didn't fight alone. Acipa, Akilina, and Evassier gave him speed. Foughtash gave him might. And together, they were the fae's match; Hugh's longsword met the fae's every darting stab, turned every flickering flash, broke apart the leader's assault, tore the tapestry of his sword work into shreds, and when an opening appeared he slammed the flat of his blade against the fae's head, sending him reeling.

The feathered fae bunched his legs, leaped off Hugh, relinquishing his cruel grasp, would have leaped free, but Hugh surged up after him, caught him by a scaled ankle.

Yanked him back down, eliciting a squawk of surprise from the man, and when he was level Hugh hammered a punch into the man's face that broke bone and drove the fae crashing back into the dirt at the leader's feet.

An avalanche slammed into Hugh from behind, bending him backward, drove him six yards forward in the blink of the eye, and hurled him straight into the cliff face.

Hugh turned his shoulder at the last moment, impacted, brought down a shelf of rock, crashed to one knee.

His spine should have shattered from that blow.

Almost had.

Grimacing, he raised his head, rubble and dust cascading down his shoulders, and saw the horned fae panting, half crouched, just before him, her eyes sparking fire and defiance.

By Fortuna she was beautiful in her rage and hatred.

Her ram's horns sprouted directly from her forehead, an inch above each upswept eyebrow, ribbed and curling almost a foot wide on either side. Her nose was flattened, her ears like those of a faun, but she was young, or seemed so - if she'd been human, Hugh would have pegged her as no older than eighteen. For despite the might of her horns, there was a delicacy to her features, to her pert lips, her slender jaw, that belied the strength of her attack.

Hugh rose to his feet, a few more rocks rolling off his shoulders. "Ow," he said.

Her eyes flared open wide in shock. Subtle paint made her hazel eyes all the more dramatic, flaring wings of yellow, burgundy, and light purple that swept up under her brow.

She wore a delicate necklace of what looked like cowrie shells around her neck, and Hugh saw her pulse racing there, her chest rising and falling in despair.

And in the face of her fear his joy in the battle burned away, leaving ashes in his mouth.

This was no dread foe for him to crush. This was but a young woman, no matter her shape or form, and the fear in her eyes was all too real.

The fae leader had regained his wits. He cried out a command, voice as piercing as the scream of a dying animal, and turned to dive into the boulder-choked passage, ducking under the stiff foliage that had rebuffed him before.

The feathered fae leaped high and then executed his oblique spearing down, clearing the foliage in like manner, to disappear into the shadows beyond.

The ram girl hesitated, eyes darting from Hugh to the wall of bandits that faced her, then betrayed her intent with a subtle flexing of her knees.

Hugh was on her before she could do more than turn and begin her flight.

One arm wrapped around her slender neck, the other around her slender waist, lifting her clear off the ground before she could flee.

"Irons!" roared Hugh as she screamed and bucked her head back, twisting it from side to side in a bid to crash her horns into his face. "Irons!"

Men came running up, seized her wrists and clamped manacles about them. The fae screamed at the iron's touch, and when the collar was closed about her neck, all the strength fled her frame, so that she fell limp in Hugh's embrace, writhing in helpless agony.
