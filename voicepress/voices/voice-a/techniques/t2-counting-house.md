Tam lay flat on the counting-house roof with the slates cold under his belly and did his sums.

Two watchmen. The one on the front door was asleep on a stool with his chin on his chest, which was a gift from the gods. The one walking the yard was awake, and had a dog, and the dog stopped to piss on the same rain barrel every circuit as though it were paying a toll. Forty paces round, a minute and a bit each time.

So he'd go down the drainpipe while the dog was busy at the barrel, then twelve feet across to the shutter. Pry it, slip in, and the strongroom would be the second door on the left, if the clerk hadn't lied. The clerk had been far too drunk to lie well.

Except the drainpipe was iron. Iron rang.

He could see it already: his boot on the bracket and the whole length of the pipe humming, the dog's head coming up, and about six seconds after that he'd be wearing the dog.

Not the pipe, then.

The ivy was older than the building and thick as his wrist at the root, and it ran right past the shutter. It would hold him, probably. It had held the clerk's cat, and the cat was enormous.

He thought about the four marks he owed Sallow, and what Sallow had done to Ned Pike's hands over a good deal less. Went on thinking about it for exactly as long as it took the dog to finish at the barrel.

Then he swung his legs over the edge and went down the ivy.

A strand tore loose under his left hand with a soft wet *rrrip*, and for one long sick moment he hung by three fingers over the yard with the dog not twenty paces off and turning his way -

It sneezed. Trotted on.

Tam breathed again, found the shutter and worked his blade into the crack. Easiest four marks of his life, he told himself, and didn't believe a word of it.
