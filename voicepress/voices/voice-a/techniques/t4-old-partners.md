Orsolya was mending a net on her doorstep when he came up the path, and she didn't stop mending it.

"You've got fat," she said.

"You've got old."

"I was always old. You were always going to get fat. It's nice when things work out." She bit off a length of twine. "You'll want tea."

"I want a word."

"Tea first. Words go down better with something hot behind them, and I'm not having one on my doorstep where Mother Dunn can hear. She's deaf as a post and she'll still hear it."

He followed her in. The cottage was smaller than he remembered, or he had grown, and it smelled the way it always had, of tar and woodsmoke, and of the lavender she had sworn for thirty years she couldn't stand and had never once stopped buying.

She set the kettle on the hook. Didn't look at him.

"Pell's dead," he said.

The kettle didn't care. It ticked as it warmed.

"Is he."

"Six days ago, in Harrowgate. Someone opened his throat in an alley and took his boots. He was looking for you, Orsa."

She took two cups down from the shelf and turned one over to check it for spiders, the way she always had, and found one, and tipped it gently out of the window.

"Well," she said. "He found Harrowgate."

He watched her pour. Steady hands, not so much as a ripple in either cup, and he had seen those same hands shake so badly once that she couldn't span a crossbow. The night they'd left Pell behind on the quay at Vossmouth with the Watch coming up the steps and his leg broken under him, calling their names...

She knew. She had known before he came up the path, and had sat on that step mending a net nobody needed mended so she would have something to do with her hands when he arrived.

"Milk?" she said. "I've got goat's, if you'll have it, though you used to hate goat's."

"Orsa -"

"There's no sugar. I've given it up. Doctor's orders." She handed him the cup and met his eyes for the first time, and held them until he looked away. "Drink your tea, Daven."

He drank his tea.
