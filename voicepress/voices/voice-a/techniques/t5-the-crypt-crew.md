There were seven of them when they went down the stair into the crypt. Vasko counted them again at the bottom out of habit. Counted twice, because the light was bad and he was nervous, and he'd learned long ago that the night you didn't count was the night somebody was missing. Old Mabb with the lantern, muttering to it the way she always did, as though it were a dog that might wander off. The Pellow brothers, who had never once in Vasko's hearing agreed on anything except money. Thin Ilse with her crossbow wound and ready, which made everyone else walk a little to one side of her. The priest. Vasko himself.

And Dorran, at the back, carrying the rope and saying nothing at all, which was not like Dorran. Dorran talked in his sleep. Dorran talked at funerals. Vasko filed it away and went on.

The crypt was older than the chapel above it by a few hundred years, and whoever had built it had wanted the dead to stay put. Iron bands across every niche. Lead seals on the iron. Somebody had gone to the expense of having the seals stamped, and somebody else, much later, had gone to the trouble of cutting every one of them open.

"Well, that's encouraging," said the younger Pellow.

"It's a good sign," said the elder. "Means somebody's been in and out already. Means it's -"

"Means somebody's been *in*," said Ilse, and nobody laughed.

The priest knelt by the nearest niche and held his hand over the cut seal without touching it. Vasko watched the old man's lips move. Watched him stand, too, and take a slow step back from the iron, then another, until he was standing a good deal closer to the stair than anyone else. Ilse saw it as well. Her crossbow drifted a little toward him.

"Something you'd like to share, Father?" Vasko said mildly.

"No," said the priest. "Nothing at all."

"Only you've moved."

"My knees are old," said the priest, "and the floor is... well. Cold," and every one of them knew it for a lie, and nobody said so, because they needed him to read the inscriptions further in.

They went on, Mabb and her lantern first. The light swung away from the niches and the dark came back to fill them.

In the third niche from the stair, something that had been lying very still for a very long time turned its head to watch them go.

Twenty paces further in, the passage opened into a round chamber, and in the middle of the floor somebody had dug a hole, neat and square, and lined it with the stamped lead seals laid edge to edge, each polished bright as though whoever did it had knelt there for days with a rag and nothing else in the world to do. The younger Pellow looked at it for a long time.

"Who," he said at last, "tiles a fucking hole?"
