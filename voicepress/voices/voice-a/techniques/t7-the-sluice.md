The sluice gate at Morrow Weir had been built to be opened by twelve men on a windlass, and Tobin had three, one of whom was drunk and another of whom was eleven. And a flood an hour upriver, maybe less.

On paper the answer was the windlass, and the windlass was no answer at all. Three men could turn it, slowly, but the chain had rusted into the gear teeth somewhere around the reign of the last king, and every quarter-turn the whole frame shrieked and bucked like it wanted to throw them into the race. Another half hour of that and they might have the gate a foot open. A foot wouldn't drain a bathtub.

Tobin stood on the walkway with the spray in his face and looked at the problem the way old Harrel had taught him at the mill. He looked at what held the gate shut.

Two iron pins, each as thick as his forearm, through the hinges on the downstream side. The whole weight of the river was pressing on that gate. It wanted to open. It had been wanting to open for sixty years. The windlass wasn't there to lift it. It was there to stop it swinging wide all at once and tearing itself off its own hinges.

So stop fighting the river, and let it do the work.

"Pull the pins," said Tobin.

The drunk one stared at him. "It'll go."

"That's rather the idea."

"It'll go all at *once*, it'll take the walkway, it'll take *us* -"

"Not if we're off the walkway first." Tobin was already fitting the long bar under the first pin. Clever, Tobin, very clever. Now be clever and fast.

Upstream, the river had changed its voice, from a roar to something deeper that he felt in his teeth before he heard it.

An hour, he'd thought. Half that now, or less.

Lady of the Ford, he prayed, which he hadn't done since his mother's funeral, I'm doing the work here. All I'm asking is you don't do anything clever of your own. Just this once. Stay out of it.

The first pin came free with a scream of iron and dropped into the race. The gate groaned and leaned, and held on the one remaining hinge. He got the bar under the second pin and heaved, and felt it move.

"Off!" he yelled. "Everybody off!"

The boy went, and the drunk went faster than any sober man Tobin had ever seen. Tobin gave the bar one last heave, and the pin slid, and slid, and stuck.

A sliver of rust, no wider than a fingernail, had wedged between pin and hinge, and the whole weight of the river was leaning on it now, pinning it tight.

Upstream, the trees along the bank began to fall over, one after another, in a long brown wave.

Well, thought Tobin. That's the Lady for you.
