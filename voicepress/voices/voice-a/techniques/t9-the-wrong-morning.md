"Get up," said Wenna, and Arlo said "No" and pulled the blanket over his head.

"You promised Mother you'd fix her gate."

"Your mother," said Arlo into the pillow, "is a gate."

Wenna laughed despite herself and sat down on the edge of the bed, and he rolled over and looked up at her with one eye, his hair standing straight up on the left side. She did the thing they always did. Poked him in the ribs and said "Fish," and he said "Fish" back with a perfectly straight face, and neither of them had ever been able to explain to anybody else why that was funny.

Then he pulled her down.

She meant to object. She had a whole objection ready, about her mother's gate, and the shutters being wide open, and somehow it went out of her head when he kissed her. He tasted of nothing, the way he always did since the fever, not even of sleep. The healer had said it was the scorch in his blood and it would pass, and it hadn't passed, and Wenna had stopped minding about the third week.

And why not, she thought, with his hand on her hip. Why not now? They'd said the words at the stone in front of half the village. The priest had only wanted them to wait for the harvest blessing because the priest wanted a fee. And she'd been waiting since spring, and so, she was fairly sure, had he, and the house was empty. Mother was at the market until noon. Well. Probably until noon.

She sat up and reached for the laces of her shift, taking her time about it so he'd see, and her heart was going hard -

"Gods," said Arlo, swinging his legs out of the bed on the other side, "I've got to piss like a plough horse."

He stood and stretched, scratching his belly. Wandered off toward the yard, whistling, and Wenna sat on the bed with her laces in her hand and listened to him go.

"Did you say something about the gate?" he called back through the door. "Is it the hinge again or the post?"

"The post," said Wenna.

"Right," said Arlo. "Fish."
