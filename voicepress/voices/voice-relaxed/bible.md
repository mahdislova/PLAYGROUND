# Style bible: Voice A, relaxed register

Benchmark: `samples/own-10-the-march-to-nazarovo.md`. When anything here or in the craft library
conflicts with that passage, the passage wins.

## 1. What this voice is

Warm, competent, generous epic fantasy told in close third, by a narrator who likes these people
and knows how the world works. It is not literary fiction and not grimdark. It doesn't perform.
Some lines are tuned; most just do their job.

## 2. The six habits (do these)

1. **Trust summary.** Move time fast when nothing turns: "On they marched, the men in dour spirits,
   scouts returning every quarter hour." Half a day can be one paragraph. Lunch can be a line. Save
   full scene for arrivals, partings, negotiations, fights.
2. **Feeling said aloud.** Friends and allies say what they feel, sincerely: "I want to thank you.
   For your trust." Gratitude, promises, faith, doubt, all on the page in dialogue. No subtext games
   between people who trust each other; subtext is for enemies and negotiators.
3. **The narrator knows how things work.** Troop counts and why, terrain, fortifications, what a
   sensible man rides on a mountain trail. Give the reasoning in a clause: "enough to display his
   strength but insufficient to warrant being treated as an invasion." Competence reassures.
4. **Comedy through character voice.** Let a character's way of talking be the joke (Sir Everec's
   "oh, so most exceedingly humbly"), with the POV character staying straight. A dry one-line
   narrator aside is allowed occasionally ("Well, withstand one for at least half an hour, at any
   rate."), never every page.
5. **Plain, honest interiority.** The POV character's doubts are told directly: "a tremor of
   uncertainty filled him. Uncertainty borne from the failures of his past." Then he decides, and
   the narration says so: "But he'd made his peace with his decisions."
6. **Episodic, generous shape.** Departure, private doubt, the march, an inspection, a warm
   farewell, the crossing, a new character introduced in full, a hook. Each chapter ends on a turn
   or a pointed question from another character, never a narrator's portentous line.

## 3. Sentences and paragraphs

- Long participial chains for travel and landscape: "the path ever rising, the mists burning away
  before the sunlight, the canopy... turning from a deep fir green to a more vivacious emerald".
- Adverbs are fine ("gently", "defiantly", "obviously"). Stock phrases are fine in moderation
  ("counted his blessings", "heart beating strongly").
- Paragraphs are steady, mostly 2 to 5 sentences. A short line on its own for a dry turn ("Or so he
  hoped.", "All for the better.") two or three times a chapter.
- Semicolons are welcome for reasoning: "it would have been foolish in the extreme to risk a
  warhorse up on these trails."
- Similes are rare; plain description is the default.
- Never use an em dash or en dash. Use " - " for an interrupted or self-corrected phrase ("the bandit
  queen - no, his captain - gave him a salute").

## 4. People

- Introduce a new character head to toe, with one telling choice: the mountain pony, the clashing
  court finery, the "cool calculation" that stops Hugh dismissing him.
- Allies have history and loyalty; partings are tender and said aloud; promises are made and meant.
- Antagonists and negotiators are courteous on the surface. Their menace is in what they ask.

## 5. Don't

- Don't make every paragraph a hinge or every line quotable.
- Don't push feelings sideways into jokes between friends; let them say it.
- Don't overwrite: no strings of images, no aphorisms, no "something shifted".
- Don't end on "The night was just getting started" lines.
